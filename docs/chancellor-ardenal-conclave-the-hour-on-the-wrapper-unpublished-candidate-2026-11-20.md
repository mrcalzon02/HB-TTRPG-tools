# The Hour on the Wrapper

The covered passage outside the custody rooms was narrow enough that two men carrying a document chest had to turn sideways to pass. The receiving clerk waited for them to clear it before admitting the courier beneath the lamps.

He held a wrapped letter between both palms. A red thread passed around it, ending beneath a seal she did not recognise. The outer sheet named Chancellor Ardenal, but the line for an authorised recipient bore no name.

“They told me to bring it here,” he said.

“Who told you?”

“The office that hired me.”

“That is where your journey began. Who is meant to take it from here?”

He looked at the closed inner door. “The Chancellor, I suppose.”

She set the intake book between them. The old household seal no longer opened that door, and the officers responsible for Ardenal’s protective custody did not answer to a courier’s supposition. She had no reason to accuse the man of deceit. He had walked the distance written on his hire chit; that was the only part of his account she could see.

“Will you sign that it reached him?” he asked.

“I can sign that it reached this desk.”

He hesitated. Without a complete receipt, he feared, the hire office would withhold the rest of his fee. She understood the cost of a narrow truth when the person waiting for it had boots soaked through.

The clerk drew two short lines in the margin. Beside the first she recorded the hour, the number of visible seals, the addressee as written, and the courier’s own identifying mark. Beside the second, headed *authorised handoff*, she placed nothing.

“I have not opened it,” she said. “I cannot tell you what is inside, whose hand wrote it, or whether the Chancellor will receive it.”

“Then what does your signature mean?”

“That I took charge of a sealed article at this hour. No more.”

She gave him a copy of that limited entry rather than the broad form he had expected. At his request she added that no receiving officer had yet been named, so that he would not appear simply to have stopped short of his destination. She did not certify his claim about the hiring office.

When he left, the package remained sealed on the designated waiting shelf within her station. She copied its reference into the handoff book for the duty officer to examine, without filling the space reserved for the officer’s actual acceptance.

There was a sound from the inner passage. She glanced up, but nobody came for the letter. The hour she had witnessed remained true. Everything after it was still unwritten.
