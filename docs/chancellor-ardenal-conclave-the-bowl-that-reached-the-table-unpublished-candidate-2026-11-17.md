At the lower kitchen, the soup had begun to skin over. The serving spoons were locked away.

A red preservation cord crossed the cupboard where the household's marked silver lay. The cord was not a confiscation order, the junior cook had been told. It was a warning that nobody could move the contents and afterward claim the shelves had always been empty.

That distinction did not help her carry broth.

She found an iron ladle among the unmarked work tools, scrubbed it, and placed it beside the pot. At the serving door a neutral officer examined the narrow instruction brought from the service desk.

"Provisional issue," he read. "Food already held for the household. Witness the quantity served. Do not break preservation seals. Do not certify a treasury payment."

The cook looked at the pot. "Does this let them eat?"

"It lets us record what we actually give them."

"That's a lawyer's answer."

"I'm not a lawyer."

"Then you might understand why it's a poor one."

He did not argue. He counted the bowls she had washed and set his own pencil on the counter. The ration sheet bore no claim that fresh stores had been purchased or that wages could now be paid. She had no wish to make another kind of paper dinner.

A scullion brought the first tray. She had been waiting for a portion herself but stood aside to let two exhausted attendants through. One held an empty bowl with both hands, as though he expected it to be taken away for evidence.

"Will I need to sign?" he asked.

"Only if you are accepting a bowl," the officer said. "Not a settlement."

The cook put soup into it. The attendant sat on the bench just beyond the door and began eating before the second tray reached him.

They counted each filled bowl as it crossed the threshold. When one was returned untouched, the cook drew a neat line through its tally rather than pretending it had fed anyone. The officer initialled the correction. He could witness a meal. He could not witness what the attendants had been promised last week, or what the receiving vault had done with a sealed pouch.

At last the scullion took her own portion. She ate standing, one hand still gripping the ladle.

"Write that we have enough for tomorrow," the officer suggested, looking at the remaining pot.

"Write that this pot has some left," she said. "Tomorrow has not come here yet."

He wrote it that way. The silver stayed sealed, the wage column stayed open, and the bowls, for once, were empty for the right reason.
