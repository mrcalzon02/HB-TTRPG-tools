---

The first envelope called Grellholm the sector's certain voice upon Terra. The second described him as a temporary preference. The third left a space where his title ought to have been.

He set them beside his breakfast and allowed the tea to cool.

The Epistolarian letter came on expensive paper. It congratulated him upon the Synod's certified vote and supplied a list of institutions whose petitions, it suggested, ought to travel with him. The Demoskian memorandum warned that no ambassador should presume to speak for offices whose custody was under examination. The Sindrian note requested a written account of how competing petitions would be recorded and answered, should a representative ever be confirmed.

None of the three asked what he believed.

A secretary waited by the door with a fourth document: the unendorsed confirmation form. It said nothing. It was the only sheet in the room whose silence Grellholm trusted.

“Shall I prepare acknowledgments?” the secretary asked.

“Yes. Not congratulations.”

“The Epistolarians will take offense.”

“They have learned to survive worse.”

“The Demoskians will insist that silence yields the field.”

“Then we shall answer them plainly.”

Grellholm took up his pen. He owed the Epistolarians something: they had supported him when others considered him too austere, too old, or too unwilling to accept remedies with uncertain provenance. But gratitude could not become an undisclosed order of precedence. A representative who began by carrying only the requests of his sponsors might arrive on Terra as a voice and return as an echo.

He wrote beneath the first letter: *The vote is certified. Confirmation remains outstanding. No petition has been accepted for transmission.*

Beneath the second: *Questions of office custody belong to the competent authority. I possess no warrant to settle them.*

The secretary read the third note aloud twice. Its language was dry enough to preserve meat.

“This one asks for a method,” he said.

“Methods can become promises too,” Grellholm replied. “Tell them that if confirmation comes, every submission must carry its author, authority, and date. A petition deserves to be considered, not disguised as another man's command.”

“And if confirmation never comes?”

“Then the next man may use the rule without needing my permission.”

From the courtyard came the scrape of cart wheels bringing parish bread to the lower kitchens. Grellholm looked at the three seals, each an argument about who would own a journey he had not yet been permitted to make.

“Send the replies by ordinary registry,” he said. “Do not call them my policy for Terra.”

The secretary hesitated over the blank destination line.

“Leave it blank,” Grellholm said. “For once, the record ought to know what it does not know.”
