# Chapter Thirty-One — The Cost of Refusing

*Working chapter with integrated procedural follow-up and domestic aftermath. All developments remain provisional until continuity reconciliation and canonical review. Do not include this draft in the public numbered-chapter reader.*

The refusal had a price.

Ilyan learned this because the price had been entered in a column headed *ordinary administrative expense*, which seemed an unkind description of the effort required to tell a powerful office no.

He was reading a schedule of civic-review fees when Judge Vaun came in with rain on her sleeves and an expression suggesting that the weather had tried to argue with her.

"You're early," she said.

"I couldn't sleep."

"Then you should have slept later."

"An excellent solution. I shall attempt it yesterday."

She took the schedule from him.

The room had been prepared for a morning of careful work. Neral had set a second writing board near the window, where the light was best, and laid out a pot of ink, clean sand, and three cups. The third cup was Damaris's. It had a chipped handle, and she preferred it because no one else wanted it. A ridiculous preference, Ilyan thought, but one he now remembered without trying.

He had put her cup closest to the door.

Vaun looked at the fee schedule.

"Where did you find this?"

"Public rules shelf."

"And what were you hoping to find?"

"The cost of refusing an initiating instruction."

"To whom?"

"The receiving commission."

"Not what I asked."

He sat back.

The surviving record concerned an initiating-side scope reservation against the receiving officer's proposed closure of controlled non-person custody. A civic duty commissioner had reviewed the receiver's authority, allowed the closure to stand, and found no need for sealed judicial escalation. They knew that much. They did not know what had been held, why the receiving officer had chosen that moment, or what factual circumstance had made the changed-interest clause relevant.

Ilyan had called it a refusal because that was how it felt.

Vaun was waiting for him to remember that feelings were not entries in a register.

"Who paid for the review?" he said.

"Better."

"The initiating office entered the reservation. The receiver had to answer. The civic commissioner had to consider it. Somebody copied the notices and somebody kept the books. There must have been an administrative cost."

"There was."

"And a time cost."

"Yes."

"And someone must have been told that the two offices had ceased to agree."

"That is what we are here to ask."

Ilyan touched the corner of the schedule.

"Without asking what they disagreed about."

"Precisely."

Damaris arrived carrying a parcel wrapped in brown cloth and smelling faintly of warm pastry. She set it on the table, took the chipped cup, and looked from Ilyan to Vaun.

"Has he accused a clerk of murder yet?"

"Not before breakfast," Vaun said.

"I am surrounded by affection."

"You are surrounded by people who remember you at seventeen," Damaris said.

She opened the parcel. Small hand pies lay inside, glazed with honey and sprinkled with ground nuts. Ilyan reached for one.

Damaris slapped his fingers away.

"Wash your hands."

"I've been handling paper."

"Which has been handled by a century of dead officials."

"Some of them were surely clean."

"Go."

He went.

When he returned, Vaun had divided the work into three columns. One concerned the authority to issue notice. One concerned the office required to receive it. The third concerned what, if anything, could be certified without disclosing protected particulars.

It was not a dramatic arrangement.

Ilyan was beginning to respect it.

---

The registrar was a woman with a white streak through otherwise black hair and the patient bearing of someone who had spent twenty years telling important people that their importance did not exempt them from filing requirements.

Her name was Halen. Ilyan remembered her from the earlier closure inquiry. She remembered him too.

"Judge," she said to Vaun. Then, to Ilyan: "Have you come to ask who signed the paper, or have you learned that the answer was already given?"

"I've come to ask about the notices."

Halen considered him.

"Progress."

The civic register office was busy. At the next desk a mason was disputing the classification of a collapsed retaining wall. Two apprentices waited with a crate of seal wax. A woman in a blue work dress had brought a petition concerning the custody of tools after a guild partnership dissolved. None of them had any reason to care about Silver Night. All of them wanted a clerk's attention before noon.

Halen brought out the general notice rules, not the sealed commission record.

"These are public," she said. "You may read them here. You may copy the general procedure. You may not use it to infer a protected recipient or an undisclosed address."

Damaris inclined her head.

"We aren't seeking one."

"People often discover they are seeking one after they have already asked three innocent questions."

She left them with the book.

Ilyan opened it to the section on disputed closure.

The rules were written for ordinary cases as well as extraordinary ones. A receiver could propose ending a controlled obligation. An initiating authority could reserve scope. The receiver could maintain its determination. The civic reviewing office then examined whether the receiver possessed lawful authority to act as it had, not whether the initiating office preferred a different result.

The distinction was old. It had survived revisions, changes of magistrate, and at least one reform undertaken after an unrelated commercial custody scandal.

The review had a timetable.

An initiating reservation required prompt registration. The receiver's authority answer had its own deadline. A civic commissioner could request clarification, but the clarification could not demand disclosure of protected particulars merely to satisfy curiosity. If the disagreement remained material, the review could go under seal to a judge.

Ilyan copied the deadlines, then stopped.

"There are two kinds of notice."

Damaris leaned over.

One notice told the initiating office that its reservation had been received and registered. Another told the civic reviewing authority that an unresolved difference of position required examination. Neither was the same as the eventual acknowledgment that the closure could stand.

"Three moments," she said.

"At least."

"And three different offices may have held records of them."

"May have," Ilyan repeated.

Damaris smiled.

"Look at you."

He looked back at the page.

The general rules required the reviewer to record the procedural ground of the decision. They did not require the reviewer to circulate the underlying protected material to the initiating office. A closure could therefore be upheld while the initiator remained ignorant of facts the receiver had properly withheld.

It was possible to lose an argument without learning everything about the person or property over which one had argued.

Ilyan thought of his family. He suspected several of his relatives would consider that arrangement an affront to civilization.

He did not write that down.

Halen returned.

"Well?"

"Can we ask whether a review notice was generated in this commission's dispute?" Damaris said.

"That is narrower than asking for the notice."

"Can we ask which office class received it, without naming a person?"

"Possibly. It depends on the class."

"And whether it entered an exception register?"

Halen's eyes moved to Vaun.

"That is a different request."

"I know."

"Do you?"

Damaris did not bristle. She placed the general rules between them and indicated the two separate registration duties.

"We know the initiating reservation existed. We know the civic review happened. We do not know whether a separate administrative exception notice was sent beyond the immediate dispute officers. We want to know whether there is an ordinary register in which that class of notice would be recorded. We do not want the protected case entry."

Halen looked at the page, then at Damaris.

"That is a question I can answer from the rules."

Ilyan waited.

"An exception register exists for disagreements that reach civic review," Halen said. "Its purpose is audit of the procedure, not reconstruction of the protected matter. Whether any particular case entered it is a question for the custodian of that register."

"Who is the custodian?"

"The civic review office for the period concerned. Not the Tribunal merely because the Tribunal initiated the protection."

Ilyan felt his first disappointment of the morning.

He had wanted Salver's later Secretariat appointment to be a path through the records. Halen had just reminded him that a path on an organizational chart was not the same thing as a hand on a document.

Damaris saw his face.

"Good," she said.

"Why is everyone pleased when a possibility disappears?"

"Because it was a false possibility."

"Not necessarily false."

"Then unproved. You know what I mean."

Halen closed the general rules.

"You may request the existence and office-class routing of the exception entry through Judge Vaun. The custodian may answer in a bounded form or explain why even that would be disclosive. Do not ask this desk to guess."

Vaun rose.

"Thank you."

"You're welcome, Judge. And if the young man wishes to pay the ordinary copying fee, I shall be delighted to accept it."

Ilyan stared at her.

"For the public rules?"

"For the copy he has been making."

Damaris laughed all the way to the door.

---

By midday, the question had acquired a proper shape and a small bill.

Neral paid the copying fee from the investigation's authorized expenses. Ilyan tried to pay it himself. Neral refused.

"It is an office cost," he said.

"I have coins."

"Congratulations."

"You sound like Vaun."

"I have worked very hard."

Neral entered the amount with the care he gave to everything that might one day be inspected by a person less sympathetic than himself. It was not much money. It was enough to remind Ilyan that a lawful investigation consumed labor, time, paper, ink, wages, access and patience, none of which grew from a noble family's desire to be vindicated.

"How many requests do we have pending?" Ilyan asked.

"Seven."

"That many?"

"Two are for the western inquiry. One concerns the Academy's disposition notice. Two are cross-index permissions. One is the civic commission's bounded notice question. One is the personnel assignment request you keep trying to write as though the answer has already arrived."

Ilyan looked at the last page.

Neral had left the space for Salver's later duties blank.

"Thank you."

"I thought you might notice."

"I noticed."

"Then my day has been a success."

The little joke landed more gently than Ilyan expected. Neral had spent his morning ensuring that people with titles and compelling grief did not create trouble for ordinary clerks who had to keep working after the titles went home.

There were costs to refusal, he thought, and costs to insisting upon an answer.

Neither made the refusal wrong.

He folded the receipt into the account book.

---

In the west, Thessa stood beneath a bridge and listened to a carpenter explain why the river had no respect for appropriation schedules.

The bridge was narrow, stone at either end and timber over the deepest channel. Spring water ran brown and quick below it. The road surface had been patched twice since the thaw, and a wagon loaded with grain had to wait while another crossed from the opposite bank.

Thessa had arrived intending to compare payment dates.

She was now holding a chisel.

"Here," the carpenter said.

He pointed to a newer timber brace beneath the deck. It had been installed after the flood had undercut a support and made the bridge unsafe for loaded traffic. The brace was sound. The joinery was good. The crew had not invented the repair to collect money for imaginary work.

"What did you order?" Thessa asked.

"Two beams, four plates, bolts, labor, and a man to keep the wagons from trying to cross while we were under it."

"Who supplied the beams?"

The carpenter named a timber yard already present in the district accounts.

"And who paid you?"

"Intermediary's clerk, first part. District works office, second part."

"Two payments?"

"One job."

He looked offended by the suggestion that the difference might be obvious.

Thessa climbed out from beneath the bridge, put the chisel back in his hand, and brushed river grit from her coat.

"Do you have the dates?"

"I have my own book."

"May I see it?"

"Are you going to take it away?"

"No."

"Are you going to tell me I wrote the numbers wrong?"

"Only if you did."

He gave her a look, then went to fetch it.

Captain Sorn, waiting by the road, watched the wagons inch forward.

"You've found a real repair."

"Yes."

"And real wages."

"Yes."

"And you are still unhappy."

"The road account paid the intermediary for stone. The intermediary helped pay for timber. The bridge had its own appropriation. I want to know whether the second appropriation reimbursed the first payment or paid the same labor twice."

"Do you think someone stole it?"

"I think somebody made a ledger that doesn't tell me what happened."

Sorn nodded.

"That is not the same accusation."

"No."

"Good."

Thessa looked at her.

"You sound pleased."

"I have spent too many years with officers who see a crooked line in a book and begin arresting people before they have found the bookkeeper."

The carpenter returned with a notebook wrapped in oiled cloth. His handwriting was uneven, but the entries had dates, amounts, names of men who had worked the repair, and a notation beside the final payment.

Thessa read it twice.

The final payment had come late.

Not dramatically late. Late enough that a man who lived from one contract to the next would remember it.

"Did you have to borrow to pay your crew?" she asked.

He looked away toward the bridge.

"Paid them from my own purse. Didn't want them waiting."

"Was that difficult?"

"Of course it was difficult."

He said it without self-pity. The fact needed no decoration.

Thessa wrote down the dates and the sum, then asked permission to copy the notation.

She had been chasing a mismatch between accounts. The mismatch had briefly become a man's wages and the money he had needed to advance to keep his workers fed.

It did not yet tell her whether the district had made a harmless reimbursement error, used one fund to cover another, or paid twice. It told her that an accounting decision had imposed a real burden somewhere along the road.

Sorn looked over her shoulder.

"Can you prove why he was paid late?"

"No."

"Then don't put the reason in your report."

"I wasn't going to."

"You were making the face."

"What face?"

"The one you make when you have decided the universe is badly managed and intend to correct it before supper."

Thessa considered the captain.

"I like you less every day."

"That is what I hoped."

The bridge shuddered as a wagon crossed. Thessa steadied herself against the parapet and watched the grain go east.

She would send Ilyan the dates. She would not send him a culprit.

Not yet.

---

The district works office was housed above a cooper's yard, where the smell of wet oak rose through the floorboards whenever someone opened the window. By the time Thessa reached it the following morning, two carts had already stopped outside with complaints about the bridge, the road, or the price of repairing either.

Captain Sorn stayed at the door. She had brought the carpenter's figures, a list of the men he had paid, and a packet of district warrants that Thessa had requested the day before. The clerk who received them was young enough to be careful about the way he addressed a noble and old enough to resent having to be careful.

"You are asking whether the bridge was paid for twice," he said.

"I am asking you to show me how it was paid for once."

He looked down at her papers.

"The bridge has an appropriation."

"I've seen it."

"And an emergency works warrant."

"I've seen that too."

"Then you have the answer."

Thessa pulled up the chair opposite his desk. Its back had been repaired with a strip of darker wood.

"Will you put your name to that answer?"

His fingers stopped on the warrant.

She regretted the question as soon as she saw his expression. It had sounded like a threat. In this room, with her house name and a Warden's badge, perhaps it was one.

"I'll put mine to the request," she said. "And to anything I conclude from it. You needn't accept my conclusion."

Sorn, at the door, made no attempt to rescue her.

The clerk drew a second book from the shelf. Its pages were narrower than the district ledger, ruled for cash actually issued rather than money authorized. He turned it toward her but kept a hand on the binding.

"The appropriation is permission to spend," he said. "This is what the cashier says left the office. They are not the same book."

"Thank you."

He glanced up, surprised by her tone, and then found the entry.

The bridge account had paid the carpenter the balance of his labor and timber claim. The date matched the notation in the man's own notebook. A separate entry charged the emergency intermediary for an earlier advance, but the office copy did not contain the intermediary's original payment instruction. The amount was close to the first installment the carpenter remembered. Close was not identical.

"Fees?" Thessa asked.

"Possibly. Cartage. An advance to the timber yard rather than to the carpenter. Or a correction entered later."

"Can you show me the correction?"

"Not here. If there was one, it would be in the intermediary's clearing book."

"And the road-stone charge?"

He found the road ledger and set it beside the cash book. The stone warrant had been properly authorized as an emergency purchase. Its payment reference led to the same intermediary, not to the quarry. The attached description still read *replacement stone*. There was no delivery acknowledgment in this office's copy.

Thessa stared at the empty space where she wanted a receipt.

"Could the quarry have supplied stone through the intermediary?"

"Yes."

"Could the money have been used temporarily for the bridge and then restored?"

"Yes."

"Could the two warrants have covered the same cost?"

"Yes."

"You're enjoying this."

"No, Warden. I'm trying not to say something that gets repeated as a finding."

That made her look at him properly. He had a burn mark on one cuff, probably from the stove beside the accounts window, and ink along the side of his smallest finger. A pile of ordinary wage vouchers waited under his elbow. The man had been working before she arrived and would have more work after she left.

"Neither am I," she said.

He allowed her to copy the two payment references. He would not let her take the book. She had not intended to ask.

Outside, a hammer struck a barrel hoop three times. Sorn came to the desk.

"We were due on the north road by noon."

"I know."

"That's a day's ride if the ford is passable."

"I know that too."

The clerk looked between them.

Thessa had been assigned a road inspection, not a roving inquiry into every suspicious account in the western districts. The first task mattered. A washout could strand a village as thoroughly as a stolen appropriation. She had already spent longer at the bridge than her orders allowed.

Sorn waited.

Thessa closed her notebook.

"We don't hold the north-road patrol for this."

"Good."

"But I want the source documents preserved."

The clerk's hand tightened on the cash book.

"Preserved how?"

"Not impounded. Not sealed away from your work. A written request that the original warrants, delivery acknowledgments, intermediary clearing entries and any corrections be retained until the district auditor can compare them."

"Those records have ordinary retention periods."

"Then the request may cost you nothing."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Put the cost in your reply. I'll take it to my commander."

Sorn's eyes shifted toward her, but she said nothing.

The clerk considered the pages. "An audit request can make a cashier reluctant to release the next payment."

"Then the request must say that it is not an order to stop payments. I don't want the bridge crew waiting another fortnight because I dislike a column."

"The auditor may choose to suspend something."

"That will be the auditor's decision, on the auditor's evidence. Not mine."

He took out a clean form.

"What finding shall I enter?"

"None."

He paused.

"Not even an irregularity?"

"Enter that the available copies do not yet reconcile the road-stone charge with the bridge advance. Enter the missing documents by name. That's what I can stand behind."

He began to write.

Thessa watched the nib move. A great many people could make a convincing accusation from the two ledgers on the table. Her family had built fortunes on the ability to read accounts faster than their rivals. She had expected to be pleased when the arithmetic stopped making sense. Instead she found herself thinking of the carpenter who had paid his crew from his own purse, and of a clerk whose signature might be carried into a dispute long after she had ridden away.

"Your name?" he asked.

"Thessa Eris-Vey."

"I have that."

"On the request. As the officer responsible."

He looked up.

"It will be there."

"And note that you supplied the records without endorsing my interpretation."

Sorn gave a small cough, which Thessa suspected was hiding a smile.

The clerk finished the form and handed her a copy. Its wording was dry and unremarkable. That was a relief.

They were halfway down the stairs when he called after them.

"Warden?"

She turned.

"The intermediary keeps its clearing books at the river market, not here. If you write to the district auditor, tell her to ask there first. Otherwise someone will spend a week sending notices to the wrong office."

"Thank you."

He nodded and returned to his work.

Outside, Sorn untied the horses while Thessa wrote a note to her commander explaining the delay, the records she had seen, and the patrol hours they could no longer recover. She did not blame the clerk, the bridge, or the weather. Her own name went at the bottom.

"You've made yourself responsible for a question that might have a perfectly dull answer," Sorn said.

"That is becoming a habit."

"One day you'll discover a crime."

"One day I'd like to discover a well-kept account."

Sorn swung into the saddle.

"Ambitious."

Thessa put away her pen. Before mounting she wrote one more note, this one for Ilyan. She gave him the payment dates and the two references, and asked him not to circulate them beyond Vaun's inquiry until the original clearing entries could be checked. Then, because she was tired of every letter between them resembling an affidavit, she added a line about the carpenter's terrible tea and the way the river had soaked her boots.

*Tell me what you've been eating. Properly. I know what you call supper when nobody is there to stop you.*

She folded the letter and sealed it.

By the time they reached the north road, the light had begun to change. They would have less daylight for the inspection than they ought to have had. Sorn made Thessa calculate the remaining distance aloud, then made her choose which section could be safely examined before dusk and which would require a return visit.

The work did not wait politely while she solved another piece of it.

At the first milepost north of the ford, a cart stood with one wheel in the ditch and its driver sitting on a stone. He was not injured. His mule was eating the grass beside him with the serene expression of an animal that had already decided the day's work was finished.

Sorn dismounted first.

"What happened?"

"The edge gave way," the driver said. "Not much. Enough."

He showed them where a narrow runnel had eaten under the packed road. The surface looked sound from above. When Thessa pressed her heel into it, the earth shifted, leaving a crack no wider than her thumb.

"How many carts came through before yours?" she asked.

"Six since dawn. Mine's the heaviest."

"Where were you going?"

"North mill. Flour sacks. The miller is waiting."

Sorn walked the verge with a measuring cord while Thessa helped the man ease his wheel clear. The axle had survived. The road had not. Beneath the thin cap of gravel the spring water had carried off the smaller stones, leaving a hollow that would swallow the next loaded wheel in much the same place.

A farm boy arrived from the far side of the bend with a pair of oxen and a length of chain. He offered both before anyone asked. He knew the driver, who called him a nuisance and accepted the help.

By the time they had hauled the cart free, the light had dropped behind the trees.

"Can you get to the mill by the east track?" Thessa asked.

"With this load? If the ford holds."

"Does it?"

The driver scratched his cheek. "This morning it did."

Sorn gave her a look. They had both heard the difference.

Thessa took out the district road sheet. The north route was under Warden inspection because the thaw had damaged several water crossings. The east track lay outside their immediate assignment, but a teamster could not be ordered onto it merely because the inspected road had become inconvenient.

"We can mark the hollow and stop the heavy traffic until morning," Sorn said quietly. "We can send a rider to the district road crew. We cannot repair a drainage cut in the dark."

"The miller won't thank us."

"The miller can come and inspect it himself if he wants."

Thessa crouched beside the damaged verge. Someone had packed fresh gravel into the rut within the last few days. The new material was pale against the older surface, but there was no timber support beneath it and no open channel for the water to escape.

"Was this patch here yesterday?" she asked the driver.

"It was. They did it after the last rain."

"Who?"

"Road hands, I suppose. I didn't stop to ask."

She could have taken the answer back to the works office as another suspicious expenditure. She had no reason to do so. A bad repair did not prove a false payment, and a road hand could be doing exactly what he had been told with exactly the materials he had been given.

She rose and brushed her hands on her coat.

"Put the warning posts on both approaches," she told Sorn. "Heavy loads wait. Light traffic passes on the inner side, one at a time, until the crew can judge the ground. I'll sign the restriction."

Sorn nodded, then pointed at the road sheet.

"And the northern crossing?"

"Tomorrow. We won't pretend we've inspected what we haven't seen."

The driver stood beside his cart, unhappy but listening. Thessa explained the restriction to him herself. He asked whether the Wardens would pay for the flour arriving late. She said she did not know, but gave him the office where a delay claim could be filed and promised to record his name and the time. She did not promise the claim would succeed.

He looked at her badge, then at the broken road.

"At least you've written it down," he said.

The farm boy helped drag the first warning post into place. Sorn set the second while Thessa drew the damaged section on the back of her road sheet. She measured it twice, using the driver's cart wheel as a reference because the light had become too poor to see the marks on the measuring cord.

When she finished, Sorn handed her the lantern.

"Your commander will have a cheerful report."

"Two incomplete inspections and a road restriction."

"And one cart recovered without a broken axle."

"Three people delayed."

"Four, if you count the mule."

The mule had resumed eating.

They took the north-road patrol shelter for the night. It was a stone hut with a leaky eave, two narrow benches, and a hearth that smoked until Sorn opened the upper shutter. Thessa dried her boots by the fire and copied the day's observations into the Warden book while they were still clear in her mind. She put the missing clearing entries from the district office on a separate page. The road failure went into the patrol report, where it belonged.

Afterward she unfolded the copy of her letter to Ilyan. She had kept the rough sheet by accident, along with the carpenter's figures. Her last question to him looked strangely domestic beside the payment references.

She imagined him in Vaun's workroom, forgetting a meal while he waited for someone to answer a carefully worded request. The thought made her smile. It also made her want to shake him.

Sorn passed her a bowl of lentils from the pot.

"Eat before you write another letter."

"Who says I'm writing one?"

"You have been looking at that page for ten minutes."

Thessa folded it away and accepted the bowl. She would have to ride at first light. Whatever Ilyan learned tomorrow would reach her later, after the roads and the couriers had had their say. For the moment, there was hot food, an honest account of an unfinished day's work, and a road that would still need watching when morning came.

---

Mara had pinned the map to their borrowed desk with four horseshoes. From the coaching yard below came the smell of hot iron and the sound of a horse refusing to have its hoof lifted.

"You're late," she told Rhyl.

"I'm still here."

"Which is why you're going to be late."

The spring water had moved two of the stones marking the safe approach to the charcoal burners' ford. Rhyl had seen the damage that afternoon. He had not seen the far bank clearly enough to certify the crossing, and the old map still showed the markers standing where they had been before the thaw.

Mara reached for the pencil behind his ear.

"Cross them out."

He let her. She wrote *far approach unverified* beside the ford.

A stable boy brought word that two drivers were waiting downstairs. Someone at the market had assured them the crossing was safe. Their wagons were loaded with charcoal, and the paved road would cost them a toll.

Rhyl went to speak to them.

"We crossed last month," the older driver said.

"Last month there were three markers. Now there's one."

"Then we'll watch our wheels."

"You will. The next wagon may follow your tracks without knowing why you chose them."

The younger man studied the wet field sheet Rhyl showed him. The older one asked whether Rhyl intended to close the road.

"I haven't authority to close it from this yard," Rhyl said. "I can tell you what I found, and I can sign that warning."

"Sign it, then. Our master won't pay the toll because of a conversation."

Rhyl understood. He took the paper upstairs and wrote the warning in his own name. Mara read it over his shoulder.

"You've said what you know," she said.

"And what I don't."

"Yes. There are still people who can tell the difference."

On the desk lay Damaris's invitation. She had written a list of answers he was forbidden to give when her mother asked whether he had eaten. *Something at the yard* had been crossed out twice. Below the list she had added, in smaller handwriting, *I want you there. Not as an explanation.*

Mara caught him looking at it.

"You're wearing the dark coat?"

"Yes."

"The one Damaris had you press?"

"She suggested it."

"With great delicacy, I'm sure."

He folded the warning.

"I don't mind dinner."

"I know."

"I mind her family treating me as an Orthell representative."

"Then don't represent us."

He gave her a look. Mara knew as well as he did that people sometimes saw a house before they saw the person wearing its name.

"The routes stay in the patrol book," he said.

"They were always going to."

He could have asked Mara to inspect the far bank in the morning. She would have done it, but she had her own boundary reports to complete. A family dinner did not relieve him of a duty he had already accepted.

"I'll take the ford at first light," he said. "If the water's higher, I'll come back and notify the road keeper. You finish the boundary report."

"That was my plan."

"You might have told me."

"I was waiting to see whether you'd ask."

Below, the stable boy called that the driver had returned. Rhyl carried the signed warning down and received a curt nod that might, with time, become thanks.

When he came back, Mara had put his pressed coat over the chair. Its collar smelled faintly of the laundry. He changed out of his wet riding clothes and checked the report once more before leaving. Nothing in the field book mentioned Damaris. Nothing in her invitation entitled House Caladren to the Red Hart's private routes.

He would have to keep making that distinction if he meant to go on loving her in public. He was beginning to understand that a private choice did not remain private simply because the people making it were sincere.

Outside, the evening lamps were being lit. The survey would be waiting at dawn. Damaris was waiting now.

---

Damaris's mother had laid the table for four.

That was the first thing Damaris noticed when she and Rhyl arrived. The second was that her mother had chosen the smaller dining room, where the windows looked into the enclosed garden and the chairs did not force guests to address one another across an acreage of polished wood.

Rhyl wore a dark coat Damaris had persuaded him to have pressed. He had not tried to make himself look like a courtier. She was glad.

Her mother kissed her cheek, greeted Rhyl with measured warmth, and asked about his field case before anyone had sat down.

"The leather strap," she said. "Damaris tells me you repaired it."

"I did."

"Was it difficult?"

"No, my lady."

"Then you have an advantage over half the people who own such things."

Rhyl smiled.

Damaris watched the exchange with the apprehension of a woman who had invited two competent people to assess one another and could not now pretend she had not arranged the meeting.

Dinner began with spring greens, fresh cheese, and bread still warm enough to leave flour on the cloth. The fourth place belonged to a cousin who arrived late from estate accounts and apologized with the ease of someone whose lateness had been predicted.

Nobody interrogated Rhyl.

That was almost worse.

Her mother asked about the western routes. The cousin asked how a Red Hart field observer distinguished an ordinary animal crossing from the track of a mounted messenger using the same gap in a hedge. Rhyl answered carefully, not because he was frightened, but because he disliked pretending to knowledge he did not possess.

"It depends on the ground," he said. "And the weather. And how much time has passed. Sometimes you can't."

The cousin nodded as though that were an answer worth keeping.

The cousin reached for his wine, then seemed to think better of the question he had been about to ask.

"We have charcoal coming down from the uplands," he said instead. "Our carrier thinks the ford will spare him a toll. I understand you were riding that way."

"I was."

"Would you take his route?"

"Not with a loaded wagon tonight."

The cousin's hand remained around the stem of his glass. He was neither offended nor reassured.

"Is that a formal closure?"

"No. I issued a warning about the approach. I haven't seen enough of the far bank to say more."

"He'll want something he can show the factor."

"I signed the warning. He can have a copy from the yard."

Damaris knew what would come next. Her cousin had spent the afternoon trying to settle a disputed delivery charge; the difference between a public warning and a private assessment would be several days of argument, and perhaps a cart standing idle while the merchant who owned it demanded compensation. She had watched him calculate such costs since she was a child. It was how he cared for the people who depended on the estate accounts, and also how he could make another person's caution sound like a personal inconvenience.

"Could you mark the safe line on our survey?" he asked.

Rhyl put down his fork. Beneath the cuff of his newly pressed coat, Damaris could see the pale scrape across his knuckle where a strap had caught him the previous week. She wanted to touch it. Instead she watched his face.

"Not from the Red Hart field book," he said. "And not before I've inspected it."

Her cousin considered him over the table. "I wasn't asking for your book."

"No. But that is where the information would come from."

Damaris felt a hot, familiar urge to make the room comfortable. She could have said that Rhyl was tired, that the survey was unfinished, that someone might ask again another day. Each would have softened the refusal by making it sound temporary.

"The factor can request a public road assessment," she said. "Or we can pay the toll."

"We can," her cousin agreed, a little ruefully. "I was hoping not to."

Her mother passed the bread.

"Then the toll may be the cheapest answer we have this evening."

Nobody applauded Rhyl for possessing a boundary. Her cousin asked for the butter, and the conversation moved to the kitchen garden, where an ambitious young pear tree had apparently been planted in the only patch of soil that the cook wanted for herbs. Damaris was grateful for the ordinary quarrel. She was less grateful that her mother had made the decision seem so easy. At home, the family could respect a line in one room while still pressing hard against a different line elsewhere.

Under the table Rhyl's knee brushed hers. She looked at him. He did not look particularly triumphant, only hungry, and slightly concerned that he had put her in a difficult position. She moved her hand beside his on the bench. Their fingers met for a moment where the tablecloth concealed them.

She had wanted him here as himself. It had cost her less than she feared to say so, and more than she could yet measure.

Damaris's mother poured him a little more wine.

Damaris felt an unreasonable rush of affection for the way he said *sometimes you can't*. Her family lived by estimates, hedges, qualified forecasts and accounts that often concealed more than they revealed. Yet she had spent her whole life watching relatives make uncertainty sound like authority. Rhyl was not incapable of judgment. He simply knew the difference between a track and a story about a track.

At the second course, her mother asked about the investigation.

Damaris put down her fork.

"Only the parts I'm permitted to discuss."

"Naturally."

"I mean it."

"I know."

That was the difficulty. Her mother did know. She had taught Damaris to guard a confidence long before the Academy had made it an examination subject.

"The work has become narrower," Damaris said. "Which is progress."

"Does it feel like progress?"

"Not always."

"Then perhaps it is the kind that lasts."

Rhyl listened without supplying her missing words.

Her mother noticed that too.

Later, when the cousin had gone to fetch a ledger and Rhyl was examining a small carving near the hearth, her mother leaned close enough that only Damaris could hear.

"You are happy with him."

Damaris did not look away.

"Yes."

"Good."

That was all.

She had expected advice, or an inquiry into his family, or a warning about the visibility of a relationship that had ceased to be merely private. Instead her mother touched her hand once and returned to the conversation.

Damaris was almost annoyed.

She had prepared several excellent defenses.

When they left, Rhyl took her hand on the front steps. The evening was cool. A servant was bringing lamps into the hall behind them.

"Well?" she asked.

"Your mother is formidable."

"That is not an answer."

"She likes you."

Damaris laughed.

"I should hope so."

"She watches whether people listen when you speak."

Damaris looked at him.

"Does she?"

"Yes."

"And did you pass?"

"I wasn't aware I was being examined."

"Rhyl."

He turned toward her.

"I listened."

She kissed him before he could say anything more, briefly enough to remain within the household's tolerance for front-step affection and thoroughly enough that the servant carrying the lamps found an urgent reason to look at the garden.

They walked home with their hands linked.

The dinner had not resolved the questions between family loyalty and independent choice. It had given those questions a room, a table, and witnesses who would remember that Rhyl had been invited.

That was enough to make tomorrow different.

---

The River Ledger had three copies of the road warning by breakfast, and none had arrived by way of Damaris.

One accompanied the carrier's account. One came from the coaching yard. The third was folded into a letter from the charcoal merchant, who wanted to know whether a signed warning counted as a reason to charge him more for a delivery he had already bought.

Damaris laid them side by side. The handwriting differed, but Rhyl's signature and the description of the missing ford markers agreed.

Lady Maeryn Caladren-Voss examined the merchant's letter over her spectacles. She wore a dark green gown with narrow silver buttons, and her hair had been pinned up to survive a day of arguing with borrowers and relations. A small pile of coin stood beside her inkstand. Damaris suspected it was there to remind visitors that money existed in pieces, not merely in accounts.

"Your cousin says the paved road is an extravagance," Maeryn said.

"He said the toll was an extravagance."

"By his third letter, I believe he meant the entire road."

The cousin stood at the window, his coat still damp from the morning mist. He had come straight from the warehouse, where the charcoal wagons had spent the night waiting for instructions.

"The contract allows the carrier to choose the route," he said. "It doesn't say we must buy him the dearest one."

"No," Damaris agreed. "It doesn't require his driver to take a loaded wagon into a ford he has been warned may be unsafe, either."

"The ford hasn't been closed."

"I know."

He sounded tired rather than angry. A kiln was waiting on the charcoal, and its owner had threatened to charge for every hour it stood cold. Her cousin was trying to get a wagon moving before the delay cost more than the toll.

Maeryn pushed the letters toward Damaris.

"Then tell me what we're paying for."

Damaris spread out the calculation she had made before coming: the toll, the additional distance, the driver's time, and the warehouse charge if the charcoal missed the next firing. She had also noted the cost of a broken axle, though that estimate ceased to be useful if a horse or driver was injured.

"The paved road is cheaper than another day in the yard."

"If they depart now," her cousin said.

"Yes."

"And if the ford proves safe tomorrow?"

"Then we've paid to avoid a risk we couldn't judge today."

He studied her. "Easy to say when it isn't your margin."

"It is our account. My name is on the recommendation."

Maeryn tapped the paper. "Your name isn't a purse. Which account bears it?"

Damaris turned to the next sheet. The charcoal had been priced before the thaw. The merchant had agreed to a fixed delivered price, and the carrier's ordinary toll allowance applied only to a route named in advance. No route had been named. The difference would come from the Caladren carrying margin.

Her cousin stared at the figures.

"We'll make almost nothing."

"On this delivery."

"That distinction will comfort the factor."

"Then the next contract needs a route contingency. We can't charge yesterday's customer for a clause we neglected to write."

Maeryn's mouth moved very slightly. Damaris had seen that expression before. It did not mean approval. It meant her aunt had found something worth testing.

"And your Red Hart gentleman?" Maeryn asked.

Damaris felt herself color. Her cousin became interested in the garden.

"What about him?"

"Would you have accepted the warning from a stranger?"

"Yes."

"Would you have paid the toll?"

"Yes."

"Would you have argued quite so fiercely?"

Last night Rhyl had sat at her mother's table and refused a favor that would have made this morning cheaper. She had been proud of him. She had also known, before her cousin asked, that he would refuse.

"I hope so," she said. "But I can't prove it by telling you how I feel about him."

"No," Maeryn said. "You can't."

She separated the coaching-yard copy from the others.

"Have the road office certify this for the contract file. Your gentleman needn't be asked for anything. If there is a dispute, I want an office copy and its issue date, not a dinner guest's recollection."

Damaris nodded. She had already drafted the request.

Her cousin picked up the calculation. "And the wagons?"

"Pay the toll," Maeryn said. "The carrying account bears the difference. I'll sign after the clerk verifies the rate."

He exhaled. "I could have had that answer yesterday."

"Yesterday you were trying to get a different one."

He did not deny it. He folded the calculation, unfolded it to check the distance, and went to inform the warehouse. He was good at his work; Damaris knew he would remember the loss when next season's prices were set. It would not be a sentimental lesson. Someone would have to explain a thinner profit to people expecting a thicker one.

At the door he paused. "Shall I tell the merchant we found the ford unsafe?"

"Tell him the crossing is under warning and the carrier has elected the paved road."

He nodded and left.

Maeryn put away her spectacles and reached for the next account. Damaris remained seated.

"You brought him to your mother's table," Maeryn said.

"I did."

"Was it pleasant?"

"Mostly."

"Your cousin says he was courteous. And difficult."

"He wouldn't give us something that wasn't ours."

"Good. I've known a great many men who give away other people's property and expect gratitude for it."

Damaris laughed.

"But don't make him a virtue you can spend instead of money," Maeryn added. "The driver still needs his toll. The kiln still needs charcoal. Your cousin still has to make the accounts balance."

"I know."

"You may have to know it again next week."

At the cash desk, Damaris waited while the clerk counted the advance into a canvas pouch. The driver came to collect it, cap in hand, with a crease between his brows.

"Is this for the paved road?"

"Yes."

"And the extra miles?"

"Entered here. Bring the toll receipt back."

He checked the amount with the clerk. He did not thank Damaris for doing what his employer should have arranged before dawn. He asked whether the warehouse had been told to expect him late. She showed him the notice.

"Then I'd best be off," he said.

He paused at the door. "The younger lad wanted to try the ford with an empty cart first. Told him he could do it on his own time."

"Was he annoyed?"

"Terribly."

He grinned and went down the steps, shaking the pouch to settle the coins.

Damaris watched him cross the yard. He was thinking about his team, the daylight and the delivery. He had no reason to care that the man whose warning had altered his route had kissed a Caladren daughter on her mother's front steps.

Back at her desk she turned to the harbor reconciliations. There were missing weights to query, a disputed wharf fee, and a miller's letter written in a hand so cramped she had to turn the page sideways. She worked until the bells marked noon.

Only then did she take a fresh sheet and write to Rhyl.

*Your warning cost us a toll, two letters and a morning's argument. I thought you would like to know the wagons took the paved road.*

She considered adding that she had been proud of him. The words looked rather grand for a note about charcoal.

She wrote them anyway, beneath a line about her aunt's opinion of men who gave away other people's property.

She sealed the note with her own small mark and left it for the ordinary yard messenger. She did not ask when Rhyl would be back from the ford. He had told her what he intended to do, and she had work enough to fill the time before he could tell her what he found.

---

At the Hall, the first bounded answer arrived just before the lamps were lit.

It was not an answer to who had been protected.

It was not an answer to who had caused Silver Night.

It was an acknowledgment that the civic review office maintained a procedural exception register for the relevant period, and that an authorized judge could seek a case-neutral confirmation of whether a specified dispute class had entered it.

The confirmation would be prepared by a separate custodian. The protected entry itself would not be released.

Ilyan read the answer and passed it to Damaris.

"Another request."

"Yes."

"Another fee."

"Probably."

"Another person who must leave ordinary work to satisfy our curiosity."

Damaris considered the paper.

"Not curiosity."

"No."

"But the cost is real."

She set it down.

Vaun had returned from a hearing and was standing at the window, unfastening the clasp of her cloak. The late light made the stone courtyard look almost golden.

"We can ask," Ilyan said, "whether the exception class was recorded. We cannot ask what was protected."

"Correct."

"And the register belongs to the civic review side."

"Yes."

"Salver's later Secretariat post gives us no automatic access path."

"None."

"But if we can establish what his later assignment actually required, we may learn whether he handled summaries of those disputes."

"Possibly."

He could live with possibly.

It was a small word. It left room for a clerk to have done his job without knowing anything monstrous. It left room for an office to have followed its own rules and for a protection to have worked as intended. It also left room for someone, somewhere, to have received a notice that mattered and acted upon it.

Vaun brought the request form to the table.

"Draft it."

Ilyan took the pen.

He began with the commission number, stopped, and looked at her.

"Is that too narrow? Could the combination itself disclose something?"

"That is why the custodian reviews the question before answering. Ask for the procedural class, not the protected circumstances. If the class itself is disclosive, we accept a refusal."

He stared at her.

"And pay for it."

"If the rules require a fee."

Damaris smiled into her cup.

Ilyan wrote the request.

Outside, the bells marked the end of the working day. Neral came in for the account book. The room smelled of cooling tea, wet wool, and the faint sweetness of the last uneaten hand pie.

There were people who could demand obedience with a seal and a family name. There were also people who could refuse, write down why, bear the delay, and return to work the following morning.

He had spent months looking for the hand that made the disaster possible.

He was beginning to notice how many other hands had spent those same days keeping smaller disasters from happening.

He signed the form.

The answer would take time.

For once, he did not mistake that for failure.

---

Two mornings later, Halen returned the application with a narrow strip of blue paper tucked beneath its string.

It had not been refused. The civic custodian had marked two lines for correction and sent it back by the ordinary messenger, who had also delivered a petition about a broken public well and a sack of account books smelling strongly of damp flour.

Ilyan read the first mark. Then he read it again.

"That is the commission number," he said.

"Yes," Halen replied.

"They already know the commission number."

"The receiving office knows it. The ordinary correspondence register does not need it."

They were standing at the public counter, where morning light caught the dust above a shelf of petition boxes. A cooper in a leather apron waited behind them with a complaint about a toll gate. He had been waiting before they arrived and made no effort to conceal his opinion of people who believed their questions should be answered at once.

Halen turned the paper around.

"You've put the commission reference in the heading and the exception class in the question. Either one might be harmless on its own. Together they tell the ordinary filing clerk which sealed proceeding you have in mind."

"I didn't ask for the contents."

"No. You gave the clerk a way to connect two things that ought not to be connected on this desk."

Damaris leaned close enough to read the blue slip. Her sleeve brushed Ilyan's hand. He had been preparing an argument and lost the first three words of it.

"She's right," Damaris said.

"I hadn't said she wasn't."

"You were about to."

"I was about to ask what we do instead."

Halen's expression suggested that she had heard the distinction before and did not intend to charge him for it.

"The judge can send the identifying particulars under seal to the custodian. The public request goes in separately, with a neutral reference. The answer, if there is one, comes back to the judge."

A boy at the far end of the room dropped a stack of blank forms. Nobody looked around except the cooper, who bent to help him gather them.

"Will that take longer?" Ilyan asked.

"Of course."

"How much?"

"I don't know. The custodian has other work."

The cooper straightened with the last of the forms in his hand.

"Some of us do too."

Ilyan turned. The man's face was tired rather than hostile.

"You're right," Ilyan said. "I'm sorry."

The cooper gave a small, surprised shrug. He was not obliged to forgive the delay. Halen called him forward and took his petition before addressing another word to Ilyan.

They stepped aside.

Damaris folded the blue slip into quarters. "You could have had this delivered to Vaun."

"Then Halen would have had to write the explanation again."

"She did write it."

"I wanted to hear what I'd done."

Damaris looked at him with a curiosity he knew from their Academy years. It had usually meant she was deciding whether he was becoming sensible or had merely found a more elaborate way to be difficult.

"Well," she said, "you heard."

Outside, the street was full of Bloomtide carts: cut branches for household garlands, baskets of soft cheese under damp cloth, a wagon of pale stone intended for a new charter hall. A woman in a red apron argued with a carrier over the number of broken flowerpots in his load. No one turned to watch the young Valeric heir leave a records office without an answer.

---

Judge Vaun received the returned application between two hearings. One concerned an enchanted boundary post moved during a drainage repair; the other had left her with a headache and an ink stain across the heel of her hand.

Neral brought fresh water. Damaris put the blue slip beside the draft request. Vaun read both without sitting.

"The clerk was right," she said.

"We've established that," Ilyan replied.

"Good. Then you won't mind paying to correct it."

He looked up.

"Paying?"

"The sealed delivery requires an authorized courier. The custodian's review is not free. Neither is my time, although that part of the bill will be paid by people who have never heard of you."

She removed her cloak and hung it beside the door. Its hem was dark with street water. For a moment she stood with her back to them, rubbing the ink from her hand with a cloth that had already failed at the task.

"I can sign an expedited request," she said. "I won't."

Ilyan had not asked her to. He found that he wanted to.

"Why not?"

"Nothing before me shows an imminent loss of evidence. The register is held under retention. We are not racing a fire."

"Someone might destroy it."

"Someone might. Have you a reason to think they will?"

He did not.

From the adjoining room came the scrape of a chair and a burst of angry voices. Vaun waited for them to subside.

"An expedited inspection would displace other applications. Some concern people who cannot wait to know whether they may enter their own homes. I won't put ours ahead of theirs because your family name makes the uncertainty difficult to bear."

The words stung. Neral set the water down and returned to the account book. Damaris had gone still. Ilyan could not tell whether she was embarrassed for him or simply tired of hearing the Valeric name spoken as though it were a separate person in the room.

"I understand," he said.

"Do you?"

"I want to say yes. I may complain about it later."

"That is allowed."

She sat and pulled a clean sheet toward her.

The new request did not contain the commission number on its public face. Neral assigned an ordinary office reference and copied it into the investigation account. Vaun wrote the identifying material on a separate sealed enclosure for the civic custodian. She asked whether the custodian could determine, without disclosing protected particulars, whether the relevant procedural class had generated an exception-register entry and whether any lawful office-class acknowledgment could be certified. She authorized the custodian to withhold even that limited answer if the combination would identify a protected matter.

Ilyan watched her strike out one sentence he had drafted.

"That was the part about Salver."

"Yes."

"Why remove it?"

"His later Secretariat employment has no demonstrated connection to the civic register. If we want his assignment terms, we ask the personnel office for those. We do not put his name into a sealed inquiry about somebody else's protected business."

Damaris reached for the discarded draft.

"I'll prepare the personnel request."

"Tomorrow," Neral said, without looking up.

She glanced at him.

"The last copy has to go out before the charter docket closes. And the runner is due in half an hour. Unless you wish to pay another messenger to carry a question we haven't finished."

Damaris made a face, but left the paper where it was.

Vaun signed the sealed request. The pen caught slightly on the last stroke. She sanded the signature and gave the sheet to Neral.

He checked the enclosure, string, and seal, and entered the expense against the authorized inquiry rather than Ilyan's private purse. Ilyan reached for his coin pouch anyway, then stopped. Damaris noticed. She did not smile.

When the courier came, he was a broad-shouldered man with mud on his boots and six deliveries on his list. Neral made him repeat the civic office address and watched him put the packet in the locked compartment of his satchel. The man accepted the receipt without ceremony. He had a dozen other things to carry before supper.

Only after the door closed did Vaun look at Ilyan again.

"The personnel question is yours to frame. You may ask what duties belonged to Salver's post, when the assignment began, and which office maintained its records. You may not assume that an office which later employed him received an earlier notice."

"I know."

"Then write as though you know."

He took the blank sheet Damaris passed him.

She sat opposite, one elbow resting on the table. He remembered how she used to watch him struggle with a translation at the Academy, refusing to supply a word until he had exhausted every wrong one. He had once believed that meant she enjoyed watching him fail. He was no longer certain it had ever meant that.

"Are you going to help?" he asked.

"Yes."

"You look as though you're waiting for me to make another mistake."

"I am."

"Comforting."

"Then don't disappoint me."

He laughed despite himself.

The draft began with the transfer date already established in the personnel book: two years after Silver Night. He wrote the post title next, then a request for the duties attached to it and the date on which those duties took effect. He left out the sealed commission, the name of the receiving authority, and the theory that had been keeping him awake.

Damaris corrected one office title and made him specify whether he wanted the original assignment order or a later summary. He chose the original, if retained, and a certified statement of unavailability if it was not.

"That last part will annoy them," he said.

"It may. But it gives them a way to tell the truth if the paper is gone."

When Neral returned, the new draft was ready for Vaun's review the following morning. It had not yet been sent.

The sealed inquiry was on its way to the civic custodian. The personnel request remained on the desk. Neither answer belonged to them yet.

Ilyan gathered the unused sheets and returned them to the drawer. Across the room Damaris was trying to work a kink out of the ribbon that held her notes together. She had dressed carefully that morning; he noticed the tiny crease near her collar and wondered what other obligations had kept her up late. He did not ask.

At the door she paused.

"Will you eat with us tonight?"

"Who's us?"

"Rhyl, if he's back from the ford. Possibly Mara. Whoever finishes work before the kitchen closes."

He considered the pile of records he still wanted to read.

"Yes," he said.

Damaris raised an eyebrow.

"Without bargaining?"

"Before you change your mind."

She went out ahead of him. The room smelled of wet wool and ink, and the afternoon was already slipping toward evening. The question that had kept him awake remained unanswered. He could carry it home without making everyone else stay at work to bear its weight.

---

By the time they reached the kitchen, the supper promised to Ilyan had become two bowls of barley stew and the end of a loaf. The cook had left them beneath a folded cloth, with a note asking that whoever ate last put the shutter bar back in its brackets. No one had stayed to serve them.

Damaris lifted the cloth. "She remembered you."

"She remembers everyone who comes in hungry."

"Not everyone gets the heel of the loaf."

Ilyan looked at the bread. It was the part he liked best, though he could not remember telling anyone. He sat while Damaris found spoons, and for several minutes neither of them spoke. The stew was thick with onions and a little sharp cheese. He had expected to be thinking about the sealed packet, but his body had more immediate opinions.

A third bowl stood upside down on the shelf.

"Rhyl?" he asked.

"If he's back before the kitchen closes. I haven't heard."

"Should someone send to the yard?"

"He knows where I am." She broke off a piece of bread. "He said he would inspect the ford. I said I would be here. We weren't making an appointment."

Ilyan watched her take a mouthful. She had glanced toward the door twice since they sat down, and he had been on the verge of asking whether she was worried. Now he suspected she was, and also that she would not thank him for making her account for it.

"I could ask Mara," he said.

"Mara has a boundary report to finish."

"Yes."

"And if you ask her where he is, she'll tell you what she is allowed to tell you, which may be nothing useful."

"You're making me sound terribly inquisitive."

"You are terribly inquisitive. It has become your principal occupation."

He was too hungry to defend himself properly. Damaris gave him the last of the bread and watched with open satisfaction as he accepted it.

Beside her bowl lay a narrow River Ledger book. He recognized the blue binding from her committee work, but the figures visible at the edge of the page meant nothing to him. She moved it away from the stew before the cover could be stained.

"Will you be at the Hall tomorrow?" he asked.

"Not until afternoon. Maeryn wants the harbor reconciliations before the charter sitting."

"Could I help?"

"No."

He raised his eyebrows.

"You don't know the carriers, and the carriers don't know you. I'd spend half the morning explaining why a Valeric was looking over their invoices."

"I do know how to add."

"That has never been the part of your education I questioned."

He laughed, and she smiled back, but did not open the book for him. Her refusal was so ordinary that it hardly seemed to deserve the name. She had work that belonged to her, people who relied upon it, and a lover whose patrol obligations did not become hers simply because she wished him home.

Ilyan thought of the public counter and Halen taking the cooper's petition while he stood aside. He had gone there hoping to find a person who would shorten the wait. Instead he had learned the cost of occupying someone else's place in it.

"Thessa would have told me to eat first," he said.

"Thessa would have taken the spoon out of your hand, eaten half your supper, and then told you to eat first."

"She is not quite so ruthless."

"Write and ask her."

"Her last letter hasn't reached me yet."

"Then write something that doesn't require an answer."

He considered that. The habit of correspondence had become a succession of questions with dates and seals attached. He could remember the last time he had asked Thessa how she was sleeping, but not whether he had waited long enough to hear the truth.

Damaris gathered the bowls. He stood to help, and she handed him the heavier one without ceremony. At the washstand, cold water ran over his wrists. The kitchen was quiet except for the scrape of crockery and the last sounds of carts outside.

When they finished, Damaris turned the third bowl upright and left it covered, with a small piece of bread beside it. She did not know whether Rhyl would come before the food cooled. She had no reason to pretend otherwise.

"I'll be late tomorrow," she said.

"I know."

"That means you have to remember your own lunch."

"An unreasonable burden."

"An adult one."

She took her ledger and went upstairs. Ilyan put the shutter bar back in place, as the cook had asked. The sealed inquiry had left the Hall. The personnel request had not. Neither fact had changed over supper.

He went home intending to write a letter that asked no one to prove anything.
