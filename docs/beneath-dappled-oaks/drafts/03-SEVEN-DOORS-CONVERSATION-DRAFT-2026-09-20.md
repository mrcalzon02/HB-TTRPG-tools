# Chapter Three
## Seven Doors

The morning after graduation, the Academy began giving them away.

It did so politely.

There were no chains.

No magistrates standing beneath the dappled oaks with writs of possession. No herald announced that seven young adults who had spent years eating from the same refectory tables, sleeping beneath the same roofs, borrowing one another’s books, insulting one another’s handwriting and surviving one another’s romances were henceforth property of seven different institutions.

Instead there were envelopes.

They appeared with breakfast.

Cream paper for Mireth.

White for Caelen.

Red-edged grey for Thessa.

Silver-blue for Lyrion.

River-green for Damaris.

Brown for Rhyl.

Black for Ilyan.

Seven doors disguised as stationery.

Mireth found hers propped against the silver coffee pot in the small dining room House Vael-Aesryn had occupied for graduation week.

She stared at it while an attendant placed warm seed bread beside her plate.

“Must it be there?”

The attendant followed her gaze.

“My lady?”

“The letter.”

“I was instructed to place it where you would see it.”

“I see many things. I do not necessarily want them beside my breakfast.”

The attendant possessed enough discipline not to smile.

Mireth admired her for that.

She picked up the envelope.

The seal belonged to the Green Covenant rather than her family: a branching tree worked around an open seed pod.

The wax was still faintly fragrant.

Rosemary.

Juniper.

Something green and bitter she could not identify.

Her aunt sat opposite her, already dressed.

Of course she was.

Aunt Selyra gave the impression that she awoke each morning fully assembled by political necessity.

Her gown was dark moss velvet. A narrow line of old gold followed the cuffs. Her black hair had been gathered into a braided knot pierced by two green pins.

She buttered toast.

“You should open it.”

“I was hoping that if ignored long enough it might lose confidence.”

“It is an institution.”

“Institutions are remarkably sensitive when denied attention.”

Selyra drank her tea.

Mireth broke the seal.

The letter was brief.

That worried her immediately.

Long letters explained themselves.

Short letters assumed obedience.

**Initiate Mireth Vael-Aesryn is requested to present herself at the House of Rooted Memory at second bell. Formal greens. No family attendants. Bring no written notes.**

Below that:

**You will be examined regarding remembrance, obligation and living testimony.**

Mireth read the sentence twice.

Selyra watched.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“That is your lying face.”

“I have a lying face?”

“You have several.”

Mireth folded the paper.

“Apparently I am being examined regarding remembrance.”

Selyra stopped buttering the toast.

Only for a heartbeat.

But Mireth saw.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“My lying face is hereditary.”

Her aunt looked toward the window.

Rain had passed during the night. The gardens beyond the glass were bright with wet leaves. Water still clung beneath the enormous oaks, occasionally dropping in silver threads when the wind moved through them.

“The Covenant remembers promises,” Selyra said.

“I know.”

“No.”

Her aunt looked back at her.

“You know the school version.”

Mireth felt something unpleasant settle beneath her ribs.

There it was again.

The phrase adults had begun using since graduation.

The school version.

As though everything before yesterday had been an elaborate children’s performance.

Mireth pushed the plate away.

“What is the adult version?”

Selyra considered her.

Then smiled faintly.

“You are about to learn.”

Mireth hated that answer.

More troublingly, some part of her wanted it.

That was perhaps the first betrayal adulthood demanded: curiosity regarding the machinery one had spent years claiming to despise.

She stood.

Selyra looked at the untouched bread.

“Eat.”

“I am not hungry.”

“You will be.”

“I can eat afterward.”

“You do not know when afterward is.”

Mireth looked at her aunt.

Selyra gestured toward the chair.

“Sit.”

Mireth sat.

The attendant returned with honey.

Mireth tore the bread.

She thought of Ilyan.

He had barely eaten yesterday.

Damaris had threatened him into taking bread.

They had all laughed.

For several minutes, anyway.

It seemed impossible that laughter had happened less than a day ago.

Thirty-six students and six servants were dead.

Yesterday the seven survivors had graduated.

Today the adults had appointments for them.

Civilization, Mireth thought, possessed astonishing administrative stamina.

---

The House of Rooted Memory stood beyond the Academy’s northern wall in a grove so old that the city had been forced to curve around it.

Mireth had passed the place hundreds of times.

She had never been invited inside.

That had once seemed unremarkable.

Now it seemed deliberate.

Students knew the outer gardens.

They attended public rites beneath the ash arches. They studied Green Covenant jurisprudence, conservation law, botanical magic, sacred-land agreements, living wards and the theory of arboreal testimony.

They did not enter the inner house.

Graduates did.

Adults did.

The difference was one night.

Mireth climbed the moss-dark steps.

There were no guards.

There did not need to be.

The doorway itself was alive.

Two white-barked trees had grown around one another until their trunks formed an arch. No carpenter had cut them. No mason had placed them. Their branches intertwined overhead and vanished into the roof.

Mireth touched the bark.

Warm.

The door opened.

Scholar Silae Vael-Aesryn waited inside.

Mireth stopped.

Her older cousin smiled.

“Silae.”

“Initiate.”

“You could have warned me.”

“I could have.”

“You did not.”

“No.”

“I dislike adulthood.”

“You have been practicing it for less than a day.”

“It has made a poor first impression.”

Silae laughed and embraced her.

That surprised Mireth more than the letter.

Silae smelled of parchment, cedar oil and the faint mineral scent of rain.

She had always been one of Mireth’s favorite cousins precisely because she seemed to possess no interest in becoming anyone’s mother, aunt, political instructor or moral improvement project.

She simply knew things.

Unfortunately, Mireth was beginning to suspect that knowing things was itself political.

Silae released her.

“Come.”

The interior was nothing like the Academy’s Green Covenant classrooms.

There were no desks.

No lecture platforms.

The walls were living wood polished by generations of hands. Roots emerged from the floor and had been shaped into benches, shelves, niches and low tables. Hundreds of clay tablets rested between them.

No paper.

Mireth noticed immediately.

“No written notes,” she said.

Silae nodded.

“Paper can be altered.”

“So can memory.”

“Yes.”

Silae smiled.

“That is why there are three trees.”

She led Mireth into a circular chamber.

Three enormous trunks rose through the floor.

One pale.

One dark.

One red-barked.

Their branches disappeared through openings in the ceiling.

Three chairs stood between them.

Mireth stopped.

The chairs were plain.

That was almost shocking.

No velvet.

No carved house insignia.

No ceremonial height intended to establish which grandmother had won a tariff dispute.

Just oak.

Silae sat.

Mireth remained standing.

“What is this?”

“Your first lesson.”

“I thought I had completed lessons.”

“That was the school version.”

Mireth groaned.

Silae’s eyes brightened.

“Everyone has said that to you already?”

“Constantly.”

“Good. It saves time.”

Mireth sat.

Silae folded her hands.

“Tell me what happened in the west tower.”

Everything inside Mireth stopped.

There it was.

No preparation.

No condolences.

No gentle approach.

She looked toward the trees.

“Which version?”

Silae’s expression changed.

Very slightly.

“Interesting.”

Mireth regretted the answer.

Too late.

Silae leaned back.

“What versions do you know?”

“The public version.”

“And?”

Mireth said nothing.

The pale tree above them rustled.

There was no wind.

Mireth looked up.

Silae followed her gaze.

“It is not reading your thoughts.”

“That is comforting.”

“It is listening.”

“Less comforting.”

“To what you say.”

Mireth stared at her cousin.

Silae’s face had become serious.

“Living-memory testimony is not mind reading. Remember that. The Covenant does not reach into people and extract truth. Anyone who tells you otherwise is either ignorant or trying to frighten you.”

“Then what does it do?”

“It remembers what was said in its presence.”

Mireth looked around.

Three trees.

Three witnesses.

Her skin prickled.

“For how long?”

“The oldest reliable testimony here is two hundred and seventeen years old.”

Mireth slowly looked back at her.

Silae continued.

“The tree does not decide whether a speaker lied. It preserves voice, cadence, magical resonance and the conditions of the statement. Jurists compare testimony. Investigators compare contradictions. Memory is evidence, not judgment.”

Mireth thought immediately of Ilyan.

Of his confession.

Of thirty-six dead students.

Silae saw something move across her face.

She did not ask.

That was worse.

“Why teach me this now?”

“Because yesterday you were a student.”

Mireth nearly snapped at her.

Silae raised one hand.

“I know. You are tired of hearing it.”

“Deeply.”

“But this matters.”

Her cousin leaned forward.

“Protected students are not normally brought into House litigation, succession disputes, covert investigations or dynastic intelligence. We teach you the law. We do not make you practice the ugliest parts of it.”

Mireth stared.

Silae’s voice softened.

“You were supposed to have a little longer.”

The words entered somewhere Mireth had not armored.

For a moment she was back beneath white mourning cloth.

Thirty-six chairs.

Six servants.

People who had not reached this morning.

People who would never receive envelopes.

Never complain about breakfast appointments.

Never learn the adult version.

Mireth looked down at her hands.

Silae waited.

That kindness nearly undid her.

Mireth breathed.

“Why were we shielded?”

“Because civilized people generally agree that using students as pieces in dynastic war is disgusting.”

“Generally.”

“Yes.”

“That word is doing considerable labor.”

Silae smiled sadly.

“It often does.”

Mireth looked at the three trees.

“And someone ignored that.”

“Yes.”

The answer came too quickly.

Mireth’s head turned.

Silae had gone still.

“What do you know?”

“Not enough.”

“That is not an answer.”

“It is exactly an answer.”

“Silae.”

Her cousin looked toward the dark-barked tree.

“When a protected generation is attacked before graduation, the question is not merely who hated them now.”

Mireth listened.

“It is who feared what they would become.”

A chill moved across her shoulders.

Dmitrigical chairs.

Licenses.

Tribunals.

The motive Ilyan had already described.

But Silae continued.

“Seats. Marriages. children. alliances. heirs.”

Mireth’s breath caught.

Silae looked at her.

“Some houses count generations the way Caladren counts ships.”

Mireth remembered Damaris complaining once that her relatives could calculate the future value of a wedding before the couple finished dancing.

She had thought it funny.

It was still funny.

That was perhaps the horrible part.

“What are you telling me?”

“That Silver Night may have killed more than thirty-six students.”

Mireth frowned.

“There were thirty-six.”

“Yes.”

Silae’s gaze remained steady.

“But a murdered heir also kills every child they might have had.”

Silence.

Mireth felt suddenly cold.

“Whose family?”

“I do not know.”

“You suspect.”

“I have heard demographic arguments.”

“From whom?”

Silae shook her head.

“Not yet.”

Mireth stared.

“You brought me into a room that remembers everything and then refuse to speak?”

“Precisely.”

“That is maddening.”

“It is also why this room still has legal authority.”

Mireth stood.

Silae did not stop her.

“Ilyan said—”

She stopped.

Too late.

Again.

Silae’s eyes sharpened.

Mireth cursed silently.

“Ilyan said what?”

“Nothing.”

“Mireth.”

She turned away.

The red-barked tree rustled.

Damn the tree too.

Silae rose.

“Whatever you are protecting, understand this before you leave.”

Mireth looked back.

“Living memory can preserve testimony.”

Silae’s expression had become grave.

“It can also prove that someone never said what another person later claims they said.”

Mireth understood.

A contradiction.

Not proof.

But something independently testable.

Exactly what they needed.

Silae touched her arm.

“And Mireth?”

“Yes?”

“If you begin investigating Silver Night, do not assume the person who committed the act and the person who benefited from it are always the same.”

Mireth said nothing.

She carried that sentence with her when she left.

---

Caelen’s door was made of steel.

Literally.

The Royal Martial Fellowship occupied an old Crown barracks along the eastern rise, where the streets climbed steeply enough that horses disliked them and military architects considered this evidence of moral weakness in horses.

Caelen arrived at third bell.

Commander Ysran Avariel-Caith was waiting.

Caelen recognized his older cousin immediately.

Everyone did.

Ysran was difficult not to recognize.

He stood well over six feet, broad across the shoulders, dark hair cut short in military fashion, one ear visibly notched from an old blade wound. His formal uniform was midnight blue rather than black, silver piping following the chest and cuffs.

A sword hung at his hip.

Not ceremonial.

Caelen looked at it.

Ysran noticed.

“Still checking weapons before faces?”

“Faces are usually less immediately dangerous.”

“Court will cure you of that.”

Caelen smiled.

They embraced.

Ysran gripped his shoulder.

“You look older.”

“I graduated yesterday.”

“That explains everything.”

Ysran stepped back.

His eyes went immediately to Caelen’s chin.

“Keep the scar.”

Caelen stared.

“No.”

“What?”

“I have heard enough opinions about my chin.”

Ysran laughed.

“Woman?”

“Several.”

“Excellent.”

“No.”

“Same woman?”

Caelen walked past him.

Ysran followed.

“Ah.”

“Stop.”

“House?”

“Stop.”

“Vael?”

Caelen stopped.

Ysran nearly walked into him.

Caelen looked over his shoulder.

“How does everyone know?”

“You have been staring at Mireth Vael-Aesryn since you were old enough to understand that staring can become a problem.”

Caelen resumed walking.

“I hate this family.”

“No, you don’t.”

“Temporarily.”

“That is allowed.”

The training hall beyond the steel door smelled of oil, leather, old wood and sweat.

Caelen relaxed immediately.

Weapons he understood.

Racks lined the walls.

Practice swords.

Spears.

Shields.

Blunted polearms.

Training circles had been painted into the floor.

Several young officers sparred beneath high windows.

No velvet.

No diplomatic seating arrangements.

No mothers discussing marriage.

Caelen exhaled.

Ysran noticed.

“Comfortable?”

“Yes.”

“Good.”

Then he punched him.

Caelen barely got his arm up.

The blow drove him sideways.

“What in the hell—”

Ysran attacked again.

Caelen moved.

Training took over.

Block.

Turn.

Elbow.

Ysran caught it.

Sweep.

Caelen jumped.

They collided shoulder to shoulder.

Ysran laughed.

Caelen did not.

“You could have warned me.”

“Enemies often do.”

“That is proverbially false.”

Caelen drove forward.

Ysran gave ground.

For several minutes there was no politics.

Only movement.

Breath.

Weight.

The clean honesty of bodies obeying physics.

Caelen loved it.

That frightened him sometimes.

He caught Ysran’s wrist.

Turned.

Nearly threw him.

Ysran twisted free.

Then stopped.

“Better.”

Caelen breathed hard.

“You hit me before breakfast.”

“It is nearly midday.”

“I had a complicated morning.”

“Mireth?”

Caelen lunged.

Ysran laughed again.

Eventually they sat on the edge of the training platform drinking water.

Sweat darkened Caelen’s shirt between his shoulders.

His hair had come loose.

Ysran looked at him.

“Now the unpleasant part.”

“Marriage?”

“Service.”

“Worse.”

Ysran’s humor disappeared.

“You are Moon-Crown now.”

Caelen nodded.

“That means something.”

“I know.”

“No.”

Ysran’s expression became almost sympathetic.

“You know the school version.”

Caelen shut his eyes.

“If one more person says that phrase, I am going to become a hermit.”

“You would last three days.”

“Four.”

“Two if Mireth remained in the city.”

Caelen looked at him.

Ysran raised both hands.

“Finished.”

He was not.

They both knew it.

Ysran leaned forward.

“Royal service means you will know things your friends cannot know.”

Caelen’s smile disappeared.

“There it is.”

“What?”

“The same warning.”

“From?”

“My mother.”

Ysran considered that.

“Then she is right.”

Caelen looked toward the training circles.

“What if the Crown knows something about Silver Night?”

Ysran did not answer.

Caelen looked back.

His cousin’s face had changed.

Not guilt.

Not recognition exactly.

Calculation.

That was enough.

“What?”

“Caelen.”

“What?”

“You are one day into service.”

“Thirty-six of my classmates are dead.”

“And six servants.”

Caelen stopped.

Ysran held his gaze.

“Yes,” the commander said. “Remember them too.”

Shame struck immediately.

Caelen looked down.

“Fair.”

“The servants matter because people will eventually begin talking as though Silver Night was only a noble succession problem.”

Ysran’s voice was hard now.

“Six people died because they happened to work near important children.”

Caelen nodded.

Ysran continued.

“The Crown possesses records.”

Caelen looked up.

“Which records?”

“I did not say.”

“You just did.”

“No. I said records exist.”

“About Silver Night?”

“Records exist about everything.”

Caelen laughed once.

“Gods, you people become irritating quickly.”

“You people?”

“Adults.”

Ysran’s mouth twitched.

Then he stood.

“Your first assignment is tomorrow.”

Caelen rose.

“What?”

“Escort.”

“Whom?”

“An archive courier.”

Caelen stared.

Ysran handed him a folded paper.

Moon-Crown seal.

Route.

Time.

Destination.

Caelen read it.

Hall of Veils.

Truth Beyond the Veil.

His pulse changed.

Ysran watched him.

“Problem?”

Caelen folded the paper.

“No.”

It was his first deliberate lie in royal service.

Ysran looked at him for several seconds.

Caelen wondered if it had been convincing.

He could lie to instructors.

Guards.

Strangers.

He had done it often enough as a student.

But Ysran had known him since childhood.

The commander said nothing.

That was somehow worse.

---

Thessa’s door opened onto a courtyard full of soldiers.

Lyrion’s opened onto a room full of diplomats.

They met afterward and immediately agreed that Thessa had received the better arrangement.

“At least soldiers admit they are armed,” Lyrion said.

They walked beneath an arcade overlooking the western gardens.

Late afternoon light had turned the wet stone pale gold.

Thessa carried her Warden cloak over one shoulder.

She had spent three hours learning regional authority structures, emergency mobilization procedure and exactly how many different relatives believed her new office should improve Eris influence over the western roads.

Lyrion looked infuriatingly untouched.

His silver-blue diplomatic coat remained immaculate.

Thessa distrusted anyone who could spend an afternoon in administrative instruction without wrinkling.

“What did they ask you?” she said.

“To listen.”

“That sounds harmless.”

“It was terrifying.”

She looked at him.

Lyrion smiled.

“They explained that friendship is access.”

Thessa stopped walking.

Lyrion stopped too.

There.

One sentence.

The same lesson.

Again.

“What exactly did they say?”

“That people reveal more to friends than negotiators.”

Thessa’s jaw tightened.

“And?”

“And that my academy friendships represent an unusual cross-institutional network.”

“Us.”

“Yes.”

“Did they tell you to spy on us?”

Lyrion looked away.

That was answer enough.

Thessa felt something ugly twist beneath her ribs.

“Lyrion.”

“They told me to remain attentive.”

“That is diplomatic language.”

“I am a diplomat.”

“It means spy.”

“Sometimes.”

She stepped closer.

“On me?”

His eyes returned to hers.

The playfulness was gone.

“No.”

“You answered too quickly.”

“Because that answer was easy.”

Thessa stared.

He was close.

Closer than necessary.

They had always done this.

Long before graduation.

Long before adults discovered their friendship had strategic value.

Lyrion’s face was familiar enough that she knew the tiny scar near his left eyebrow, the one he insisted came from a duel and she knew came from falling out of a window at seventeen.

His collar sat slightly open.

She could see the hollow at the base of his throat.

Her attention remained there.

Damn him.

Lyrion noticed.

His voice softened.

“You are staring.”

“I am deciding whether to hit you.”

“That usually involves my face.”

“Not always.”

He smiled.

Thessa should have stepped back.

She did not.

The arcade had become quiet.

A breeze moved through the garden.

Water fell from leaves.

Lyrion lifted one hand.

Slowly.

He touched a loose strand of copper hair near her cheek.

Not her skin.

Almost.

That was worse.

Thessa’s breath changed.

His fingers caught the strand and tucked it behind her ear.

A small gesture.

Old familiarity.

Except nothing felt old today.

Everything they had done as students had acquired new meaning overnight.

Lyrion’s fingers remained near her ear.

Thessa could feel their warmth without being touched.

She looked at his mouth.

A mistake.

He saw.

Of course he saw.

The smile vanished.

Something else replaced it.

His hand moved to the side of her neck.

Barely.

Thumb beneath her jaw.

Thessa inhaled.

There were a thousand reasons not to.

Their Houses.

Their orders.

The instructions.

The possibility that someone watched from the windows.

The fact that yesterday they had been students and today apparently every touch could become intelligence.

Lyrion leaned closer.

Thessa whispered:

“Were you told to do this?”

He stopped.

The question struck him harder than a slap.

His hand fell.

Immediately.

Thessa regretted it.

Immediately.

Lyrion stepped back.

“No.”

“I did not mean—”

“Yes, you did.”

“Lyrion.”

He looked away.

She caught his wrist.

“Look at me.”

He did.

Hurt.

Not dramatic.

Worse.

Real.

Thessa swallowed.

“I hate that I had to ask.”

“So do I.”

“I still had to.”

“I know.”

They stood holding one another’s wrists like duelists who had forgotten their weapons.

Thessa loosened her grip.

Lyrion turned his hand.

Their palms met.

“Were you?” he asked.

“What?”

“Told to use me?”

“No.”

He searched her face.

She hated that he needed to.

Then he nodded.

“Good.”

Thessa stepped closer again.

Not as close as before.

Enough.

“What were you told?”

“To learn what the Silver Bough knows about Warden movements?”

His eyebrow lifted.

“That sounded suspiciously like a question.”

“Because it was.”

“You are terrible at espionage.”

“I graduated yesterday.”

Lyrion laughed.

The tension broke.

Not completely.

Perhaps never completely again.

He squeezed her hand.

“My first formal reception is in three nights.”

“Congratulations.”

“You are invited.”

“Why?”

“Because I requested you.”

Thessa looked at him.

There.

An active plan.

His.

Not his family’s.

Not his order’s.

His.

“Why?”

Lyrion’s eyes moved over her face.

Then lower.

The red Warden uniform fitted her closely at the waist, black leather reinforcing the shoulders and ribs. She had spent the entire afternoon hating it.

The way he looked at her changed her opinion somewhat.

“Because,” he said, “I would like to see what you wear.”

Thessa felt heat rise into her face.

“Idiot.”

“Yes.”

She released his hand.

Then took it again.

Neither mentioned that.

Damaris’s induction involved ledgers.

Of course it did.

The River Ledger maintained its Academy offices in a long blue building beside the eastern canal, where barges moved beneath willow branches and clerks could observe commerce while pretending it was scenery.

Damaris sat before Lady Meris Caladren-Ilyra.

Her aunt.

Wonderful.

Meris was beautiful in the severe Caladren fashion: dark hair, dark eyes, river-blue gown, no jewelry except a narrow chain of linked silver coins.

She placed three documents before Damaris.

“Read.”

Damaris read.

Grain shipments.

Bridge tolls.

Insurance.

Nothing interesting.

Then she noticed the dates.

Silver Night.

One shipment had been delayed the evening before the disaster.

Another had been redirected.

A third—

Damaris looked up.

Meris smiled.

“You see it.”

“The Academy received fewer alchemical stabilizers that week.”

“Did it?”

“That is what this says.”

“No. It says the shipment was reassigned.”

“To whom?”

“Excellent.”

Damaris looked again.

No destination.

Just an authorization mark.

Valeric?

She searched for it.

Nothing.

Meris watched.

Damaris’s pulse accelerated.

Was this a test?

Did her aunt know?

Did she know Damaris knew?

That was adulthood, apparently: wondering whether every document had been deliberately placed where you could see it.

“Why show me this?”

“To teach you that politics leaves receipts.”

Damaris looked at her.

Meris leaned back.

“People lie. Ledgers are more difficult.”

“They can be falsified.”

“Yes.”

“Destroyed.”

“Yes.”

“Rewritten.”

“Yes.”

Meris smiled.

“And every alteration creates another transaction.”

Damaris thought of Ilyan.

He needed independent evidence.

This was not enough.

But it was something.

A discrepancy.

Exactly the sort of thing that could make an official story bleed.

She memorized the authorization mark.

Meris saw her doing it.

Naturally.

“Good.”

Damaris’s stomach tightened.

“What?”

“Remember it.”

“Why?”

“Because Ilyan may recognize it.”

There.

No ambiguity.

Damaris stared at her aunt.

Meris folded her hands.

“You were instructed to gain his trust.”

“I remember.”

“You sounded displeased.”

“He is my friend.”

“Good.”

Damaris blinked.

Meris continued.

“People trust friends more easily.”

Disgust rose in her throat.

Her aunt saw.

“Oh, do not look at me like that.”

“How should I look?”

“Like an adult.”

“There it is.”

“What?”

“Nothing.”

Meris sighed.

“Damaris, your friendship with Ilyan existed before this assignment.”

“Yes.”

“I am not asking you to manufacture affection.”

“That does not improve it.”

“No.”

The admission surprised her.

Meris looked toward the canal.

“I am asking you to decide whether protecting your friend and protecting your family are always the same thing.”

Damaris said nothing.

“Sometimes they will be.”

“And when they are not?”

Her aunt looked back.

“That is why adulthood is difficult.”

Damaris hated her for making sense.

Meris slid another paper forward.

An invitation.

Three nights hence.

Silver Bough reception.

Lyrion.

Damaris nearly laughed.

Of course.

The seven were separating.

And already the world was arranging to put them back in the same rooms.

Rhyl received a horse.

This immediately convinced him he had chosen the correct order.

The mare was dark chestnut, narrow-faced and deeply unimpressed by him.

Rhyl stroked her neck.

“She hates me.”

Mara Orthell-Faen leaned against the stable door.

“She has standards.”

Rhyl looked at his cousin.

Mara smiled.

She wore Red Hart field leathers, mud still dried along one boot.

No ceremonial nonsense.

Rhyl envied her.

“What is her name?”

“Bracken.”

“Original.”

“She bit the last man who suggested changing it.”

“Bracken is beautiful.”

The mare flicked one ear.

Mara handed him a leather packet.

“Your first route.”

Rhyl opened it.

Western road.

Academy district.

River quarter.

Three villages.

A return through the outer Greenwood.

“Courier?”

“Observation.”

“Of what?”

“Road conditions. Bridge traffic. Unusual travelers. Damage.”

Rhyl frowned.

“That sounds simple.”

“It is.”

“Everyone else is being recruited into conspiracies.”

Mara shrugged.

“Roads are conspiracies with drainage.”

Rhyl laughed.

Then noticed the final note.

**Inspect retaining wall below Academy western service road. Recent repair.**

He looked up.

“Why?”

“Subsidence.”

“Near the west tower?”

“Below it.”

Rhyl’s attention sharpened.

Mara noticed.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“That was poor.”

“I know.”

She studied him.

Then, unexpectedly, she let it go.

Rhyl looked again at the route.

Mobility.

Observation.

A reason to go where the others could not.

His assignment looked apolitical.

Perhaps it was.

That itself might prove useful.

He folded the packet.

Then thought of Damaris.

Her emerald dress.

Her hand over Ilyan’s yesterday.

The way he had hated seeing it.

That embarrassed him now.

Not because he did not want her.

He did.

That was increasingly difficult to pretend otherwise.

But Ilyan was their friend.

And Damaris had looked frightened, not seductive.

Rhyl had mistaken the gesture because he had wanted it to mean something about himself.

That was foolish.

He would not do it again.

Probably.

Mara watched his face.

“Woman?”

Rhyl stared.

“Why does everyone do that?”

“Do what?”

“Know.”

She laughed.

“Because you are twenty-something and looking miserable beside a perfectly good horse.”

Rhyl mounted Bracken.

The mare tried to bite his boot.

“Excellent,” he muttered.

Mara grinned.

“Whoever she is, the horse already disapproves.”

Ilyan’s door was black.

No ornament.

No handle.

He stood before it at sunset.

The Hall of Veils had emptied after his induction, but the deeper offices of Truth Beyond the Veil occupied a structure behind it that students never saw.

Students.

Again.

The distinction.

Yesterday he had been one.

Today he wore the black ring.

The door opened.

His uncle stood beyond it.

Ilyan’s stomach turned.

“Congratulations,” the man said.

Ilyan did not answer.

His uncle smiled.

“You altered the oath.”

“Yes.”

“Dramatically.”

“I meant it.”

“I know.”

That disturbed Ilyan more than anger would have.

His uncle stepped aside.

“Come.”

The corridor beyond was narrow.

Stone walls.

Black lamps.

No windows.

Doors on either side.

Seven, Ilyan counted.

Then more beyond the turn.

His uncle walked slowly.

“You believe this order has become corrupt.”

Ilyan looked at him.

“Have I said that?”

“No.”

“Then why assume it?”

“Because I know you.”

Ilyan almost laughed.

“Do you?”

“Yes.”

The answer was gentle.

That was unbearable.

His uncle stopped before an archive alcove.

Shelves disappeared behind iron lattice.

Hundreds of black document boxes.

Each labeled.

Ilyan’s pulse hammered.

Somewhere in here.

Silver Night.

His uncle followed his gaze.

“Truth is not morality, Ilyan.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

“Yes.”

“Truth can start wars.”

“So can lies.”

“Of course.”

His uncle smiled.

“That is why you may become good at this.”

Ilyan hated the small surge of pride the sentence caused.

Hated it.

Family did that.

A compliment from someone monstrous did not stop being a compliment when some childhood part of you still wanted their approval.

His uncle unlocked the lattice.

“Your first responsibility is indexing historical oath forms.”

Ilyan went still.

“What?”

“Oaths.”

His uncle looked amused.

“You objected publicly to the current wording. It seems appropriate that you learn what you were objecting to.”

Trap.

Opportunity.

Both.

Ilyan stepped inside.

Boxes.

Years.

Versions.

Exactly what he needed.

His uncle remained at the entrance.

“Do not remove documents.”

“I know.”

“Do not copy restricted language.”

“I know.”

“Do not discuss sealed oath forms outside the Order.”

Ilyan looked at him.

“There it is.”

“What?”

“Necessary truth.”

His uncle’s smile disappeared.

For the first time.

Good.

Ilyan walked deeper between the shelves.

He found the current year.

Then the previous.

Then ten years before.

His hands trembled slightly.

He opened a box.

Oath form.

Black ink.

Formal hand.

He scanned.

Nothing.

No necessary truth.

His breathing changed.

He checked another.

Twenty years.

Nothing.

Thirty.

Nothing.

He looked toward the lattice.

His uncle was gone.

Ilyan returned to the shelf.

The altered phrase was new.

Not ancient doctrine.

Not ceremonial tradition.

New.

He kept searching.

Then he found something else.

A requisition slip tucked between two forms.

Wrong place.

He nearly ignored it.

Then saw the date.

Three days before Silver Night.

**Additional witness screens requested.**

**Additional sealed testimony capacity requested.**

Reason:

**Anticipated academy incident.**

Ilyan stopped breathing.

No.

He read it again.

Anticipated.

Not response.

Before.

Someone inside Truth Beyond the Veil had prepared to receive testimony before the west tower fell.

His hands became cold.

He looked at the authorization signature.

Not his uncle.

Not anyone he recognized.

A title.

**Deputy Keeper of Necessary Records.**

Necessary.

Again.

Footsteps sounded beyond the shelves.

Ilyan replaced the slip exactly where he had found it.

His uncle appeared at the end of the aisle.

“Learning?”

Ilyan turned.

Every instinct screamed.

Do not react.

Do not show him.

He thought of Damaris.

She could smile through almost anything.

Of Lyrion, who could turn embarrassment into performance.

Of Mireth, who became sarcastic when frightened.

Of Caelen, whose face betrayed him whenever someone mentioned Mireth.

Ilyan nearly smiled at that.

His uncle saw.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“Find something amusing?”

Ilyan looked at the old oath form.

“Yes.”

“What?”

He lifted it.

“You were right.”

His uncle’s eyes narrowed.

“About?”

“I did not understand what I was objecting to.”

That was true.

Not the truth his uncle heard.

The distinction made Ilyan feel ill.

His uncle smiled.

“There you are.”

Approval.

Again.

Poisonously warm.

Ilyan hated himself for feeling it.

They met beneath the oldest oak after dark.

Not officially.

No invitation.

No family knowledge.

At least they hoped.

The moon was not yet full, and its wounded face hung between the branches while rainwater glittered along the leaves.

Mireth arrived first.

Caelen second.

He looked tired.

His hair was still damp from training.

There was a bruise beginning along his jaw.

Mireth stared at it.

Caelen noticed.

“Not the scar this time?”

“Who hit you?”

“My cousin.”

“Why?”

“Instruction.”

“Your family teaches affection strangely.”

“You have met my mother.”

Mireth smiled.

Then touched the bruise.

Without thinking.

Two fingers.

Lightly.

Caelen went very still.

So did she.

His skin was warm beneath her fingertips.

Stubble rough against them.

The scar lay just below.

Mireth should have withdrawn.

She did not.

Caelen’s eyes moved to her mouth.

The world narrowed.

Again.

Always with him now.

She felt his breath against her wrist.

His hand rose.

Stopped beside her waist.

Not touching.

Asking.

That almost-touch was somehow more intimate than if he had simply taken hold of her.

Mireth’s pulse beat against her throat.

She looked at his hand.

Then back at him.

“Caelen.”

“Yes?”

She had forgotten what she intended to say.

Annoying.

His fingers finally touched the fabric at her waist.

Barely.

The pressure was so slight that she could have pretended it accidental.

Neither of them did.

Mireth moved closer.

The green stone at her throat brushed his coat.

Caelen looked down at it.

Then at her.

His voice was rougher.

“You keep doing that.”

“What?”

“Standing close enough that I forget you are usually dangerous.”

Mireth smiled.

“You should improve your memory.”

His thumb moved once against her waist.

Her breath caught.

There.

He heard it.

His expression changed.

The smugness vanished.

That made everything worse.

For a heartbeat they simply looked at one another.

No houses.

No orders.

No dead.

A lie, of course.

All of those things remained.

That was precisely why the moment mattered.

Caelen leaned down.

Mireth lifted her face.

A branch moved overhead.

Footsteps approached.

They separated so quickly that Mireth nearly laughed.

Thessa emerged from the path.

Lyrion beside her.

Their hands were not touching.

Suspiciously.

Damaris came next.

Rhyl after her.

Ilyan last.

Seven.

Again.

For now.

They stood beneath the tree that had watched them become students.

Now adults.

Apparently.

Mireth looked around.

“What did they ask of everyone?”

Silence.

Then Lyrion laughed.

“All of us?”

“Yes.”

“Everything?”

“Yes.”

Caelen looked at Mireth.

“This could take a while.”

“Good.”

They sat beneath the oak.

On roots.

On damp stone.

On the grass.

No ceremonial chairs.

No velvet.

No hierarchy.

One by one, they told each other.

Not everything.

That would have been impossible already.

But enough.

Mireth told them about living-memory testimony.

About demographic pressure.

Not the suspected family.

Silae had not given her one.

Caelen told them about his courier assignment.

Hall of Veils.

Tomorrow.

Ilyan looked sharply at him.

Thessa described Warden interest in Silver Night records.

Lyrion admitted that the Silver Bough had identified their friendship as an intelligence network.

Damaris showed them the authorization mark she had memorized.

Rhyl described the repaired retaining wall beneath the western service road.

Then Ilyan spoke.

Old oath forms.

No necessary-truth language.

The phrase was new.

And the requisition.

Three days before Silver Night.

Anticipated academy incident.

No one spoke for a long time.

The oak moved overhead.

Mireth felt suddenly very young.

Not childish.

Young.

There was a difference.

Yesterday they had crossed a line everyone told them was adulthood.

Today they had discovered that adults had been waiting on the other side with secrets.

Caelen looked at Ilyan.

“Can you get the requisition?”

“No.”

“Copy it?”

“Not safely.”

“Remember it?”

“Yes.”

Damaris leaned forward.

“The authorization title.”

“Deputy Keeper of Necessary Records.”

Her expression changed.

“What?”

She looked at Caelen.

“Your courier.”

He understood.

“Archive courier.”

“To the Hall of Veils.”

Ilyan’s pulse jumped.

Thessa looked between them.

“What are you thinking?”

Damaris smiled.

It was not a pleasant smile.

“Politics leaves receipts.”

Rhyl looked at her.

“Who taught you that?”

“My aunt.”

“You dislike your aunt.”

“Yes.”

“And you are using her lesson.”

“Yes.”

Rhyl smiled.

Damaris looked at him.

Something warm passed between them.

Then she remembered Ilyan.

Her smile faded.

Not guilt exactly.

Responsibility.

Good.

Mireth noticed.

She would remember that.

They all would.

That was the danger of seven friends.

Someone always remembered.

Ilyan looked around the circle.

“We have our first independent contradiction.”

“Not proof,” Mireth said.

“No.”

“Better.”

He looked at her.

“Better?”

“Something we can test.”

She thought of the three trees.

Of memory preserved without judgment.

Of Silae’s warning.

The person who committed the act and the person who benefited from it are not always the same.

Caelen stood.

“Tomorrow I escort an archive courier into the Hall of Veils.”

Ilyan stood too.

“I can determine where incoming records are registered.”

Damaris rose.

“I can identify whether the authorization mark appears in Caladren shipping records.”

Thessa stood.

“The Wardens want records.”

Lyrion smiled.

“The Silver Bough wants access.”

Rhyl remained seated.

Everyone looked at him.

He shrugged.

“I have a horse.”

For several seconds no one said anything.

Then Mireth laughed.

Thessa joined her.

Lyrion.

Damaris.

Caelen.

Even Ilyan.

Rhyl looked offended.

“I am serious.”

“That,” Damaris said, still laughing, “is what makes it funny.”

He stood.

She brushed grass from his sleeve.

An ordinary gesture.

Rhyl looked down at her hand.

Damaris noticed.

She left it there a moment longer than necessary.

Then withdrew.

Small things.

Everywhere.

The world would use them if it could.

Perhaps they would use the world back.

Mireth looked at the six people around her.

Yesterday, they had been students protected—imperfectly, disastrously—by a social rule none of them had fully appreciated until someone broke it.

Today, they belonged to seven institutions.

Seven Houses or branches.

Seven sets of expectations.

Seven doors.

And yet here they were beneath the same oak.

Caelen moved beside Mireth.

His hand found hers in the darkness.

No audience.

No mothers.

No political benefit she could identify.

She let him hold it.

That mattered.

Perhaps not enough.

Perhaps more than anything.

Above them, the wounded moon showed through the branches.

And somewhere beyond the Academy walls, ledgers, oath forms, courier manifests, living trees and a repaired retaining wall had begun telling different pieces of the same story.

Silver Night had been planned.

Not merely by the men who had caused the wards to fail.

Someone had expected the aftermath.

Someone had prepared for testimony.

Someone had moved supplies before the dead knew they were in danger.

And for the first time since Ilyan’s confession, the seven possessed something more useful than horror.

They possessed places to look.
