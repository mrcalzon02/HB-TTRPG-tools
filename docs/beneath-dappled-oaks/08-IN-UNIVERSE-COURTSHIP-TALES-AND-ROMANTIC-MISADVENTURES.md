# Beneath Dappled Oaks — The Young Lady's Book of Courtship Misadventures

This document preserves short in-universe stories, anecdotes, cautionary tales, popular comic episodes, household legends, and romantic disasters circulated among adults in Elaranth.

The stories are not necessarily documentary history.

Some are memoir.

Some are exaggerated family stories.

Some are serialized fiction pretending to be memoir.

Some began as village gossip and acquired a moral only after the third retelling.

All romantic or sexualized characters in these tales are adults.

A recurring cultural pleasure of the genre is the young adult who believes she has invented courtship, seduction, jealousy, or scandal and then discovers that mothers, grandmothers, widows, old maids, retired servants, and respectable matrons have been dealing with exactly the same foolishness for generations.

# I. The Adraderis Biscuits of Bellweather Green

Common title:

**The Biscuits That Emptied Bellweather Green**

Alternate title:

**Why One Labels the Adraderis Tin**

Traditional attribution:

Often printed as an anonymous "young lady's confession," though several editions attribute it to **Meriel Daven**, an adult daughter of a prosperous Bellweather grain factor.

Whether Meriel existed is disputed.

The village insists she did.

Three neighboring villages insist Bellweather invented her to excuse what happened.

## The tale

Meriel Daven was newly betrothed and very much in love.

She was also twenty-one, which in later editions is presented as sufficient explanation for most of what follows.

Her betrothed, Charlie, was affectionate, handsome, patient, and—in Meriel's private opinion—altogether too calm about the matter of being betrothed to her.

A traveling apothecary had recently sold Meriel a packet of **Adraderis petals**, a famous adult aphrodisiac herb.

Adraderis was legal to possess and used openly in some marital tonics, honeymoon wines, fertility celebrations, and mutually agreed romantic preparations.

Secretly administering it to another person was another matter entirely.

Meriel, being in possession of twenty-one years of accumulated wisdom, concluded that this distinction was largely theoretical.

She baked butter biscuits.

She added honey.

She added spice.

She added Adraderis.

She added rather more Adraderis than the apothecary had recommended.

Her plan was simple.

The household maid would carry the plate to Charlie when he arrived.

Charlie would eat one or two.

Charlie would finally stop behaving as if courtship were a matter to be approached with the patience of a bridge engineer.

Meriel congratulated herself on the elegance of the scheme.

The household then demonstrated why households are natural enemies of elegant schemes.

Before the maid arrived, Meriel's grandmother entered the kitchen.

Grandmother Daven discovered a tray of fresh biscuits cooling beside the window.

Grandmother Daven was due at her afternoon sewing circle.

Grandmother Daven took the biscuits.

All of them.

Meriel returned to find an empty cooling rack.

There followed several moments of silence in which she is traditionally said to have achieved a complete theological understanding of damnation.

Then she ran.

She crossed the kitchen garden.

She crossed the back lane.

She nearly knocked over a cooper.

She reached Grandmother Daven's cottage just as the sewing circle had settled around tea.

Seven respectable older women were present.

The biscuits were on the table.

Several had already been eaten.

Grandmother Daven held half of one in her hand.

Meriel burst through the door and shouted:

**"Grandmother, do not share those biscuits!"**

Every head turned.

Grandmother looked at the half biscuit.

"Why?"

Meriel stopped breathing.

The sewing circle waited.

Finally she whispered:

"They have Adraderis petals in them."

There was a very long silence.

Then Widow Pell took off her spectacles.

Mistress Arden looked at the biscuit in her hand.

Old Lady Corren slowly raised one eyebrow.

Grandmother Daven said:

"You put Adraderis in biscuits?"

Meriel nodded.

"For Charlie?"

Meriel became red enough that later illustrators traditionally color her face to match the hearth.

Grandmother looked at the remaining half biscuit.

Then at Meriel.

Then at the plate.

"You used too much."

At this, according to most editions, the entire sewing circle sighed.

Not in horror.

In recognition.

Widow Pell reportedly said:

"Girl, you should have said something."

Mistress Arden added:

"Three pinches?"

Meriel admitted it might have been four.

At which point Lady Corren, who had been unmarried for forty-eight years and deeply resented being called an old maid by anyone except herself, calmly reached for another biscuit.

Meriel slapped her hand away.

"Absolutely not."

Lady Corren replied:

"My dear, I know exactly what Adraderis does."

That was the beginning of the disaster.

## The evening at Bellweather Green

By sunset, every woman at the sewing circle had gone home.

This should have ended the matter.

Instead it merely distributed it geographically.

Bellweather Green was a farming village of fewer than eight hundred people.

Everyone knew everyone.

Everyone knew who had attended the sewing circle.

And, by the second hour after supper, nearly everyone knew that something unusual was occurring in at least six households.

The husbands of the married women reacted with varying degrees of enthusiasm, alarm, suspicion, exhaustion, and strategic retreat.

One reportedly left through his own kitchen window.

Another went to check the sheep and did not return until morning.

A third was seen walking rapidly toward the hills while still carrying one boot.

Widow Pell sent a servant to ask whether the cooper was still unmarried.

Mistress Arden's husband took refuge in the hayloft.

Grandmother Daven's husband, according to the scandalous edition, simply locked the front gate and announced that he had survived forty-three years of marriage and was not about to embarrass himself by running now.

Lady Corren disappeared from the village for two days.

She refused ever to explain where she had gone.

By midnight, Bellweather Green had effectively ceased to conduct ordinary civic life.

The night watch abandoned its usual route to deal with domestic arguments.

Two husbands slept in barns.

One climbed a pear tree.

A widower was seen leaving Widow Pell's house at an hour that ensured he would be discussed until Frostgate.

The village baker sold out of breakfast rolls before sunrise because half the adult population had apparently failed to sleep.

The magistrate cancelled the morning irrigation meeting.

The local priest announced that no theological position on Adraderis would be issued until after breakfast.

For approximately one night, Bellweather Green did not so much govern itself as endure itself.

Later editions dramatically claim that "the whole village disbanded."

This is not literally true.

The phrase became proverbial.

To **disband Bellweather** now means to cause such widespread romantic or domestic chaos that ordinary business becomes temporarily impossible.

## The morning after

The Adraderis effects began to diminish.

Embarrassment did not.

Meriel expected punishment.

Instead, her grandmother sat her down over blackseed coffee and explained several things.

First:

Adraderis was not obscure.

Second:

older women knew what it was.

Third:

older women had once been young.

Fourth:

secretly dosing one's betrothed was both foolish and legally questionable.

Fifth:

if Charlie required encouragement, Meriel might begin by actually speaking to Charlie.

Sixth:

if she ever again put four pinches of Adraderis into one tray of biscuits, Grandmother would personally send her to spend a month working under the apothecary until she learned measurements.

Charlie eventually heard the entire story.

He laughed so hard he had to sit down.

This nearly ended the engagement.

Not because he was angry.

Because Meriel threatened to kill him if he told anyone.

They married the following year.

The wedding biscuits contained no Adraderis.

Grandmother checked.

Twice.

## Cultural legacy

The story became a popular comic cautionary tale throughout the Realm.

It is commonly used to teach several contradictory lessons:

- do not secretly dose your betrothed;
- label magical ingredients;
- do not leave enchanted pastries unattended;
- never underestimate a grandmother;
- old maids generally know more than young lovers think they do;
- household logistics defeat romantic genius;
- effects expire faster than reputations;
- if six sewing-circle ladies all give you the same knowing look, you are probably not the first person to have had your idea.

Adraderis confection jokes are particularly common during Bloomtide.

Confectioners sometimes sell completely non-magical **Bellweather Biscuits** flavored with rose, ginger, and honey.

Respectable grandmothers buy them for sewing circles specifically to embarrass their grandchildren.

# II. Adraderis Petals — Cultural Note

Adraderis is an established adult aphrodisiac herb.

Its legitimate uses include consensual adult preparations associated with:

- marriage;
- honeymoon customs;
- fertility celebrations;
- private romantic dinners;
- physician-supervised treatment.

Its illicit uses include secret administration intended to alter another person's arousal or inhibition.

Adraderis is culturally famous enough that experienced adults recognize its name immediately.

This matters to the setting.

Young adults may believe they have discovered a daring romantic secret.

Older adults may instead recognize the ingredient, dosage mistake, intended purpose, and likely consequences before the explanation is halfway finished.

The resulting tone can range from criminal investigation to intergenerational farce depending on consent, intent, harm, and circumstance.

# III. Story-Runner Use

This tale may be:

- quoted;
- referenced by title;
- assigned as comic moral reading;
- mentioned by servants or grandmothers;
- sold in cheap illustrated editions;
- performed on stage;
- invoked as a proverb;
- used to embarrass a young courtier;
- referenced when magical food contamination occurs.

Characters may say:

"Do not Bellweather the kitchen."

"Label the Adraderis."

"Four pinches? Are you trying to empty the village?"

"Grandmother has seen this before."

The story should reinforce a recurring cultural truth:

**young lovers frequently mistake inherited romantic knowledge for their own invention.**
