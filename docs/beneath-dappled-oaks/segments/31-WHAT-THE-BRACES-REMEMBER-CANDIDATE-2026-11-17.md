# What the Braces Remember

The carpenter's apprentice had spent the morning pulling splinters out of his palms. The bridge had been made safe enough for the traffic its keepers presently allowed, but the work had left an astonishing amount of timber lying in the grass: trimmed wedges, split pegs, and one handsome offcut too short to brace anything.

He put the useful pieces in a sack. The rest would dry for kindling. Even that required asking whose wood it was.

His master stood beneath the repaired span, writing against the back of a board. On the bank above, two wagons waited for one another to clear the narrow crossing. Every time a wheel struck the join in the decking, the apprentice wanted to look up.

"Has the district paid you?" he asked.

"The men have been paid."

"That was not my question."

"No," the carpenter said. "It wasn't."

The apprentice watched the man's thumb flatten a page that the river wind kept trying to turn. He knew the crew had received their wages because he had carried the envelopes. He also knew where some of that money had come from. His master's household purse had been lighter after the last pay morning.

"Then write that you paid us twice. Perhaps they'll send twice the money."

His master looked at him over the board.

"I am joking," the apprentice said.

"You're tired enough to believe half your jokes. Bring me the adze."

The adze lay by the tool chest, its haft worn smooth beneath the head. Beside it was a smaller chisel borrowed from a smith down the road. The apprentice had sharpened it badly. Its edge showed a crescent of blue where he had let it heat too far.

He turned it over to hide the mark.

"Not that one," the carpenter said, without looking. "That's Brin's."

"Brin will want it back."

"With its edge as he lent it. We can ask him to grind it properly. Put the work against my tools, not the bridge."

"There's already a line for repairs."

"For the brace. For the men who fitted it. Not for every foolish thing that happened near it."

The apprentice set the adze on the board. It was tempting to call everything one job, especially when one account appeared to have money and another had none. He had listened to people argue over the stone warrant, the timber, the intermediary's advance. None of them had asked whether a boy could tell the truth about ruining an edge.

"I did it," he said.

"Yes."

"I'll tell Brin."

"Good. I'll stand beside you."

They carried the small chisel uphill together. His master took the tally board, but left the unsettled reimbursement column exactly as it was. Nothing on that page yet told them where the money had finally come from.

At the smith's door the apprentice held out the chisel with its damaged edge facing up. That, at least, was a repair he could name.
