# The Hour Nobody Could Assign

By the time the last visitors left the Covenant's city garden, the lamps had been lit under the covered walk. Mireth closed the gate herself. She had spent the afternoon helping people who wanted directions, water, an answer from the infirmary, or simply someone to hear why their journey had been difficult. None of those things had been foolish to ask.

Caelen stood beside the gate with his practice coat folded over one arm. He had finished his own duties and come without an escort, which she appreciated more than she intended to show.

“You've been here long?”

“Long enough to learn that a woman can ask the same question in six different ways and still deserve an answer.”

“Did you give her one?”

“I gave her the one I had.”

He held out his hand. She took it, but did not immediately turn toward the street. They could hear someone washing cups beyond the wall, and farther away the evening traffic of Aurelith.

“I told Elen I'd come back tomorrow if she needs help,” Mireth said. “Then I told two other people much the same thing. I think I've lent out tomorrow before seeing whether it belongs to me.”

“Mine was lent out at birth,” Caelen said.

She looked at him.

“That was an attempt at wit,” he admitted. “I can still refuse a request. I only dislike discovering how often I say yes because somebody assumes I will.”

“And when do you say yes because you want to?”

“Now.” He moved closer. “I want an hour with you in which neither of us is useful to anybody.”

She laughed quietly, then considered the seriousness beneath it.

“What if the infirmary calls?”

“Then you go if you're needed. What if my watch changes?”

“Then you tell me. Neither of us is a deserter for having wanted an evening.”

He watched her face. “And if nothing calls?”

“Then perhaps we stop inventing the interruption ourselves.”

Mireth led him to the far end of the walk, where the wall hid them from the gate without shutting out the smell of the garden. It was not much privacy. No servant had been ordered away; no family had granted permission. He set his folded coat on the stone ledge and sat beside her. She put her hand into his again.

After a while she rested her head against his shoulder.

“I don't want my work to be the reason you wait for me,” she said.

“I'd rather wait for you than for a convenient version of you.”

“And I don't want you to apologize for loving yours.”

“I may complain about the drill.”

“You already do.”

He kissed her hair. The lamps burned down by a finger's breadth. Neither counted the minutes aloud.

When Mireth finally rose, the gate remained closed and the infirmary lantern was steady. They had taken an ordinary hour, not secured a promise about every one that would follow.
