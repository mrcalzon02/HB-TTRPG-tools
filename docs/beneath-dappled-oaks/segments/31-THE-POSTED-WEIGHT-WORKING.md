# Chapter Thirty-One — The Posted Weight

The warning board was still blank when Thessa returned to the northern verge.

Someone had carried it out from the road keeper's store during the night, a length of seasoned oak with two holes bored near the top. It leaned against a hurdle beside the repairers' fire. The paint pot stood open at its feet. A laborer had placed a stone on the lid to keep the wind from taking it.

Captain Sorn looked at the board, then at the road.

"That seems to be the difficulty," she said.

The stream under the bank had grown louder in the night. Not much water was visible, but the sound came from beneath the cart track rather than beside it. The repair crew had cut a shallow drain through the grass. A man in a wool cap was working wet gravel into sacks to hold the drain open, his sleeves rolled to the elbows despite the cold.

Thessa had brought the certified quarry extract in her satchel. She had read it once at the coaching house and again before breakfast, when the ink was dry and there was no steward to answer her questions. Two loads had been booked against the district's road-stone warrant. The quarry had not entered a dispatch against that booking; the reservation had lapsed; the quarry had not invoiced those loads. There might have been another supplier, another purchase order, a later correction. None appeared on the paper she carried.

The repairers did not need her to explain the accounts. They needed stone.

"Where's the keeper?" Sorn asked.

"Coming from the lower road," said the foreman. "He sent word at first light. I've kept the loaded wagons off."

"Under whose authority?"

"Mine, for the moment. I can refuse to put my men under a wheel."

That was a practical answer, though not one that would satisfy the merchants gathering beyond the hurdles. A milk carrier had unloaded her full churns into smaller panniers and was leading a patient mule across the supervised inside line. A baker's boy waited with two covered baskets and an expression of deep resentment. On the far side, a wagoner stood beside a cart of flour, examining the road as though sufficient disappointment might make it firm.

Thessa dismounted and took a measuring rod from the foreman.

The ground gave under her boot where it had been sound yesterday. She stepped back. Sorn watched without comment while she tested the margin again from firmer ground.

"Another handspan," Thessa said.

"Mark it."

They drove a stake at the new edge. The foreman had brought a narrow strip of red cloth; Thessa tied it where a driver would see it before turning onto the weak ground. She could hear the baker's boy muttering to himself.

"You'll have to go around," she told him.

"I have gone around. That's why I'm here."

"The mule can take your baskets on the inside line, one trip at a time. No wheels."

"Who's paying the mule?"

The milk carrier looked up. "The boy who wants it to work, I should think."

"I haven't got a mule."

"You can hire mine when she's finished. Not for free."

The boy's face turned scarlet. He was old enough to carry a business's promise to its customers and young enough to feel personally accused when the road refused to cooperate. Thessa was about to offer to carry a basket herself when Sorn touched her elbow.

"You can help him lift it," Sorn said quietly. "You cannot promise him a free delivery."

So Thessa helped unload the baskets. The boy found two coins, bargained badly, and watched the mule take the first load across. It was a small transaction, irritating to everyone involved. It kept bread moving.

By midmorning the road keeper arrived with a clerk, a folding rule and a face made red by the wind. He had been called from a culvert repair farther south. His boots were clean above the ankles, which made the foreman look at them with a kind of professional hostility.

"You've made a restriction," he said to Sorn.

"We've made a warning," Sorn replied. "We've kept loaded wheels away from a bank we watched fail. Your office can make the restriction."

The keeper examined the fresh stake, then the old one, and said nothing for a while. He walked to the inside line, where the mule was returning without its panniers, and made the carrier wait while he tested the ground. She gave him a look that would have been dangerous had he been a cart of grain.

He put his rule against the new break. The foreman showed him the relief channel. Thessa gave the measurements she had taken the day before, then the new measurement. The keeper asked her to repeat the second. She did. He knelt to look under the bank, but did not put his weight over the hollow.

"Where's the proposed repair?"

The foreman took him to the pile of coarse fill. The keeper lifted a handful and let it fall.

"That won't hold a loaded axle."

"I told your clerk that yesterday."

"My clerk told me you wanted dressed stone."

"I do."

"And a cart to bring it."

"That would be a pleasant change."

The keeper looked toward the waiting wagons. A broad-shouldered merchant had left his team at the bend and come down on foot.

"Will you close it or not?" the man demanded.

"To loaded wheeled traffic," the keeper said. "For now."

"And the rest?"

"Pack animals on the inside line while it remains sound. Pedestrians under supervision. No carts of any weight until the repair is inspected."

"That's not a road."

"No," the keeper said. "At present it's a bank with ambitions."

The merchant stared at him. Then he asked where the formal notice would be posted. It was a fair question, and the keeper's expression changed when he realized the man was not merely shouting.

"At both approaches. The clerk will carry a copy to the market office before noon."

"Will it say the restriction began yesterday?"

"It will say when the Wardens warned traffic, and when my order takes effect. Those are different times."

"Good. My carrier was turned back yesterday. I don't intend to be accused of inventing a delay."

The keeper nodded. "Give the clerk the name and the cart number."

The merchant went to do so. Thessa watched him pass the baker's boy, who was now arranging his empty baskets and trying not to look pleased that a man with a fine coat had been delayed too.

The clerk took the blank board onto the grass. He painted the words slowly, leaving room for the keeper's seal and the date. The foreman supplied a second board from the repair shed. A driver offered a hammer. Two people who had been complaining about the delay helped raise the first notice where the road divided.

The restriction would cost them money. They still wanted it to be legible.

Sorn asked the keeper about materials.

"The works office must authorize purchase," he said. "I can certify the hazard and the quantity required. I can't spend the district's replacement-stone warrant merely because someone wrote those words on an old form."

"The quarry has two loads available," Thessa said, then caught herself. "It had two loads reserved. The reservation lapsed. I don't know whether the same stock remains."

"Who told you?"

"The quarry steward. I have his certified extract. The auditor requested it."

The keeper held out his hand. She gave him the measurements instead.

"The extract is addressed to the auditor," she said. "I'll ask her to send you a copy if the works office needs it. I can give you the quarry's ordinary contact details."

For a moment she disliked the care required to make that distinction. The road was failing within sight of them. The quarry had cut stone. Somewhere between the two lay the authority to buy, load and carry it.

The keeper did not seem offended.

"Give my clerk the contact. I'll send the quantity certification today. If the works office finds an existing valid order, they can use it. Otherwise they'll need a new one."

"And the people going around?" Thessa asked.

"They can submit the documented extra cost through the ordinary road-loss process."

"Will they be paid?"

"Some may. I won't promise a result before anyone has examined a claim."

Thessa thought of the flour wagon, the milk carrier, the baker's boy and the merchant whose delivery hour had already passed. The keeper was not refusing to see them. He was refusing to spend money he had not been granted.

She had made the same distinction yesterday and hated it more when it came from another mouth.

The foreman called them back to the drain. Water had begun to overtop one of the sacks. Sorn and Thessa helped the men move it to firmer ground. The keeper removed his clean coat, rolled up his sleeves and joined them. By the time the water ran clear of the track again, his boots were indistinguishable from the foreman's.

At noon the board stood upright. The keeper had signed a temporary restriction, the foreman had a written measurement for the stone he needed, and the market office was expecting a copy. There was still no purchase order and no cart of dressed stone.

Thessa sat on the low wall beside the repairers' fire and ate a heel of bread with a piece of hard cheese. She had intended to ride back early enough to finish her other patrol notes. Sorn had not reminded her of them. That was worse than a lecture.

"You were right about the restriction," Thessa said.

"Which part?"

"That we couldn't make the keeper's order for him."

Sorn took a bite of her own bread. "You could have frightened half the district into obeying you for a day. Your name would have helped."

"And tomorrow?"

"Tomorrow they would have asked which office owed them money."

Thessa looked at the board. A farmer was reading it aloud to a companion who could not make out the small writing. They argued about the word *loaded* until the clerk came over and explained that the order covered all wheeled carts, not merely the heaviest ones.

"That's what the notice says now," Sorn observed.

The clerk returned to his paint pot and made the correction in larger letters.

The post rider arrived while they were saddling the horses. Thessa handed over the personal letter she had written at the coaching house, addressed east to Ilyan. She had not enclosed the quarry extract or the road keeper's order. She had written about stone dust, an unflattering steward and how much she missed having someone nearby who would argue with her for pleasure.

The rider tucked it beneath the oilcloth with other people's news. He promised nothing about when it would arrive.

Thessa went back to the keeper and signed the Warden witness line on the temporary notice. Her signature would not repair the road. It would make clear which officer had seen the bank fail and warned the first drivers away.

Sorn was already mounted.

"Now," she said, "we have a southern boundary to inspect."

Thessa looked once more at the boards, the men shoveling wet gravel and the line of carts turning toward the toll road. Then she swung into the saddle.

She could not bring the stone by wishing for it. She could make certain the next report was not left on the wrong desk.
