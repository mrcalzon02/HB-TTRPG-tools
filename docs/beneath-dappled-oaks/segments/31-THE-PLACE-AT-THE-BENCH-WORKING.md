# The Place at the Bench

The foreman laid his narrow loading book beside the wage ledger. Its pages opened at the repair notation in the master's own hand, written two days before the accident.

“You knew about the rail,” the apprentice said.

“I knew it needed tightening.”

“Why wasn't it done?”

“We had the river order. The upper rack was holding.”

“I should have kept the new load off it,” the foreman said.

The master looked at him. “You were at the river job when it fell.”

“I helped load it before.”

“You wrote down what we did.”

“I did. Now I wish I'd made us move the staves.”

No one asked him to claim he had witnessed the fall. The book established the loading and repair notation. The young man had described being struck by the bundle. What caused it to fall still required examination.

Ilyan watched the master's thick hands, one joint swollen from an old break. Those hands had taught the apprentice a trade and withheld his wages. Ilyan had expected to dislike him. It was inconvenient to find him tired and frightened about keeping the shop solvent, because the apprentice's need for lodging did not become smaller on that account.

The assessor returned to the first page.

“Five days completed before the accident. Their payment date has passed. Can you pay them today?”

The master looked at the purse.

“Yes.”

“Without asking him to abandon the injury claim?”

“Yes.”

The apprentice sat very still. His uncle began to speak, but the young man raised his good hand.

“Will I still be on the books?”

“Your agreement hasn't been cancelled,” the master said. “Come back after the fortnight with the physician's advice. We'll see what you can do.”

“That isn't a promise of my bench.”

“No.”

The apprentice stared at him, then nodded. Ilyan was grateful the master had not offered a promise he might withdraw the next morning.

Neral counted the coins while the assessor watched. The apprentice checked the count himself, slowly sliding each piece into a row with his good hand. He signed the acknowledgment awkwardly against the table's edge. It covered the five completed days only. The disputed stock deduction, medical certificate, repair entry and question of responsibility remained for the guild.

The master put away his lighter purse.

“I'm not pleased about this.”

“I know,” the apprentice replied.

“I've kept that shop forty years.”

“I know that too.”

His uncle helped him rise. The foreman held the door. Outside, the greens seller was drawing a tarred cloth over her baskets as the rain began again.

The foreman stayed on the threshold until the boy had gone. He looked as though he wanted to say something to his master and could not decide which version would be least damaging.

“Go back to the shop,” the master told him.

The foreman went. The assessor gathered the papers for copying. The master's ledger would return with him; the Hall would keep the certified entries and the guild its own hearing record. Ilyan watched Neral separate the sheets into their proper piles, the small work by which none of the promises made that morning would depend entirely on memory.
