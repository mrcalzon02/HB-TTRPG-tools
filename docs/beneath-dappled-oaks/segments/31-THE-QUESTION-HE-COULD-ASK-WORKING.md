# The Question He Could Ask

Vaun was hearing the end of a millrace dispute when Ilyan returned. One party insisted the sluice had been raised too early. The other insisted the river had risen on its own, which seemed a persuasive defense until Vaun asked why the gatekeeper's wage sheet showed an extra night watch.

Neral waved Ilyan into the smaller workroom. They set the transfer certificate beside Salver's appointment order.

“Well,” Neral said.

“Is that good?”

“It's useful.”

“It makes the later route less likely.”

“It makes ordinary access at that desk less likely. He could have requested the volume.”

“Yes.”

“Or not.”

“Yes.”

Neral checked the certification again. “We still don't know whether the volume contains the acknowledgment.”

“No.”

“Then write that.”

Ilyan sat down. He had imagined Salver, older and perhaps weary of his own decisions, taking a register from the shelf and recognizing a case he had encountered years before. The picture had felt persuasive. Now the room itself was wrong. The old volume had been recorded in historical custody before Salver's transfer.

He did not know why he felt so foolish. A more convenient theory had failed. No one had died because of that.

The people who had died were why it mattered.

By the time Vaun entered, the millrace parties had agreed to appoint an engineer neither could afford to bribe. She read the two dates without speaking.

“This doesn't undo the earlier records,” she said.

“No. He still logged the lawful timing material and the later memorandum.”

“What would you ask next?”

Ilyan had rehearsed a demand for every requisition Salver had ever made. On paper, it looked like a fishing net dragged through other people's sealed business.

“Whether this transferred volume was requisitioned during his two weeks on the returns desk,” he said. “Which office requested it, if that can be certified. Nothing about the protected matter.”

“And if the schedule cannot be separated without disclosing restricted consultations?”

“Then they say so.”

She gave him a clean sheet.

“Draft it.”

Neral made another entry in the Hall's expenses. Beneath the depot fee was a note about the cooper's nephew: a physician's report requested, a witness statement still missing. The clerk had dealt with it while Ilyan sat among old records.

“Did they find the foreman?” Ilyan asked.

“Not yet. The boy will walk.”

“Can I help?”

“Finish this. Take the witness docket tomorrow.”

Vaun struck two phrases from Ilyan's draft: one assumed a consultation had occurred, the other asked for a clerk's name before establishing whether any public record supported it. She signed the narrower inquiry for ordinary dispatch, without priority. Neral placed it in the outgoing tray. The courier had not yet collected it.

Ilyan remained by the window as the Hall emptied. From the kitchen came the smell of onions and bread warming near the hearth. He thought of Thessa on the western roads, pursuing her own stubborn inquiry into missing payments. He missed the certainty of her hands on his shoulders. He also knew she would be merciless if he wrote to announce that he had proved a man could not read a book merely because it was stored elsewhere.

He smiled, folded the certificate into its sleeve, and went to find the witness docket.
