# The Afternoon Packet

When the assessor had gone, Ilyan discovered he had been pressing his thumbnail into the soft wood beneath the table.

“You didn't say much,” Neral observed.

“I had several things to say.”

“I could see.”

“I thought the master would make the boy apologize for getting hurt before he gave him the money.”

“He might have wanted that. He also brought the money.”

“Not enough to settle the injury.”

“We weren't hearing that claim.”

Ilyan released the table. He was growing weary of learning the same lesson in different rooms. He was more weary of how often it proved necessary.

Vaun came in from the courtyard with her cloak wet across one shoulder. She asked whether the apprentice had been paid. Neral showed her the acknowledgment and the reserved items. She read them standing up.

“Good. The guild has the rest?”

“Once the copies go over.”

“Send them this afternoon.”

Ilyan had brought another sheet downstairs. He waited until she finished the wage record before placing it beside her.

“The circulation inquiry.”

She turned directly to the second paragraph, where he had removed Salver's name. The question was whether an administrative register for the earlier interference memorandum's circulation had existed, and which office held it if it survived. He had not asked for its entries or a recipient's name.

“Who prepared this?”

“I did. Neral checked the office designation.”

Vaun read the address again. “Why this office?”

“It's where the memorandum was handled. I kept looking at the later Secretariat because Salver worked there. The depot has answered the requisition question for his two weeks. I have no reason to ask that office to account for him again.”

“Nor to clear him.”

“No.”

She struck a phrase that assumed the register survived. Ilyan rewrote it to ask whether any surviving register or transfer schedule could identify the custodian. Vaun signed the request.

“Ordinary dispatch. Keep its fee apart from the guild matter.”

Neral nodded. He had already separated the accounts.

The porter took the packet with the guild copies and the afternoon mail. Neral watched him mark the outgoing book before returning to his desk. The request had left the Hall; no one knew whether an office could answer it.

Ilyan went to the arcade for air. The greens seller was packing up. Near the gate, the apprentice and his uncle were still arguing quietly over what he ought to do with his wages.

“I can pay my own lodging,” the young man said.

“Keep some for food.”

“I will.”

“Your mother will want to know.”

“Then I'll tell her.”

The apprentice caught Ilyan watching and lifted the little purse in acknowledgment, not gratitude exactly. Ilyan raised a hand and let them go.

Thessa would have liked the way the young man had insisted on speaking for himself. She would also have disliked the uncertainty about his place at the bench. She was far west, occupied with her own work, and the letter he had sent had not had time to reach her.

He went back inside. The chairs needed carrying upstairs before the next petitioners arrived. Neral had taken two already. Ilyan took the other two without being asked.

The apprentice had his five days' pay. His arm was still injured. The master would have to decide what to do when the fortnight ended. The circulation request was on its way to a records office. For the rest of the afternoon, the Hall had other people waiting.
