# Chapter Five continuation — The Seal Belonged to a Dead Man

The Academy line remained on the slate until Vaun covered it with one hand.

"Copy first."

Damaris looked up.

"I know."

"Good."

The clerk bent over his certification sheet.

Rhyl's fingers were still resting lightly on Damaris's shoulder. She could have moved. She did not.

Across the room, Meris had gone very quiet.

That was more worrying than anger.

Damaris looked at the six other entries without opening any of them. Two council offices. A physician's cooperative. A tribunal annex. A townhouse she recognized by district but not by owner. Reedglass. The Academy.

Seven deliveries in nine days.

She refused to make the number meaningful merely because it wanted to be.

"Sponsor seal," Vaun said.

Damaris returned to the credential page.

The audit mark opened into a short administrative note.

The temporary runner had been sponsored through the Lower Reed Transfer Office. The seal number belonged to a freight assessor named Joren Tal.

Damaris read the next line.

"That's why they suspended it."

Rhyl leaned closer.

"What?"

"The same seal number already exists on older records."

"Copied?"

"Maybe."

Vaun's gaze moved to her.

Damaris corrected herself.

"Duplicated. We don't know how."

She opened the assessor record.

Joren Tal had worked bonded freight for thirty-two years.

He had died eighteen months ago.

Nobody spoke for a moment.

Then Rhyl said, "That seems inconvenient for him."

Meris closed her eyes.

Damaris looked over her shoulder.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"You made a face."

"I am trying not to encourage him."

Rhyl looked wounded.

"I am standing right here."

"Yes."

Damaris smiled despite the slate in front of her.

Vaun let them have exactly one breath of it.

"Who could use the seal after Tal died?"

Damaris searched the office record.

"His physical stamp should have been surrendered when his commission closed."

"Should."

"There is a destruction receipt."

"Signed by?"

Damaris opened it.

The name meant nothing to her.

"Sera Pell. Senior stores clerk."

Meris frowned.

"I know the office. Not the clerk."

"Current?" Vaun asked.

Damaris checked.

"Transferred to South Quay last winter."

"Living?"

Rhyl glanced at her.

Damaris gave him a warning look.

"Apparently."

"Good start."

Vaun pointed to the destruction receipt.

"Certified copy. Then we speak to her before anyone decides a dead assessor forged a runner credential."

The clerk began writing again.

Damaris looked at the Academy entry.

Four days before Silver Night.

She wanted to open it.

She wanted the receiving name, the parcel class, the internal route, every hand it had passed through.

Wanting was not the same as being ready to touch it.

"Judge."

Vaun followed her gaze.

"Not yet."

Damaris's irritation rose.

"Why?"

"Because you have one credential whose sponsorship is defective and one delivery to a place where a catastrophe happened four days later. If you begin pulling Academy records before preserving the other six deliveries, anyone watching the system learns exactly which line frightened you."

Meris looked at Vaun with renewed interest.

Damaris hated that her aunt approved.

She hated more that Vaun was right.

"So we preserve all seven first."

"Yes."

"Quietly."

"Yes."

Rhyl's thumb moved once against the seam of Damaris's shoulder.

Not comfort exactly.

A reminder that impatience was not action.

She leaned back into the touch for half a second.

Then the front bell rang.

Talia appeared in the doorway a minute later with a tribunal courier behind her.

The courier carried two sealed envelopes.

Mireth came in behind him, damp at the hem and visibly annoyed that the laboratory had managed to produce results without waiting for her personal supervision.

"Powder and tincture," she said.

Aereth had been sitting in the adjoining morning room with a book open on his knee. He had not turned a page in twenty minutes.

He came to the door.

"Which first?"

Vaun looked at him.

"Yours."

Aereth laughed once without humor.

"Kind."

The tincture report was short.

Mireth read it beside Vaun.

"The bottle contains the prescribed sleep mixture at ordinary concentration. No unexpected sedative. No detected mnemonic compound."

Aereth stared at her.

"So the medicine is not doing this."

Mireth folded the report once.

"The medicine does not explain what we've seen."

It was a small distinction.

Aereth heard it anyway.

"Right."

Damaris went to him.

He did not look at her.

"I wanted it to be the bottle."

"I know."

"That would have been simple."

"Yes."

He finally looked at her.

"You could disagree occasionally."

"You are wearing a terrible waistcoat."

Aereth looked down at himself.

It was dark green and perfectly respectable.

"Liar."

"Apparently I can still manage it."

His mouth twitched.

Damaris took the victory and did not improve it with explanation.

Vaun opened the second report.

Mireth read over her shoulder.

This time her expression changed.

Damaris felt Aereth notice.

"What?"

Mireth looked at Vaun before answering.

The judge handed her the page.

"You know the material better."

Mireth read it twice.

"The grey powder contains refined mnemonic salt."

Aereth went still.

Damaris's stomach tightened.

The west tower came back all at once: two sealed cases, custody holds, the careful handwriting of people who had moved dangerous material as part of ordinary work.

Mireth continued.

"There is also a stabilizing ash compound. It keeps the salt from degrading quickly in damp conditions."

Rhyl looked toward the rain-streaked window.

"Buried soil."

"It would help there," Mireth said.

Aereth's face had lost color.

"Can it make someone remember things that did not happen?"

Mireth did not answer immediately.

"Mnemonic salt is used in memory work. What this preparation was meant to do, whether it was active, whether it affected you, and how it would have been applied are separate questions."

Aereth looked away.

"So you don't know."

"No."

He nodded.

Damaris watched him absorb that.

The old Aereth—the one she remembered from family dinners and arguments about bridge tolls—would have demanded a cleaner answer.

This morning he only rubbed his thumb against the edge of his book.

Talia remained by the door.

Aereth noticed her.

His hand stopped.

"I am still not dismissing anyone today."

Talia's chin lifted slightly.

"Good."

"And you are still not assigned to my room."

"Also good."

He accepted that.

Damaris found herself unexpectedly proud of both of them.

Vaun put the two reports beside the certified credential copies.

"Now we have three separate problems. Someone assembled household material beneath Aereth's window with mnemonic salt. Someone used a temporary River Ledger credential sponsored under a duplicated seal. And that credential made seven deliveries, one to the Academy four days before Silver Night."

Meris said, "Separate until proven otherwise."

Vaun looked at her.

"Yes."

Damaris heard the effort in her aunt's voice.

Meris wanted the lines connected too.

Perhaps everyone in the room did.

That did not make the connection true.

The tribunal courier collected Vaun's receipt and left.

Rain softened against the windows.

For the first time since breakfast, nobody was moving.

Rhyl broke the stillness.

"Sera Pell."

Vaun nodded.

"You and I will speak to her."

Damaris looked up.

"And me?"

"You preserve the remaining route records with your clerk."

"Your clerk."

"Today he is everyone's clerk. He will recover emotionally."

The young man did not look up from his writing.

"Eventually."

Mireth laughed.

Damaris glanced at Rhyl.

He was going with Vaun.

She disliked that immediately.

Not because she doubted him.

Because she had become accustomed, with embarrassing speed, to having him within reach.

He saw it.

Of course he did.

"I'll come back."

"I know."

"That sounded convincing."

"I am practicing."

Meris turned toward the window, granting them privacy so deliberately that it became a form of teasing.

Damaris ignored her.

She caught the front of Rhyl's damp coat and kissed him once.

Not long.

Not discreet either.

When she released him, his hand remained at her waist.

"For luck?" he asked.

"No."

"Good. I don't trust luck."

"For incentive."

"To come back?"

"To avoid being stabbed by a retired stores clerk."

"That was already high on my list."

Vaun was waiting at the door.

"Orthell."

Rhyl stepped away reluctantly.

Damaris let him.

That mattered too.

After they left, she returned to the slate.

The Academy line waited where she had left it.

So did the other five.

Damaris opened a clean certification sheet.

"All seven," she told the clerk.

He dipped his pen.

Meris came to stand on the other side of the desk.

"And after?"

Damaris looked at the Academy entry.

"After, we find out what actually arrived."

Her aunt nodded.

Neither of them said Silver Night.

They did not need to.
