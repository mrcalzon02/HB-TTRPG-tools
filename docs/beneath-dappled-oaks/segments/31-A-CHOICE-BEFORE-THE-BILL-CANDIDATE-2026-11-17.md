# A Choice Before the Bill

The woman in the blue work dress had been waiting long enough to learn the order of the cracks in the plaster.

From the copying desk, the youngest of Halen’s clerks could see her petition folded beneath both hands. The mason had already asked twice whether a wall that fell outward counted differently from one that fell inward. Behind him, two apprentices shifted a crate of seal wax from one tired arm to the other.

At the far table, a judge and her companions studied the public notice rules. Their questions were quiet, but each required a book to be fetched, a heading checked, a date copied properly. Important people were no faster to serve than frightened ones.

The copyist had finished transcribing a general notice form. He sanded the ink, counted the lines, and brought it to Halen.

“Another copy,” she said, glancing at the waiting woman.

“That would put her behind the judge.”

“The judge is reading. She hasn't asked you to copy anything.”

He looked down at the form. “I thought she might.”

Halen held out her hand. He gave her the page.

“Then charge the fee for work that exists, not work you imagine,” she said. “And do not put a protected reference on a public copy because someone looks likely to ask you about it.”

He took the lesson and the page back to his desk.

The woman in blue stepped forward. She wanted the general procedure for recording a disagreement over tools that both former partners claimed. She did not ask the registry to decide who owned them. She wanted to know which notice she was entitled to request, and whether obtaining the public instructions would cost her another day's walk across the city.

He showed her the public book's headings. The public copy carried an ordinary copying fee; review expenses, where applicable, belonged to the relevant procedure and parties, not to every person who asked to read a rule. He could explain the distinction. He could not promise what any reviewer would ultimately charge or decide.

“Can I read it here first?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“Without paying for a copy?”

“Yes.”

Her shoulders dropped, though only a little. She took the book to the end of the bench, where the light was tolerable.

He returned to the line of forms. Across the room the judge's party was still reading. The mason had begun drawing his fallen wall in the margin of a scrap, and the apprentices had finally put their crate down.

There would be more fees to enter by closing time. He left the woman's name off the copying account until she decided whether she needed a copy.

For one person at least, the record could begin with a choice rather than a bill.
