# The Line the Road Could Bear

The rain had stopped, but the water beneath the north road had not.

Thessa stood on the inner track while Captain Sorn lowered a marked staff into the drainage cut. The staff touched firm ground below the gravel lip. When Sorn lifted it, a thread of brown water came away on the wood. From above, the narrow shelf of road looked almost dry.

“Yesterday the wheel stood here,” Sorn said, pointing to the old rut. “Today there is air beneath it.”

“Can you measure the hollow?” Thessa asked.

“From this side. Not its whole length.”

Two road-watch laborers had brought a coil of warning cord and replacement stakes. One, an older woman with muddy cuffs, examined the gap between the nearest post and the grass beyond it.

“If you want that line carried straight across,” she said, “someone must stand on the loose side.”

“I don't,” Thessa said.

“The last patrol told us to keep the posts upright.”

“They were right. But not at the price of putting you under the road.”

The younger laborer had already gathered the cord. He glanced at his companion before setting it down. Thessa recognized the glance: a worker asking permission to refuse without being called unwilling.

Sorn measured the distance between two points they could reach on sound ground. Thessa entered both endpoints, the length of staff lowered, and the places they had not probed. A farmer passing on foot said a wagon had used the verge three days earlier. She noted his account under his name, separate from their measurements. Three days earlier was not a survey of the earth today.

They set the replacement stakes farther back, where the inner track held their weight, and carried the warning cord around the dangerous lip rather than over it. The heavy-load restriction remained. Light travelers could still be guided along the inner passage, one at a time, as the existing notice allowed; no laborer was to step onto the exposed shelf to tend the line.

“Will the road office count this as repair work?” the older woman asked.

“No,” Thessa said. “It is a warning made safer. I will record your time and what you did. I cannot approve the office's pay sheet.”

The woman nodded, not quite satisfied. Satisfaction would have required a different officer and a different book.

Sorn glanced toward Thessa's saddlebag, where the unreturned questions about the stone warrant lay folded.

“Shall I mention the missing stone?”

“In the accounts request. Not beside this measurement.”

They could establish that the hollow had worsened, and that a cord could be moved without risking a person to move it. They could not establish what had caused the damage, where the stone had gone, or when anyone would come to mend the road.

Thessa signed the field sheet while the water went on carrying earth out of sight.
