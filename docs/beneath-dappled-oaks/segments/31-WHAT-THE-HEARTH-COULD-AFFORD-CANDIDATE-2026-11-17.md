# What the Hearth Could Afford

The bridge wages had arrived. That was the first thing his wife insisted they agree upon.

She spread the coins on the kitchen table while the last of the morning fire sank behind the grate. Her husband had worked under the carpenter who repaired the bridge. The carpenter had paid his men from his own purse when the district money came late. Whatever the offices now argued over, these coins were payment for work already done.

“He says there may be another job upriver,” her husband said.

“May be?”

“He'll ask when he knows.”

She pushed the coins back into their little cloth bag. “Then we won't spend the job that hasn't happened.”

On the shelf stood a flour crock light enough to lift with one hand. Beside the hearth was a neat stack of wood that had looked substantial when they'd carried it indoors. Much of it was still damp. The landlord's rent was approaching, and the folded household book lay beneath a mug to keep its cover flat.

Her husband opened it at the last paid week.

“Put the bridge money there.”

“I have.”

“And what the district still owes the carpenter?”

“That is his account, not ours.”

He looked at her, then nodded.

She set aside the rent money first. The remainder was less comfortable to look at.

“Flour?” he asked.

“Enough for bread, if the mill has it to sell.”

“And wood?”

“We've enough dry pieces for one proper fire. I can stretch the rest if the rain stops.”

He rubbed his hands together. The knuckles were cracked where wet timber and cold iron had worn them.

“I could take the scraps from the bridge.”

“Do they belong to you?”

He frowned. “No.”

“Then ask before you carry anything home. A repair isn't a free woodpile.”

He smiled, tired and grudging. “You sound like the district clerk.”

“I have a better kitchen.”

She found yesterday's bread beneath its cloth, cut away the hardened edge and set out beans for midday. She had hoped to bake a fresh loaf for supper, but left the plan unwritten until she knew what flour could be bought.

Outside, a cart creaked toward the mill. She could not see what it carried. The northern road warning did not tell her what sat in the miller's bins this morning.

“I'll ask for a small measure,” she said. “And I'll pay for what he weighs, not for what he says may arrive.”

Her husband tied the rent coins separately and placed them in the cupboard. He did not call the household safe. She did not call it ruined.

For now, there would be beans at midday, an inquiry at the mill, and one good fire if the evening turned cold.

She put the cloth back over the bread. Neither of them needed a promise to eat it.
