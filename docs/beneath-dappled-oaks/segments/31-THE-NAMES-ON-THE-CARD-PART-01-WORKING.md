# The Names on the Card — The Proposed Places

The printer had left space for seven people who had not been asked whether they wished to come.

Lyrion discovered it on a proof sheet smelling of hot ink and damp linen. The places occupied the centre of the reception plan, immediately below the visiting delegation and immediately above the musicians. Someone had written *surviving scholars* in an elegant hand, with seven small marks for chairs.

"Who put us there?" he asked.

Mistress Syle glanced at the sheet. "The reception office."

"Who told them they could?"

"Nobody. It is a proposed arrangement."

"With chairs."

"Most arrangements have chairs."

He laid the sheet beside her inkwell. The room was crowded with the ordinary wreckage of diplomacy: wax scraped from old seals, lengths of ribbon, invitation cards, a bowl of pears no one had found time to eat. Through the open window came the bright noise of Bloomtide carts and a woman advertising fresh watercress. In the courtyard, a boy was attempting to persuade a very large dog to release a very small glove.

"Thessa is in the west," he said. "Rhyl is on patrol. Caelen has duties. Damaris has accounts. Mireth has the Covenant. Ilyan—"

"Has a life," Alenor Syle supplied.

"They all do."

"I am aware."

He looked again at the chairs. He had been invited to receptions often enough to understand the difference between a seat offered in welcome and a seat reserved to make a room look a certain way. Seven survivors, arranged beneath garlands and observed by people who would have the luxury of going home afterward, made a handsome tableau.

"Did the visitors ask for this?"

"They asked whether the surviving class might attend. The reception office supplied the drawing. No invitation has been sent."

"Then why has the printer got it?"

"Because the reception office believes all problems can be solved by getting the cards printed before anyone objects."

She moved the proof away from the ink.

Lyrion had come to the Silver Bough expecting to learn the graceful arts of persuasion. So far he had learned that a misplaced comma could delay a delegation, that servants knew the real length of a banquet, and that everyone wanted a reply before they had finished asking their question.

"May I strike the chairs?"

"You may propose an alteration. Ambassador Ilvaren will approve the programme."

He reached for a pen.

"Read the rest first," she said.
