# Chapter Five continuation — Pellin Doss and the East Room

Rhyl found Pellin Doss three streets beyond the Lower Reed market, in a room above a cooper's shop where the rain came through one corner of the window and somebody had put a soup bowl underneath it.

He knew he had the right room because the young man who opened the door wore a Reedglass laundry runner's coat over his shirt and had a folded towel across one shoulder.

He also looked exhausted.

"Pellin Doss?"

The man's hand tightened on the door.

"Who's asking?"

Rhyl showed the Red Hart badge pinned inside his coat rather than pushing it toward his face.

"Rhyl Orthell-Faen. Reedglass House is trying to account for who handled the east rooms while Lord Aereth has been there. Judge Vaun asked me to speak with you."

Pellin's eyes moved past him toward the stairs.

"Am I under arrest?"

"No."

"Did she say I had to come back?"

"No."

That changed something.

Not much.

Enough that Pellin stopped trying to decide whether he could slam the door before Rhyl got a boot into it.

From inside the room a woman coughed hard enough to interrupt both of them.

Pellin looked back immediately.

Rhyl waited.

The room smelled of boiled onion, wet wool and the sharp medicinal sweetness of feverleaf. A young woman lay beneath two blankets on the narrow bed. She was awake, flushed, and glaring at Rhyl with the concentrated hostility of someone whose illness had failed to make her polite.

"If you're taking him," she said, "you can wash the cups first."

Pellin closed his eyes.

"Lessa."

"What? He has hands."

Rhyl looked at the cups.

"I do."

That surprised a laugh out of her, which turned into another cough.

Pellin stepped aside.

"Five minutes."

"As long as you need to answer what you remember."

"That sounds longer than five minutes."

"Usually is."

Pellin let him in.

Rhyl did not take the only chair until Pellin pointed at it. The young man stayed beside his sister's bed, one hand resting on the blanket near her ankle.

"Talia said you carried the east-floor basket the day Lord Aereth arrived."

"I did."

"Anything unusual?"

Pellin frowned.

"Unusual how?"

"Start with whatever made you hesitate just now."

That earned him a suspicious look.

Then Pellin sighed.

"There was blood on the shaving cloth."

Rhyl said nothing.

Pellin continued.

"Not much. He'd nicked himself. Talia told me to send it down separately because blood stains set if the wash is wrong. I wrapped it inside an old towel and put it in the marked tray."

"Who handled the tray after you?"

"Could have been me. Could have been Senn. Could have been one of the wash women. I don't remember."

"Did the cloth come back?"

"I don't know."

Rhyl nodded.

Pellin looked relieved that he had not been asked to improve the answer.

"Anything else from the room?"

"Two coat buttons."

"Broken?"

"One cracked, one pulled loose. His travelling coat. I took it to mending."

"Black waxed thread?"

Pellin's expression sharpened.

"Yes."

"Common?"

"At Reedglass? Very."

"Who can get it?"

"Anyone in mending. Anyone who asks mending. Anyone who takes a spool and forgets to bring it back."

Lessa spoke from the bed.

"So everyone."

Pellin looked at her.

"Not literally everyone."

"Everyone with trousers."

"Please be ill quietly."

"No."

Rhyl hid a smile.

"Why did you leave yesterday?"

Pellin's face closed.

Lessa answered for him.

"Because I started coughing blood into a rag and he panicked."

Pellin turned. "It was not blood."

"You didn't know that."

"The healer said it wasn't."

"After you panicked."

Pellin rubbed both hands over his face.

Rhyl looked at the medicine bottle on the table, the damp cloth folded beside it, the half-cut loaf, and the coat Pellin had apparently slept in.

"Who knew you were leaving Reedglass?"

"Talia. Housekeeper. Laundry mistress. Probably half the servants by breakfast."

"Anyone ask you to carry something before you left?"

Pellin's hands stopped.

Rhyl noticed.

"Yes?"

"A message."

"From whom?"

"Courier at receiving."

"House courier?"

"River Ledger. Grey coat."

Rhyl leaned forward slightly.

"What message?"

"He asked whether Lord Aereth's council packet had reached the east room. Said the transfer office had a duplicate receipt and needed confirmation before they closed the route."

"Did you confirm it?"

"I said I'd seen the packet on the writing desk."

"Had you?"

"Yes."

"Anything odd about it?"

Pellin stared at the floor.

"The seal."

"What about it?"

"Cracked. Not broken open. Just cracked across one edge. I remember because Lord Aereth got angry when I brought the laundry back."

"At you?"

"At the packet."

Pellin looked embarrassed now, as if the memory had become impolite.

"He asked why somebody had opened it. I told him nobody had, not properly. The wax still crossed the fold. He said he remembered reading it the night before."

Rhyl did not move.

Lessa had stopped making jokes.

"And had he?"

"I don't know. I wasn't there the night before."

"What happened?"

"He broke the seal. Read it. Then asked me why it hadn't been brought up earlier."

Rain ticked against the bad corner of the window.

Rhyl let the silence sit.

Pellin looked at him.

"That matters, doesn't it?"

"It might."

"That's a judge's answer."

"I've had a difficult morning."

Pellin almost smiled.

Rhyl asked him to describe the courier.

Young. Dark hair. River Ledger grey. No obvious house badge. Expensive silver ring with a green stone that looked absurd on a runner's hand. Clove scent, strong enough that Pellin remembered it because the receiving corridor usually smelled of wet paper and coal smoke.

No name.

No reason, at the time, to ask.

"Would you know him again?"

Pellin considered rather than answering quickly.

"I think so."

"Good enough. Don't go looking for him."

"Wasn't planning to."

Rhyl stood.

Pellin stood too.

"Do I come back to Reedglass?"

Rhyl looked at Lessa.

She looked dreadful but alert.

"Not because I told you to. Judge Vaun may want a formal statement. Give me an address where a runner can find you. Stay with your sister unless the healer tells you otherwise."

Lessa lifted one hand weakly.

"See? Useful."

Pellin gave her a look.

Rhyl reached the door.

"One more thing."

Pellin waited.

"Did you take anything from Lord Aereth's room besides laundry and the coat?"

"No."

"Did anyone ask you for his hair, nails, clothing, wash water, used linen, or anything personal?"

Pellin's disgust arrived before fear.

"No."

Rhyl believed the disgust.

He did not mistake it for proof.

"All right."

Lessa called after him as he opened the door.

"Red Hart."

He looked back.

"If you arrest my brother later, bring soup."

"I'll tell the judge it's a condition of surrender."

Pellin shut the door on her laughter.

At Reedglass House, Damaris was learning that the service map in her head had been drawn by someone who had never carried a full water can.

That someone was her.

Maela Sorn, the laundry mistress, corrected the third route Damaris drew across the east floor.

"No. Full cans use the central lift. Empty cans can go down the east stair."

"Why?"

Maela stared at her.

"Because water is heavy."

Damaris crossed out the line.

Talia, seated opposite her at the small accounting table, hid a smile.

"You may laugh."

"I would never, my lady."

"Coward."

"Employed coward."

That got Damaris.

She laughed, and for a few seconds the winter room stopped feeling like an annex of a court.

Aereth had been told enough to understand why Judge Vaun wanted his rooms left alone. He had responded badly, then less badly, then asked for coffee. Damaris considered the coffee encouraging.

He came down while Maela was explaining the service lift.

His silver hair had been tied back carelessly. He looked tired rather than ill, which meant nothing and nevertheless made Damaris feel better.

His gaze went first to Talia.

She went rigid.

Aereth stopped.

"Talia."

"My lord."

He looked at Damaris as if hoping she might rescue him.

She did not.

He deserved the dignity of doing this himself.

"I accused you of taking my signet."

"Yes."

"It was in my washroom."

"Yes."

"I was wrong."

Talia waited.

Aereth swallowed.

"I am sorry."

The room remained quiet.

Talia's hands were folded in her lap.

"Thank you."

Nothing more.

No instant absolution because a nobleman had managed the minimum decent sentence.

Aereth nodded.

He looked relieved anyway.

Damaris found that she loved him a little for apologizing and was still angry with him for making the apology necessary. Apparently this was going to be the governing condition of family from now on.

Judge Vaun entered behind him.

"Lord Aereth. I would like your permission to inspect the east guest room and secure your sleep tincture for testing."

Aereth's shoulders tightened.

"You think I'm drugged."

"I think you have reported poor sleep and memory problems, and there is an unidentified construction beneath your window containing personal materials. I want fewer unanswered questions."

He looked at Damaris.

She did not nod for him.

Did not shake her head either.

"Will I get the tincture back?" he asked.

"If the laboratory releases it."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I decide whether I have grounds to seek authority without your consent."

Aereth considered that.

"At least you're asking."

"Yes."

He rubbed his thumb against the side of his hand.

"Fine. Inspect the room. Take the bottle."

Vaun nodded once.

Damaris rose with the others.

The blue guest room overlooked the dead rosemary.

Nothing about it looked sinister. The bed had been made. Three council papers lay beside the writing desk. A coat hung over the chair. The washstand held a dark glass bottle with a Green Covenant label tied around its neck.

Mireth read it without touching.

"Sleep tincture. Hawthorn, moonmint, low dreamleaf. Eight drops in water."

Aereth leaned against the doorframe.

"I've taken it for months."

"Any trouble before?" Vaun asked.

"No."

"Same bottle?"

"No. This one was filled before I came here."

Mireth looked at him.

"Who filled it?"

"My physician's dispensary."

Vaun's clerk recorded the answer.

Damaris looked at the writing desk.

One packet lay beneath the others, River Transit Assessment Council stamped across the face.

Its seal was broken.

She did not touch it.

"Aereth."

He followed her gaze.

Something in his expression changed.

"What?"

"When did you open that?"

"Yesterday morning."

He answered too quickly, then frowned.

"No. The night before."

Damaris felt cold move through her stomach.

Aereth stared at the packet.

"I read it after dinner."

Nobody spoke.

He looked at Meris.

"Didn't I?"

Meris's face had gone very still.

"I wasn't with you after dinner."

Aereth looked back at the packet.

"I remember the canal figures."

Damaris stepped closer but stopped before touching him.

"What figures?"

He opened his mouth.

Nothing came.

His anger arrived almost immediately.

"Don't do that."

"Do what?"

"Look at me as if I'm already mad."

Damaris flinched.

"I'm not."

"Everyone is."

Talia stood in the corridor behind Vaun.

Aereth saw her.

The anger went out of him as quickly as it had come.

He sat on the edge of the bed.

"I don't know what I remember."

Damaris crossed the room then.

She sat beside him, leaving enough space that he could choose whether to close it.

After a moment, Aereth's shoulder touched hers.

"Then we don't decide today," she said.

He laughed without humor.

"You sound like a judge."

"Gods forbid."

That earned the smallest breath of a smile.

Footsteps came quickly up the service stair.

Rhyl appeared at the open door, damp from the rain and breathing as though he had taken the last flight two steps at a time.

His eyes found Damaris first.

Then the room.

Then Aereth.

Damaris stood.

"You found him."

"Yes."

"Is he all right?"

"His sister is sick. That's why he left."

Talia closed her eyes briefly.

Relief.

Rhyl looked at Vaun.

"He remembers a River Ledger courier asking about Aereth's council packet. Young man. Dark hair. Silver ring with a green stone. Smelled of cloves."

Damaris looked at the packet on the desk.

Rhyl continued.

"Pellin says the seal was cracked but still across the fold. Aereth told him he'd already read it. Then Pellin watched him break the seal, read it, and ask why it hadn't been brought up sooner."

Aereth went white.

No one rushed to fill the silence.

Vaun looked at the packet.

Then at the tincture.

Then at Aereth.

"We test the medicine. We preserve the packet. We identify the courier."

Aereth's voice was quiet.

"And me?"

Vaun met his eyes.

"You stay somewhere you trust tonight. You don't sign anything important until we know more."

Aereth looked at Meris.

"Here?"

Meris crossed the room and took his hand.

"Here."

Damaris watched her aunt hold her cousin's hand and thought again of Rhyl's question about cost.

Meris could turn people into useful pieces.

She could also love them.

The terrible part was that neither fact cancelled the other.

Rhyl came to Damaris's side.

His fingers brushed hers.

She turned her hand and caught them.

This time neither of them let go.
