# Two Errands and a Dispatch Mark

The outgoing tray was still full when Ilyan came back the following morning.

He found Vaun's signed inquiry beneath a notice about a collapsed stair and above a request to borrow two benches for a public hearing. Its fold was exactly as Neral had left it. Ilyan had the uncomfortable sensation that an entire night of thinking ought to have moved the paper at least an inch.

"The courier comes after the morning petitions," Neral said without looking up.

"I know."

"Then stop watching the tray."

"I wasn't."

"You have been standing in the doorway long enough for me to count four denials."

Neral pushed a narrow docket across the desk. The cooper's nephew had been examined the previous evening, though the physician's written finding had not yet arrived. The foreman's statement was still missing. The three friends who had offered to swear to the boy's honesty remained, in Neral's handwriting, under a heading marked *character, not occurrence*.

"Is that unkind?" Ilyan asked.

"It is accurate. They may be good friends. They cannot tell us how a stack fell if they were in another yard."

"Can the boy?"

"He can tell us what happened to him. That may not be the same as telling us why it happened."

Neral had arranged a short visit, subject to the physician's permission. He handed Ilyan the docket and a blank sheet for observations.

"Not a deposition," he said. "Not yet. Find out what assistance is needed, what he remembers, and who was actually there. If he tires, you leave."

"You're trusting me with this?"

"I'm trusting you to know when to stop. It is a different sort of confidence."

Outside, the market carts were beginning their slow argument with the street. A wheel struck a paving joint; someone shouted for room; the smell of wet wood rose from a load of boards. Ilyan passed the records depot road without turning toward it.

The cooper had taken his nephew into a back room where a window admitted enough light to show the dust suspended above a washbasin. The boy lay on a narrow bed with one leg supported by folded blankets. He was old enough to resent being called a boy and young enough to look frightened when no one was speaking to him.

His uncle met Ilyan at the door.

"He's honest," the cooper said immediately.

"I haven't come to test that."

"Those men know him."

"I believe they do."

The cooper looked past Ilyan, as though Neral might have followed to contradict him.

"I thought if enough people spoke for him, the Hall would have to listen."

"The Hall is listening. But the question of what they saw is separate from whether they care for him."

The man rubbed a thumb across his palm. There were pale lines there where work had worn the skin smooth.

"The physician says he must keep the weight off it for now."

"Did the physician say how long?"

"Not until he sees him again."

"Then we shouldn't write a number."

The cooper's face tightened. "A man cannot pay his rent with 'for now.'"

Ilyan had no answer that would improve the rent. He asked whether the boy wanted to speak, and whether the physician had forbidden it. The cooper shook his head at the second question and stepped aside for the first.

The boy's name was on the docket, but Ilyan did not speak it aloud. He sat on a stool rather than standing over the bed.

"Your uncle says a stack fell."

"It did."

"Were you working under it?"

"Beside it. I was carrying something away."

"What did you see before it moved?"

The boy stared at the blanket. "I saw the edge coming."

"Did you see what made it move?"

"No."

"Did you hear anyone call out?"

"I heard a shout after."

"Whose?"

"I don't know."

Ilyan wrote only what the boy had said. The boy watched the pen with an expression Ilyan recognized from witnesses who feared that a hesitation would become an accusation.

"I wasn't playing," he said.

"I haven't written that you were."

"People say lads fool about."

"People say many things when they haven't been there."

He asked if anyone else had been near enough to see the stack before it shifted. The boy remembered two workers at the far end of the yard, but could not say whether either had been looking toward him. One had helped lift the fallen material afterward. The boy could describe his coat, not his name.

"Would you recognize him?"

"I think so."

"That's enough for today."

"I could tell you more."

"You might. You might also be tired."

The boy looked relieved and annoyed at once. Ilyan closed the docket.

His uncle followed him into the passage. "Will they pay him while he can't work?"

"Who is 'they'?"

"The yard."

"I don't know what the yard's rules provide. I can ask the Hall clerk where a wage or relief question belongs. I cannot turn your nephew's injury statement into a ruling on payment."

"Then what good is the statement?"

"It may establish what happened. The physician's finding will establish what care he needs and what he can safely do. The question of wages has to be put to the people who can answer it."

The cooper was silent. Then he said, "I thought you were all one office."

"Sometimes we make that mistake ourselves."

At the Hall, Neral read the observations without praising them. He put a mark beside *shout after* and another beside the worker who had helped lift the fallen material.

"Neither proves who saw the fall," he said.

"No. But one may lead to a witness."

"And the physician?"

"Still no written report. The uncle says the boy has been told not to bear weight, but I marked that as his account, not the physician's finding."

Neral nodded. He opened a separate petition slip for the cooper's question about wages and relief. He did not copy the boy's account into it.

"Why separate the papers?" Ilyan asked, although he knew.

"Because someone may need help before we can establish fault. And because a request for help should not become evidence that the person asking has accused anyone."

Ilyan considered the signed inquiry in the outgoing tray. A request about whether a transferred volume had been consulted was also meant to remain narrower than the conclusions he wished it could support.

"Can I take the wage slip back?"

"After the clerk has checked which office receives it. Not before. You cannot promise an answer from an office that has not seen the question."

By late morning Vaun had heard the stair complaint and refused to authorize the borrowed benches until someone identified who would return them. The courier arrived with a canvas bag and a ledger held open by his thumb. Neral read the addresses aloud, checked the seals, and passed over the packets one at a time.

Ilyan stood far enough away not to obstruct him.

When Vaun's narrow requisition inquiry went into the bag, the courier entered its dispatch number and gave Neral a collection mark. Neral wrote the time beside the Hall's outgoing entry.

"Is that receipt enough?" Ilyan asked after the man had gone.

"It is proof that the courier collected it," Neral said. "Not that the custodian received it, not that anyone searched the schedule, and certainly not that anyone found a requisition."

"How long until we can ask?"

"When the ordinary delivery interval has passed. Then we may ask whether it arrived."

Ilyan imagined the folded paper among the other packets, passing through hands that had no reason to know its importance to him. It was easier to bear than the image of a secret answer waiting in a locked room. Most of the journey would consist of streets, a desk, and someone making an entry in a book.

Vaun found him at the window again.

"Have you learned anything useful?"

"The boy can say where he stood, but not what shifted the stack. The physician's written report is outstanding. We have a possible worker to locate, and a separate wage question."

"And the other inquiry?"

"Collected. Not delivered."

She glanced at him. "That distinction is beginning to sound natural in your mouth."

"It still feels like very little."

"Little things are what prevent us from telling a family the wrong thing."

He thought of the cooper's hands and the boy watching the pen. There were several ways to injure a person while insisting that one was trying to help.

Neral called from the next room. The Hall clerk had identified the proper counter for the wage question, but could not yet say what relief, if any, would be available. Ilyan took the slip. He would carry it back without adding an assurance that had not been given.

The dispatch mark stayed in Neral's ledger. The physician's report had not arrived. The foreman's account remained missing. No answer had come from the historical custodian or from the separate sealed civic inquiry. The morning had changed none of those facts.

It had given Ilyan two errands he could do honestly.

He took his coat and went out before he could turn them into a theory.
