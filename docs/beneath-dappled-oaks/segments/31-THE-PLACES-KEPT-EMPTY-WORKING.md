# The Places Kept Empty

The pear tree had survived the winter. Its ribbon had not.

Mireth held the loosened strip of cloth while Elen examined the place where the knot had cut into the young bark. A visitor had tied a promise to the branch during Leafrise, and another had tightened it after the rain. The swelling wood had nearly grown around the knot. Now there was a pale wound beneath it, wet and shallow.

“Will it heal?”

“If everyone leaves it alone,” Elen said.

“Then we may have to close the garden.”

“We may have to move the tree.”

Mireth laughed. Elen, her older cousin, did not. She was concentrating on the branch. Outside the wall, Aurelith had begun dressing itself for Bloomtide. Carts brought garlands and flowering boughs through the streets, and a woman near the gate was arguing over the price of thirteen wreaths. The garden's open afternoon would begin after the midday bells. There were seed cakes to divide, paths to sweep, and guests to seat.

A folded note waited beneath the watering jug. Mireth recognized Silae's writing.

The note concerned chairs. Two in the patron row had been set together, one for Mireth and one for Caelen. Her mother had requested the arrangement; his had not objected. Beneath their names Silae had drawn the narrow line used to mark household guests.

Mireth read it twice.

Elen looked up. “What?”

“They've found a way to make our courtship part of the furniture.”

“Whose furniture?”

“Everyone's, apparently.”

Elen read the note and returned it without comment. She began washing the wound on the tree.

“Would you sit there?” Mireth asked.

“Not if it were my courtship. But it isn't.”

Mireth wanted to be angry with her for that answer. Instead she carried the damaged ribbon to the basket and finished washing the jug. The tree needed tending whether her mother had arranged an entire banquet or only two chairs.

Silae was in the records room, surrounded by guest slips and baskets of unused ribbons. Her shoes stood neatly beneath the bench. The older woman had spent decades learning how to make a refusal sound less like an insult, and she did not seem surprised to see Mireth.

“Please tell me you have come to count cakes.”

“I've come about the chairs.”

“Less useful.”

“Did you agree to it?”

“I made space for it. You may decline.”

“Then why write our names?”

“Because the servants need to know which chairs to carry. We have six guests who cannot stand through the rite. I will not put them in the path because two mothers are hoping to be pleased.”

Mireth stopped. The two chairs were at the end of the row, not in the middle. Silae had made provision, not a proclamation. That was better, though it did not settle what the arrangement would mean to the families who had proposed it.

“I want Caelen here.”

“Then invite him.”

“I don't want the Covenant presenting him as my promised husband.”

“We wouldn't.”

“The households might.”

“They might do that if you stood at opposite ends of the garden. People who wish to arrange a marriage are rarely defeated by distance.”

Mireth sat on the bench, nudging one of Silae's shoes. A novice was practicing the afternoon response in the next room, always entering a beat too soon.

“I want to be seen with him,” she said. “I don't want to be seated as though I have already agreed to something else.”

Silae took her pen and crossed out the names. She wrote *available for patrons* beside the two places.

“There. Now help me find a chair with arms for old Master Vell, who cannot rise from the narrow ones.”

They worked through the slips. One guest had an injured knee. Another was fasting and would need a place near the water table. Two merchants were bringing children who would abandon their seats after the first song. Silae knew the older guests' habits, the servants' names, and which garden woman had a baby at home. Mireth found that arranging the afternoon for real people took longer than arguing over the two chairs that had offended her.

“Will my mother be angry?” she asked at last.

“Possibly. She may also be relieved that you have chosen for yourself before she has to guess.”

Silae reached for another slip.

“Tell him yourself. I've enough messages to send.”

---

Caelen found Captain Edras in the equipment room, inspecting ceremonial scabbards. A young attendant was laying out straps on the long table. The fittings gleamed beneath the window, except for one bent buckle set aside in a wooden tray.

“You're not on the garden escort,” Edras said.

“I was coming to ask.”

“Your cousin Ysran suggested you for the front post. I assigned another man.”

“Why?”

“Because your family will make something of your standing there, and because you are seeing one of the novices. You can do the duty. I don't doubt that. But the duty isn't there to improve your family's courtship arrangements.”

Caelen felt his ears grow warm. He had wanted to perform the escort well. A visible assignment mattered to a young man in royal service, and Ysran's recommendation was not worthless simply because his cousin could be insufferable.

“Is that a punishment?”

“No. Count the stores. There are twelve scabbards and eleven serviceable buckles. Find out why.”

The attendant kept his eyes on the table. Caelen began checking the straps. The twelfth buckle had been set aside because its tongue was bent and would not hold under strain. Nothing had been lost; the inventory was wrong.

He brought the tray to Edras.

“This one.”

“Then correct the list. Don't make the boy invent a missing piece because the paper says there should be twelve.”

Caelen wrote the correction, more slowly than necessary. He knew he had lost an opportunity to be noticed at the front of a procession. Edras had not pretended otherwise, and that made the decision easier to respect.

“May I attend afterward?”

“In a coat, when your watch ends. We are not issuing you a uniform for courting.”

“Thank you, Captain.”

“Find the other defects before you thank me.”

---

When Caelen reached the garden, the opening words had already been spoken. The novice response ended just as he passed through the gate. People began rising from their chairs, glad to talk and eat. The air smelled of mint, wet stone, and honeyed thirteen-seed cakes.

Mireth stood at the distribution table in a dark green working dress, her sleeves rolled back. She was cutting the last cake into smaller pieces for children who had already eaten more than their share. When she saw him, her expression changed so quickly that he forgot what he had intended to say.

“You came.”

“I was delayed by a buckle.”

“Was it dangerous?”

“Terribly. I entered it in the stores book.”

She caught his hand and kissed him across the corner of the table. A boy behind Caelen asked whether the cakes were free. Mireth gave him a piece and led Caelen away.

He noticed the two empty chairs at the end of the patron row. Their arrangement was almost painfully obvious.

“My mother wrote,” he said.

“I know.”

“I thought perhaps I should stay away. Then they couldn't make anything of it.”

Mireth's hand loosened in his.

“Of us?”

“Of the chairs.”

“You might have asked me.”

“I know.”

He saw the hurt before she looked away. He had imagined his absence as a kindness, a way of keeping the families from using him to speak for her. He had not imagined what it would be like for Mireth to wait in the garden and wonder whether he was ashamed to be seen with her.

“I was wrong,” he said. “I should have asked.”

“I took our names off the patron row,” she said. “I didn't take yours off my afternoon.”

Caelen laughed with relief. “Where would you like me?”

“With me, when I finish here. And not at the front of a procession because Ysran thinks it would look well.”

“He suggested it.”

“Of course he did.”

“Edras assigned someone else.”

“Did that hurt?”

“A little.”

She looked at him, neither pleased nor dismissive. That was one of the things he loved about her: she did not require his loss to be a victory for her.

“I would rather earn the next assignment,” he said.

Mireth touched the back of his neck and drew him down until their foreheads met. A woman passing with a tray of cups glanced at them, smiled in the unstartled way of someone who had seen young lovers before, and went on.

Mireth kissed him again. It was not a household announcement, though anyone nearby could have seen it.

Then she took him to the pear tree. She showed him the place where the ribbon had cut into the bark and the fresh cloth beneath the wound.

“Does it need a blessing?” he asked.

“It needs people to stop tying things to it.”

“That may be harder.”

“It usually is.”

He carried the empty watering jug while she checked the bandage. The late market bells began to sound beyond the wall. He had to return to the stores before the evening count, and she had tables to clear. Neither pretended the other's work could wait.

They walked together to the gate. The two chairs remained in the patron row. Before the last guests departed, Silae gave one to an elderly visitor who had grown tired. The other was carried back to the storehouse.
