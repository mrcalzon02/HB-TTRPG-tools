# The Book That Had Already Left

The depot sent word on the fourth working day.

Neral brought the notice while Ilyan was trying to explain to a cooper why three friends who had not witnessed his nephew's accident could not certify the hour of it. The cooper had spent most of the morning assembling people willing to swear the boy was honest.

“I don't doubt his honesty,” Ilyan said.

“Then why won't you take their statements?”

“Because none of them saw the stack fall.”

Neral took the accident form and pointed out the spaces for the foreman's account and the physician's finding. The cooper studied them. The boy, he admitted, had been asking for a physician since yesterday.

When the man left, Neral set a second paper on the desk.

“The transfer book is ready.”

“Salver?”

“A book about books, Ilyan. You will have to see what it says.”

“I can go now.”

“You can. I have the cooper's physician to find.”

Ilyan nearly asked him to come anyway. He had begun to take Neral's presence for granted, and the recognition was embarrassing enough that he let the clerk return to the petitioner docket without another word.

---

The depot reading room was long and low. The upper windows stood open, admitting the scent of damp stone and the chatter of a shopkeeper outside who was instructing his daughter how to take down the last Bloomtide garlands. She was ignoring him with great concentration.

The clerk recognized Ilyan's receipt before she recognized him.

“One book,” she said, “and you may not turn its pages yourself.”

“I wasn't going to.”

“Most people aren't, until they do.”

The annual transfer book lay open on a felt pad. Four lines described the relevant returns series: a volume number, a range of years, a count of leaves, and an accession date. A marginal notation directed subsequent consultation inquiries to a different schedule.

Ilyan read the date twice. The volume covering the post-Silver-Night reservation period had been transferred into historical custody before Salver's later appointment to the Secretariat.

He set his copy of Salver's appointment order beside the book.

“May I have this certified?”

“The inventory line, yes. It proves that a volume with that date range was recorded as transferred. It does not certify that a particular acknowledgment survives inside it.”

He nodded.

“You're disappointed,” she said.

“I thought it might have been on his desk.”

“It was recorded here before he held that post.”

“So he couldn't have seen it.”

“I didn't say that. A later requisition could have brought it back for consultation. Or there might have been a copy. Or he may never have asked for either.”

“Is there a requisition book?”

“A schedule. The entries include several classes of material, some restricted. Your judge can ask the custodian for a statement limited to this volume and a specified period. Whether anything can be certified is for that office to decide.”

Ilyan copied the four lines, the accession mark, and the office responsible for the later schedule. He asked her to repeat the last title when the scratching of his pen swallowed a word.

The inventory had no blot over a forbidden name, no mysterious missing leaf, no indication that anyone had tried to hide the transfer. A clerk had counted a book and sent it to a room where it would be kept dry.

Outside, the daughter had succeeded in taking down the garlands. Her father was now arguing about how to coil the rope.

Ilyan paid the certification fee from the Hall's allowance and stepped into the square. Salver's earlier handling of the lawful timing schedules and interference memorandum remained documented. Those dates had not changed. But the later Secretariat desk where Ilyan had imagined an old register lying within reach had not ordinarily held that volume when Salver arrived.

He had constructed a room from the name of an office. The book had already left it.
