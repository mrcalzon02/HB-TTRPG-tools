# Chapter Five continuation — The Packet Remembered Twice

Vaun began with the washstand.

Not because it was dramatic. Because it was nearest the door.

Damaris found that reassuring.

The judge's clerk photographed the room before anything moved. Mireth read labels. Vaun asked Aereth what belonged to him and what had already been in the guest room when he arrived. Talia answered only when asked about household property.

A dark bottle stood beside the basin.

Mireth leaned close enough to read the tied paper label without touching it.

"Sleep tincture."

Aereth folded his arms.

"Prescribed."

"I can see that."

"You sounded interested."

"I am interested in everything in a room someone may have taken material from."

Aereth looked at Damaris.

"Is she always like this?"

"Worse when she's rested."

Mireth did not turn around. "I can hear you."

"That was the intention."

For a moment the room felt almost ordinary.

Then Vaun asked, "When did you last take it?"

Aereth looked back at the bottle.

"Last night."

"Dose?"

"Eight drops."

He hesitated.

Damaris saw it.

So did everyone else.

Aereth's mouth tightened.

"I think."

Nobody rushed to fill the silence.

He uncrossed his arms.

"Take it."

Vaun looked at him.

"For laboratory testing?"

"Yes."

"You understand I am asking, not ordering."

Aereth gave her a tired look.

"Judge, if one more person asks whether I understand a sentence, I may become unreasonable purely for variety. Take the bottle."

Vaun's clerk sealed it.

Talia's gaze had gone to the travel coat hanging over the back of a chair.

"Those aren't the original buttons."

Aereth glanced down.

Two plain brass replacements sat where the coat crossed his chest.

"One tore off in the carriage."

"Both were loose," Talia said. "I sent it to mending the morning after you arrived."

Damaris thought of the broken button inside the bundle.

"Would mending keep the originals?"

"If they were reusable. Otherwise scrap."

"And scrap goes where?"

"Button tins. rag bins. apprentices' practice boards. Sometimes children take the pretty ones."

Aereth looked at Damaris.

"You seem disappointed."

"I was hoping for one locked cupboard and a villain with a key."

"Those are mostly in plays."

"I know. It remains inconsiderate."

Vaun moved to the desk.

Two stacks of council papers sat beneath a bronze paperweight. The upper packet carried the River Transit Assessment Council seal.

Aereth saw her looking at it.

"That came the first morning."

Damaris felt something shift in the room.

"Do you remember opening it?" Vaun asked.

"Yes."

Aereth reached for the packet, stopped, and stared at his own hand.

"I read it before breakfast."

Talia's face had gone still.

Damaris noticed.

Aereth noticed Damaris noticing.

"What?"

"Nothing yet."

"I am becoming very tired of 'yet.'"

Footsteps sounded in the corridor.

Rhyl appeared in the doorway with rain darkening his shoulders and a folded statement tucked inside his coat.

His eyes found Damaris first.

The relief was small, immediate and entirely mutual.

Then he saw Aereth and stopped.

Vaun said, "You found Pellin."

"Yes. His sister is ill. He stayed with her."

Aereth's expression sharpened.

"Why were you looking for my laundry runner?"

"Because some of the material in the garden passed through laundry or mending."

Rhyl looked to Vaun.

She nodded once.

"Tell us what he said."

Rhyl did not perform it.

He gave them the basket, the coat, the blood on the shaving cloth, the ordinary handoffs Pellin could remember. Then the courier.

"River Ledger grey. Young. Green-stone ring. Strong smell of cloves. Pellin didn't know him."

Damaris's attention caught on the ring.

"Expensive?"

"Pellin thought so."

"Thought."

"Yes."

Rhyl looked at Aereth.

"He also remembers the council packet."

Aereth's hand remained on the desk.

"What about it?"

"Pellin says you told him you had already opened it while the wax was still across the fold. Then he watched you break the seal."

Aereth stared at him.

"No."

Rhyl did not answer.

"I read it before breakfast."

Talia looked down.

Aereth saw.

"Talia."

She raised her eyes.

"Was he there?"

"Yes."

"Did I say that?"

A long pause.

"Yes, my lord."

Aereth's fingers left the packet.

"No."

The word was quieter this time.

Damaris moved closer but did not touch him.

"Aereth."

"I remember it."

"I know."

His head turned sharply.

"You weren't here."

"No."

"Then don't tell me you know."

Damaris let the anger pass through him.

"I know you remember it."

He stared at her.

For a moment she thought he would snap again.

Instead the fight went out of his shoulders.

"That's worse."

"Probably."

"You could lie."

"You would know."

"I might not. Apparently."

That hurt him as soon as he said it.

Damaris crossed the remaining distance and put her hand on the desk beside his, palm down. An offer, not a claim.

Aereth looked at it.

Then placed two fingers over her knuckles.

Very lightly.

Rhyl looked away first.

Not because the contact was private. Because he understood when attention itself became an intrusion.

Vaun asked Talia, "Anything after the packet was opened?"

Talia nodded reluctantly.

"That evening Lord Aereth asked why it hadn't arrived."

Aereth closed his eyes.

Nobody said anything.

Rain whispered against the old glass.

At last Aereth asked, "What happens to me now?"

Vaun's answer was practical.

"The tincture goes to the laboratory. The grey powder is already there. The packet is preserved. I want the receiving log and courier credential checked. Until we know more, I would prefer you not make irreversible council decisions today."

Aereth opened his eyes.

"Prefer."

"Yes."

"Not order."

"Not at present."

He looked at the council papers.

The emergency canal levy waited beneath his hand. Bridges, freight priorities, repair assessments, grain movement. Decisions with consequences well beyond Reedglass House.

"I have a committee session this afternoon."

Meris, who had been silent near the door, said, "You have a headache."

Aereth gave her a murderous look.

"I do now."

"Excellent. Then for once the family explanation is true."

Damaris almost laughed.

Aereth heard it and looked at her.

"Traitor."

"Temporarily."

He rubbed his face.

"One day."

Vaun nodded.

"One day."

Talia shifted near the wardrobe.

Aereth looked at her again.

"And you should not be serving this room."

Her expression hardened.

"My lord—"

"Not because I think you did anything."

He stopped and tried again.

"Because I accused you yesterday, and if I become confused again I do not want you standing nearest when I decide someone has wronged me."

Talia's anger changed shape.

Damaris watched her consider him.

"I can accept that," she said.

"Thank you."

"It is not forgiveness."

"I know."

That seemed to matter to both of them.

Vaun sealed the council packet without opening it further. The existing break in the wax was photographed. The paper went into a document sleeve.

Damaris looked at the River Ledger stamp.

"Receiving log first."

Meris glanced at her.

"You can access it from here."

Damaris felt the temporary cipher card in her pocket.

Rhyl's attention moved to her, not the pocket.

He knew it was there.

He did not ask.

Damaris looked at Aereth.

"Do you want us to check?"

He gave her an exhausted smile.

"I want someone to tell me whether the man who brought that packet was real."

The Reedglass receiving ledger had excellent handwriting and very little mercy.

Damaris sat at Meris's desk with Talia on one side and Vaun's clerk on the other. Rhyl stood behind her chair. Mireth had gone with the laboratory courier and promised to return before anyone did anything interesting, a promise everyone understood would be broken by circumstance rather than intention.

The entry was where Talia said it would be.

Council packet. River Transit Assessment Council. East guest suite.

The receiving signature belonged to Pellin.

The courier line contained a name.

**Eren Vale.**

Damaris read it twice.

"Know him?" Rhyl asked.

"No."

Talia shook her head too.

Vaun's clerk copied the entry exactly.

Damaris did not reach for the cipher immediately.

Rhyl noticed.

"What does the search cost?"

Meris, across the room, went still.

Damaris looked at him.

The question had become theirs now. Not accusation. Not permission. A request to name the price before paying it.

"The query will be audited. My family will know I searched the runner registry."

"Will they see the result?"

"Not automatically. They can ask why I searched."

Rhyl nodded.

"Anything else?"

Damaris considered the answer.

"It tells them this investigation touched River Ledger custody again."

Meris said, "Which I already know."

Damaris looked at her aunt.

"You are not the whole family."

Meris's mouth curved faintly.

"A cruel observation before luncheon."

"I learned from experts."

Damaris took out the cipher.

She entered the runner name exactly as written.

The registry began to search.

No one spoke.

Rhyl's hand settled on the back of her chair, close enough that his knuckles brushed one loose strand of her hair.

Damaris resisted the urge to lean back into him.

Mostly.

The registry returned a credential entry.

She read the first line.

Then the second.

Her pulse quickened.

"What?" Vaun asked.

Damaris did not answer until she had read the status twice.

"Eren Vale has a temporary bonded-runner credential."

Rhyl leaned closer.

"So he is real."

"No."

Damaris turned the registry slate so the others could see.

The credential existed.

The sponsoring seal did not.

A red audit mark sat beside the record.

**SPONSOR SEAL DUPLICATE — CREDENTIAL SUSPENDED PENDING REVIEW.**

Date of suspension: that morning.

Meris came to the desk.

"When was it issued?"

Damaris scrolled.

"Nine days ago."

"Deliveries?"

A route tab sat beneath the credential.

Damaris looked at Vaun.

The judge said, "Preserve the credential page first. Then the route."

So they did.

Only after the clerk had certified the screen did Damaris open the delivery history.

Seven entries appeared.

Reedglass House was the most recent.

The others were only addresses and dates until someone followed them.

Damaris's eyes moved down the list.

One institutional address stopped her.

She did not say why.

Not yet.

Rhyl saw her face change.

"Damaris?"

She pointed.

Four days before Silver Night, Eren Vale's credential had recorded a delivery to the Concord Academy of Aurelith.

The room went silent.

Vaun leaned over the desk.

"Contents?"

"Not listed here."

"Recipient?"

"Not here."

"Then we have an address and a date."

Damaris nodded.

That was all.

It was also enough to make the rain outside seem suddenly very far away.

Rhyl's fingers touched her shoulder.

This time she did lean back.

Only a little.

Meris read the Academy line over her niece's shoulder and said nothing at all.
