# Chapter Five continuation — Pellin Doss and the East Room

Rhyl found Pellin Doss three streets beyond Lower Reed, in a room above a cooper's shop where the stairs smelled of wet oak and boiled onions.

A woman coughed behind the door before he knocked.

Pellin opened it with his sleeves rolled to the elbows and a basin in his hands. He was younger than Rhyl had expected, perhaps twenty-six or twenty-seven, with the broad wrists of someone who spent his days carrying other people's laundry and the particular exhausted expression of someone who had not slept because another person could not.

He looked at Rhyl's Red Hart clasp.

"I sent word."

"I know."

"I'm not coming in today."

"I didn't ask you to."

That stopped him.

Behind Pellin, the woman coughed again. The room was warm enough to fog the lower panes. A kettle sat beside the stove. Medicine bottles crowded a chair near the bed.

Rhyl kept his hands where Pellin could see them.

"Judge Vaun wants to know about the east-floor basket you carried three days ago. That's all."

Pellin's face changed.

Not guilt. Rhyl had learned very quickly that guilt was a story people liked to put on faces after they had decided what they wanted the face to mean.

This looked more like calculation.

"What happened?"

"Something was found at Reedglass."

"What?"

"I can't tell you yet."

Pellin glanced back at the bed.

"Lessa."

The woman raised one hand without opening her eyes.

"I have ears."

Pellin shut his mouth.

Rhyl liked her immediately.

"May I come in?"

Pellin hesitated, then moved aside.

Rhyl took the chair nearest the door. Pellin remained standing until Lessa told him to stop looming.

"The basket," Rhyl said.

Pellin rubbed both hands over his face.

"East floor. Guest linen. Two washcloths, bath sheets, shaving cloth, one travel coat for brushing, mending from the blue room."

"Whose coat?"

"Lord Aereth's."

"What needed mending?"

"Two buttons. One nearly off, one gone."

Rhyl remembered the broken brass button on Vaun's table.

"Did you replace them?"

"Mending did. I carried it down."

"Who gave you the basket?"

"Talia."

"Who gave it back?"

"Mara in mending. Not Venn. Mara Pell. No relation to anyone useful."

Lessa opened one eye.

"She is useful."

"You know what I mean."

"I do. It remains stupid."

Rhyl looked down so Pellin would not see him smile.

"Anything unusual?"

Pellin thought.

"The shaving cloth had blood on it."

"Much?"

"No. Razor nick, I assumed."

"Was it returned?"

Pellin frowned.

"I don't know."

"Why not?"

"Because I didn't unpack the basket upstairs. I left it at the service table."

"Who did?"

"Usually Talia. Sometimes whoever is there."

Rhyl nodded.

Pellin's eyes narrowed.

"Is she in trouble?"

"No."

"That was fast."

"She's a witness."

"So am I?"

"At the moment."

Pellin looked toward his sister again.

Rhyl waited.

People often filled silence because they thought silence belonged to whoever was asking questions.

Pellin did not.

Good.

"There was a packet," he said finally.

"With the laundry?"

"No. Later. I was still upstairs because Talia asked me to carry clean water to the blue room. A courier came through receiving with a council packet for Lord Aereth."

"You saw him?"

"Yes."

"Know him?"

"No. River Ledger grey. Young. About my age, maybe younger."

"Anything you'd recognize?"

Pellin shrugged, then stopped.

"Ring."

"What ring?"

"Silver. Green stone. Too expensive for a bonded runner unless he has a rich grandmother."

Lessa said, "Or steals."

Pellin ignored her.

Rhyl said, "Anything else?"

"Cloves."

"He was chewing them?"

"Maybe. Or his coat smelled of them."

Pellin leaned forward.

"The packet was wrong."

Rhyl felt his attention sharpen.

"How?"

"Seal had a crack through it. Not broken open. Just cracked. Talia nearly sent it back. Lord Aereth came out and said he'd already opened it."

"Had he?"

"No."

"How do you know?"

"Because the wax was still across the fold. He broke it while he was saying he'd already read the damned thing."

Lessa's eyes were open now.

Pellin looked embarrassed by the memory, as though witnessing a nobleman's confusion had been an intrusion rather than a clue.

"What did Talia do?"

"Nothing. What was she supposed to do? Tell a councillor he didn't know whether he'd opened his own post?"

Rhyl thought of the signet in the washroom.

"Did Aereth seem ill?"

"Tired. Angry. He snapped at Talia. Then apologized ten minutes later and asked why she hadn't brought the packet yet."

The room went quiet.

Lessa pulled the blanket higher.

"That sounds bad."

"Yes," Rhyl said.

Pellin stared at the floor.

"I didn't steal anything."

"I haven't accused you."

"Someone will."

There was no self-pity in it. Only experience.

Rhyl looked at the room: cheap medicine, damp plaster, a basin cooling beside the bed. A laundry runner who had asked for two days because his sister was sick. A household full of nobles with a frightening object under the garden and enough anxiety to make any absent servant look convenient.

"Judge Vaun sent me because she didn't want half your street frightened," he said.

Pellin looked up.

"That true?"

"Yes."

"And if I don't come back?"

"Then you don't come back until your leave ends, unless she sends an order. I would like a written statement before I go."

Pellin laughed once, without humor.

"You people are strange."

"I've been told."

Lessa said, "Write the statement. Then make him bring coal up before he leaves."

Rhyl looked at her.

"I am an investigator."

"You have arms."

Pellin laughed properly this time.

Rhyl carried the coal.

At Reedglass House, Aereth did not look cursed.

Damaris had not known what she expected.

Something visible, perhaps. Grey skin. Trembling hands. The theatrical generosity of illness that made itself easy to recognize.

Instead her cousin sat in the blue room wearing a cream shirt and dark trousers, reading the same page of a council memorandum for the third time.

She knew because she had been watching from the doorway.

"Are you going to come in?" he asked.

Damaris stepped inside.

"How long did you know I was there?"

"Long enough to become irritated."

That sounded like Aereth.

She took the chair opposite him.

His silver hair had not been tied back. There were shadows beneath his eyes.

"Meris says a judge wants my room."

"She wants to speak to you first."

"How civilized."

"Try not to ruin it."

Aereth looked at her over the memorandum.

"You graduated three days ago and have already become unbearable."

"I had excellent teachers."

A faint smile.

Then it vanished.

"Talia told you."

"About the signet?"

"Yes."

"Aunt did."

Aereth folded the paper once, carefully.

"I apologized."

Damaris waited.

He looked annoyed.

"I did."

"Good."

"You could sound surprised."

"Would you enjoy that?"

"No."

"Then I won't."

He looked toward the window.

Rain had silvered the garden glass below. The dead rosemary was hidden beneath the awning from this angle.

"I found the signet in the washroom," he said. "I remember checking there."

Damaris said nothing.

"Twice."

Still nothing.

Aereth's jaw tightened.

"Say something."

"What would help?"

"Tell me Talia moved it."

"Did she?"

"I don't know."

The words cost him.

Damaris leaned back.

"Then I won't tell you she did."

He closed his eyes.

For a few breaths they listened to the rain.

"There are pieces of your room in the thing they found outside," Damaris said.

His eyes opened.

She watched fear arrive and become anger before it reached his face.

"What pieces?"

"A shaving cloth that may be from this room. Hair. Nail clippings. Old service cloth. A button. Thread the mending room uses. Grey powder that's gone to the laboratory."

Aereth looked at his hands.

His nails were neatly trimmed.

"My hair?"

"We don't know."

"My nails?"

"We don't know."

"Then why tell me?"

"Because Vaun is about to ask permission to inspect the room, and I would rather you hear why before six people walk in with bags."

Aereth laughed softly.

"Meris sent you."

"No."

He looked at her.

"She let me come. That's different."

Aereth studied her face.

"You always liked distinctions."

"Family disease."

This time the smile lasted a little longer.

A knock sounded.

Judge Vaun waited outside with Mireth and her clerk. Talia stood farther down the corridor, not part of the search party and plainly determined to hear whatever she was legally allowed to hear.

Aereth noticed her.

His face tightened.

Then he stood.

"Talia."

She came no closer.

"My lord."

"I was wrong yesterday."

Talia waited.

Aereth swallowed.

"I accused you of theft. I dismissed you. I was wrong, and I am sorry."

No one rescued either of them from the silence afterward.

Talia finally nodded.

"Thank you."

She did not forgive him on command.

Aereth seemed to understand that.

He turned to Vaun.

"You may inspect the room."

Vaun entered.

Mireth followed.

Damaris remained beside Aereth while the work began.

For once, there was nothing useful she could do with a ledger, a cipher, a family name or a clever answer.

So she stayed.
