# Beneath Dappled Oaks — Culinary Atlas of Courtship, Confectionery, and the Romantic Table

This document is active setting canon.

Elaranth possesses an earthlike agricultural base shaped by a long magical civilization. Its people know grain, bread, cheese, butter, wine, beer, cider, tea, coffee-like roasted drinks, chocolate-like confections, preserved fruit, pastries, ice creams, custards, cakes, layered desserts, sauces, pickles, stews, roast meats, fish, sweets, candies, jams, syrups, and elaborate ceremonial cuisine.

Magic has not replaced agriculture.

Magic has made agriculture stranger, preservation more sophisticated, luxury cuisine more theatrical, and the language of gifting considerably more dangerous.

Food is one of the great social languages of the Realm.

A plate can say:

- welcome;
- I remember where you are from;
- I am courting you;
- I am apologizing;
- I know what your mother used to bake;
- I intend to marry you;
- I intend to sleep with you;
- I am testing whether you understand the same implication;
- I am ending this gracefully;
- I am making a political offer;
- I know you are homesick;
- I know you lied to me;
- I am still feeding you anyway.

The socially competent adult is expected to understand at least the common grammar.

The truly dangerous adult understands the regional dialects.

# I. Culinary Magic and Its Limits

Most food magic in Elaranth is practical, sensory, or preservative.

Common legal culinary enchantments may:

- keep a dish warm;
- keep a dessert cold;
- slow spoilage;
- preserve a crisp crust;
- keep cream from separating;
- prevent chocolate from blooming;
- make sugar sparkle;
- reveal a pattern under moonlight;
- release a stored aroma when cracked;
- make a syrup change color with acidity;
- chill a spoon when placed in dessert;
- keep fruit from bruising during travel;
- cause edible flowers to open when served;
- suspend bubbles in a drink;
- make a confection glow faintly;
- create harmless popping or tingling sensations;
- preserve a delicate layered dessert during transport.

These techniques are especially common in:

- noble kitchens;
- confectioners' guilds;
- diplomatic households;
- large inns;
- wedding houses;
- academy banquets;
- wealthy merchant homes.

## The bright line: food must not manufacture desire

A magical food that secretly alters:

- romantic attraction;
- sexual desire;
- judgment;
- inhibition;
- loyalty;
- willingness;
- memory;
- consent;

is not considered ordinary courtship cuisine.

It is illicit compulsion.

Depending on the working, jurisdiction, and harm caused, it may be prosecuted as:

- magical assault;
- poisoning;
- fraud;
- coercive charm-work;
- Dark Arts practice;
- interference with marriage or succession.

This distinction matters enormously in a setting where seduction is politically useful.

A lover may serve someone their favorite chocolate.

They may not secretly enchant that chocolate to make the recipient desire them.

A confection may be beautiful enough to influence a person's mood.

It may not magically overwrite their choice.

# I-A. Illicit Culinary Magic, Poisoning, and the Dangerous Table

Illicit culinary magic is as important to Elaranth's political and romantic culture as legal culinary enchantment.

Food is intimate.

It enters the body willingly.

It passes through kitchens, servants' corridors, bakeries, confectioners' shops, family tables, court banquets, lovers' picnics, diplomatic dinners, festival stalls, and private bedrooms.

That makes it an ideal delivery system for both affection and treachery.

The result is a civilization with centuries of accumulated knowledge about:

- love philters;
- attraction draughts;
- obsession charms;
- truth syrups;
- sleep tinctures;
- dream tonics;
- memory fogs;
- courage and fear concoctions;
- fertility tonics;
- anti-fertility workings;
- curse-laced sweets;
- poisons;
- antidotes;
- masking flavors;
- diagnostic charms;
- kitchen chain-of-custody;
- contamination;
- swapped trays;
- mislabeled bottles;
- enchanted serving ware.

This knowledge is not confined to criminals.

Noble kitchens, royal tasters, physicians, Dark Arts investigators, confectioners' guilds, senior servants, military quartermasters, diplomatic households, and wealthy merchants all study enough of the problem to protect themselves from it.

The paradox is familiar:

**to detect dangerous food magic, somebody must understand how dangerous food magic behaves.**

## Love philters

"Love potion" is a broad folk term rather than one single formula.

Known classes include:

### Keyed attraction philters

These are prepared around a particular intended person.

The drinker becomes unnaturally preoccupied with or attracted toward that keyed individual for the duration of the effect.

A common illicit commercial grade lasts roughly **seventy-two hours**.

It does not create a permanent relationship.

It can, however, produce three days of disastrous judgment, misplaced affection, public embarrassment, contractual vulnerability, and political confusion.

### First-focus philters

These intensify attraction toward the first suitable person the drinker strongly notices after the effect takes hold.

They are considered especially reckless because delivery errors can redirect the effect unpredictably.

### Warmth draughts

These do not create love outright but artificially intensify trust, comfort, fascination, or emotional openness.

They are sometimes sold dishonestly as harmless "mood enhancers."

Secret administration remains coercive.

### Fixation confections

These produce repetitive thoughts about a person, place, promise, or interaction.

A victim may know the fixation is irrational and still struggle to disengage from it.

## Heat-stable kitchen philters

Some illicit preparations are designed to survive:

- baking;
- boiling;
- reduction;
- chocolate tempering;
- sugar cooking;
- freezing.

This makes accidental kitchen incorporation possible.

A vial intended for one cup of chocolate might instead be mistaken for:

- flavor concentrate;
- syrup;
- vanilla-like extract;
- fruit essence;
- glaze;
- medicinal cordial.

If mixed into batter or filling, the active working may distribute through an entire batch.

This is one reason professional kitchens separate medicinal, magical, and culinary concentrates.

It is also one reason crimes still happen when somebody ignores the rule.

## The First Tray Problem

A classic household catastrophe occurs when a magically contaminated batch is divided from a clean batch and the serving instructions are reversed.

For example:

A secret seventy-two-hour keyed love philter is meant to be delivered to one adult through a batch of breakfast muffins.

The philter is mistakenly mixed only into the **first tray**.

A second tray is clean.

Someone in the kitchen then receives an unrelated instruction:

> Take the first tray to her father's table. The second goes upstairs.

The father is in the middle of trade negotiations.

The intended recipient never touches the potion.

The negotiating patriarch eats from the enchanted tray.

For the next seventy-two hours, he may become absurdly and dangerously preoccupied with the person to whom the philter was keyed.

The consequences can include:

- disastrous trade concessions;
- invitations that make no political sense;
- sudden gifts;
- inexplicable defense of the wrong person;
- jealousy;
- family suspicion;
- accusations of bribery;
- accusations of magical assault;
- efforts to hide the exposure until the effect passes;
- frantic attempts to identify which pastries were contaminated;
- servants being questioned;
- rivals noticing that something is wrong;
- the intended conspirator realizing the wrong target was dosed;
- the actual intended target wondering why nothing happened.

This is exactly the kind of chain-reaction plot Elaranthine kitchens are feared for.

The enchantment may expire in seventy-two hours.

The contracts signed, insults delivered, rumors started, kisses attempted, trade secrets revealed, and relationships damaged during those hours do not expire with it.

## Truth preparations

Truth magic in food or drink exists in several forms.

Some lower inhibitions around truthful speech.

Others make deliberate lying uncomfortable.

More aggressive forms compel disclosure.

Use without knowledge or lawful authority is heavily restricted or criminal in many jurisdictions.

Diplomatic households are particularly vigilant because "a surprisingly honest dinner" can be an act of espionage.

## Sleep and dream preparations

These may cause:

- drowsiness;
- unusually deep sleep;
- vivid dreams;
- dream susceptibility;
- difficulty waking.

Legitimate medicinal versions exist.

Secret administration is another matter entirely.

A noble who falls asleep unexpectedly at the wrong banquet may wake to discover that everyone else has already decided what happened.

## Memory fogs

Some illicit preparations interfere with short-term recall or produce uncertainty around recent events.

They are highly feared in:

- sexual assault investigations;
- espionage;
- theft;
- blackmail;
- treaty manipulation;
- staged scandals.

A victim's uncertainty does not mean nothing happened.

Elaranthine investigators know this.

## Fertility and reproductive preparations

Legitimate fertility medicine exists.

So do criminal preparations intended to:

- reduce conception;
- interfere with fertility;
- manipulate reproductive cycles;
- sabotage a marriage;
- conceal or imitate symptoms;
- create misleading evidence of infertility.

These overlap legally and culturally with the forbidden sympathetic fertility curses described elsewhere in the setting.

A potion and a hidden fetish may produce similar political consequences through different magical methods.

## Curse-laced foods

Sympathetic workings may be attached to:

- cakes;
- sweets;
- preserved fruit;
- wine;
- bread;
- spice packets;
- serving vessels.

Sometimes the food is the carrier.

Sometimes consuming it creates a link.

Sometimes the food is innocent and the plate, ribbon, box, or gift wrapping contains the actual working.

Therefore investigators do not merely ask:

**What did the victim eat?**

They ask:

**Who prepared it, who packed it, who carried it, who served it, what touched it, what was substituted, and who had access at every stage?**

## Mundane poison and magical poison

Elaranth distinguishes between ordinary toxic substances and magical poisoning, but political households fear both.

High-status kitchens use:

- ingredient logs;
- sealed storage;
- trusted tasters;
- serving assignments;
- marked trays;
- locked magical concentrates;
- witness procedures for major banquets.

These precautions are imperfect.

That imperfection creates stories.

## Kitchen chain-of-custody

In important households, the journey from pantry to plate can itself be politically meaningful.

A formal banquet may involve:

1. ingredient receipt;
2. pantry storage;
3. preparation;
4. magical finishing;
5. plating;
6. tray assignment;
7. corridor handoff;
8. table placement;
9. final service.

Every step creates an access point.

A poisoner does not necessarily need to enter the kitchen.

A love philter may be introduced at the serving corridor.

A clean plate may be swapped after tasting.

A box of chocolates may be repacked.

A labeled tray may be sent to the wrong room.

A servant may be bribed.

A servant may make an innocent mistake.

The distinction between conspiracy and accident is often the mystery.

## Detection

No single test catches every illicit preparation.

Investigators may use:

- scent and residue examination;
- magical trace work;
- comparison with clean food;
- witness interviews;
- serving records;
- kitchen inventory;
- container examination;
- symptom timing;
- known duration;
- identification of who had access.

Some philters are designed to mimic ordinary emotional behavior.

That makes social evidence as important as magical evidence.

## Antidotes and countermeasures

Countermeasures exist, but they are not universally effective.

Treatment depends on:

- the class of potion;
- how much was consumed;
- how long ago;
- whether the effect is chemical, magical, sympathetic, or mixed.

Royal and wealthy households retain physicians familiar with such exposures.

A major plot may therefore revolve around whether anyone realizes a character is enchanted early enough to intervene.

## Criminal and political consequences

Secret love magic is not socially treated as a cute shortcut.

If proven, it can become:

- assault;
- poisoning;
- fraud;
- marriage interference;
- succession interference;
- diplomatic sabotage;
- blackmail;
- grounds for annulment;
- a House scandal;
- evidence in a Dark Arts proceeding.

The fact that people still use it does not make it acceptable.

It makes it useful for fiction.

## The essential plot principle

Illicit culinary magic should create consequences beyond the duration of the spell.

A seventy-two-hour love philter is interesting not because someone behaves strangely for three days.

It is interesting because by the fourth day:

- a treaty may have been signed;
- a lover may have seen something unforgivable;
- a rival may have acquired leverage;
- a parent may have publicly courted the wrong person;
- a servant may have been blamed;
- a marriage may be in question;
- an investigator may suspect deliberate poisoning;
- the original conspirator may now be desperate to keep anyone from finding the first mixing bowl.

The magic ends.

The plot remains.

# III. Fruits of the Realm

Many fruits have clear earthlike analogues but possess locally bred magical or aesthetic traits.

## Silver Pear

Earth analogue: pear.

A pale green to silver-skinned pear with white flesh.

Flavor:
floral, crisp, lightly honeyed.

Cultural importance:
strongly associated with Lysara, diplomacy, courtship, elegant gifts, and House Sylvaren.

Uses:
- poached in wine;
- candied;
- sliced with soft cheese;
- baked into tarts;
- filled with spiced cream;
- served whole at diplomatic dinners.

Romantic grammar:
A single perfect silver pear is a restrained declaration of interest.

Two matched pears indicate mutual courtship or the hope of it.

A pear served cut cleanly in two may signal reconciliation or an invitation to speak privately.

## Embercherry

Earth analogue: cherry.

Deep red-black fruit with a faint warm spice note.

Some varieties remain slightly warm after picking.

Uses:
- pies;
- preserves;
- cordial;
- chocolate fillings;
- sauces for roast meat;
- ice cream.

Romantic grammar:
Associated with bold flirtation.

Giving embercherries out of season is expensive and unsubtle.

## Starplum

Earth analogue: plum.

Dark purple skin flecked with pale specks.

When ripe, the flesh has a faintly cooling finish.

Uses:
- jam;
- pastries;
- brandied fruit;
- sauces;
- confectionery.

Romantic grammar:
A common gift after an argument because the fruit is associated with sweetness returning after bitterness.

## Honeyapple

Earth analogue: apple.

Golden apple with unusually fragrant flesh.

Common enough for ordinary households but fine cultivars are prized.

Uses:
- cider;
- pies;
- baked apples;
- cakes;
- preserves;
- caramelized confections.

Romantic grammar:
Domestic affection rather than theatrical seduction.

A basket of honeyapples says:
I am thinking about your household.

That can be more serious than flowers.

## Glassgrape

Earth analogue: table grape.

Translucent-skinned grape in pale green, rose, and violet varieties.

Used for:
- fresh eating;
- wine;
- frozen sweets;
- syrup;
- pressed cakes.

Certain vintners produce glassgrape wines that look almost clear until exposed to moonlight.

Romantic grammar:
Shared clusters are strongly associated with southern courtship.

## Velvet Fig

Earth analogue: fig.

Deep purple-brown skin, crimson flesh.

Used with:
- cheese;
- honey;
- wine reductions;
- pastries;
- chocolate;
- cured meats.

Romantic grammar:
Adult sensuality, wealth, deliberate intimacy.

Serving fresh velvet figs at a private supper is considerably more suggestive than serving apples.

## Moonskin Melon

Earth analogue: melon.

Pale rind, cool fragrant flesh, often white-green or silver-pink.

The best varieties remain naturally cool for several hours after cutting.

Uses:
- breakfast;
- chilled soups;
- fruit plates;
- sorbet;
- parfaits.

Romantic grammar:
Summer luxury and leisurely courtship.

## Sunorange

Earth analogue: sweet orange or mandarin.

Southern citrus-like fruit.

Bright aromatic peel.

Uses:
- marmalade;
- candied peel;
- cakes;
- sauces;
- chocolate;
- mulled drinks.

Romantic grammar:
Winter wealth when served fresh in northern regions.

One fresh sunorange in Longnight may cost more than a respectable dinner.

## Bloodlime

Earth analogue: lime crossed with blood orange aesthetics.

Dark green rind, ruby-tinged juice.

Used in:
- drinks;
- fish;
- sauces;
- sherbets;
- candies.

Common in southern and river cuisine.

## Frostberry

Earth analogue: blueberry/blackcurrant family.

Small blue-black berry that tolerates late cold.

Uses:
- preserves;
- tarts;
- sauces;
- winter cordials;
- ice cream.

## Cloudberry

Essentially the familiar northern berry.

Used in:
- preserves;
- cream desserts;
- rye cakes;
- hot sauces;
- liqueur.

Strongly associated with northern hospitality.

## Glowberry

Earth analogue: gooseberry/currant.

Pale green-gold berry with a harmless faint luminescence while fresh.

The glow disappears when cooked.

Children adore them.

Confectioners use them for summer desserts.

## Heartberry

Earth analogue: strawberry.

A small intensely fragrant red berry.

Not actually heart-shaped in all cultivars despite every romantic painter insisting otherwise.

Used in:
- cream;
- cakes;
- preserves;
- pastries;
- chocolate;
- ice cream.

Romantic grammar:
Obvious affection.

The Realm has spent centuries pretending there is subtlety here.

# V. Chocolate and the Sable Bean

The Realm's chocolate analogue comes from the **sable bean**, a tropical or subtropical pod seed cultivated in the warm south and imported widely.

The roasted seed is bitter.

When ground and combined with sugar, fat, milk, nuts, spice, or fruit it becomes **sable chocolate**, usually shortened in ordinary speech to **chocolate** where context is obvious.

Chocolate culture is highly developed.

## Major styles

### Black chocolate

High cacao-content analogue.

Bitter, dark, expensive in fine grades.

Associated with:
- sophistication;
- apology;
- serious private gifts;
- adult taste.

### Milk chocolate

Softer, sweeter, widely popular.

Often flavored with:
- honey;
- moonsilk;
- hazelnut;
- caramel.

### White sable

Made primarily from the pale fat of the bean, sugar, and milk.

Used heavily in elegant confectionery.

### Moon-cracked chocolate

Fine chocolate tempered with tiny crystal sugar inclusions that catch moonlight.

Harmless.

Fashionable.

### Ember chocolate

Dark chocolate with warm spices and embercherry.

Some versions create a harmless warming sensation on the tongue.

### Frost chocolate

Chocolate with cooling mint-like herbs or a legal chill charm.

Especially popular in summer.

## Filled chocolates

Common fillings include:

- embercherry;
- starplum;
- honey caramel;
- nut paste;
- coffee cream;
- moonsilk cream;
- rose ganache;
- citrus peel;
- spiced wine jelly;
- berry cordial.

## Courtship boxes

Chocolate boxes are a developed social language.

A box of **three**:
light flirtation, thanks, or invitation.

A box of **seven**:
personal interest.

A box of **thirteen**:
serious courtship or anniversary-level affection.

A box of **twenty-six**:
extravagant and often read as either apology, declaration, or attempted recovery from a mistake of impressive dimensions.

Arrangement matters.

A competent confectioner can encode:

- favorite flavors;
- regional origin;
- shared memories;
- private jokes;
- apology;
- longing;
- invitation;
- restraint.

The recipient is expected to notice at least some of this.

Popular:
formal court gifts and theater sweets.

Thin sheets of brittle caramel or nut candy.

Fine candy wafers that dissolve almost instantly on the tongue.

Often embossed with tiny words or symbols.

Used for:
- place settings;

Soft fruit jellies made with glowberry.

## Red-thread twists

Twisted berry-and-honey candies associated with lovers' fairs.

Named after the red thread imagery surrounding binding, fate, and courtship.

## Cask caramels

Dark caramel flavored with cider, beer malt, or wine reduction.

Popular during Casktide.

## Snow cream tablets

Milk-and-sugar confections flavored with moonsilk and mint.

Northern winter specialty.

## Salted honey brittle

Common in the River Cantons and road markets.

Portable and durable.

# VIII. Creams, Custards, Ice Creams, and Frozen Desserts

Magical civilization has made frozen desserts available well beyond natural icehouses.

Urban confectioners commonly use:

- ice storage;
- salt chilling;
- frost stones;
- legally regulated chill charms;
- enchanted churn jackets.

This does not make ice cream cheap.

Milk, cream, sugar, flavorings, cold-work equipment, and skilled labor still matter.

## Frostcream

The generic high-quality ice cream analogue.

Common flavors:

- moonsilk;
- chocolate;
- heartberry;
- embercherry;
- honeyapple;
- silver pear;
- pistachio-like green nut;
- coffee;
- caramel;
- cloudberry;
- rose.

## Mooncream

Exceptionally smooth pale cream flavored with moonsilk and sometimes silver pear.

Popular at court.

## Ember-and-frost

Swirled chocolate and embercherry ice cream with a cooling herb finish.

The contrast is intentionally theatrical.

## Orchard snow

Fruit sorbet.

Common flavors:
apple, pear, plum, citrus, berry.

## River ice

Crushed flavored ice or granita analogue.

Popular in hot river cities.

## Snowmilk

Frozen milk dessert between ice cream and semifreddo.

Northern origin.

## Charmed cones

Crisp pastry cones treated with a harmless moisture-resistant charm so they remain crisp longer.

Children regard this as one of civilization's greatest achievements.

Several philosophers disagree.

They are wrong.

# XII. The Romance Grammar of Food

The Realm possesses a broad, culturally accumulated body of assumptions about romantic food.

Not all regions agree.

That disagreement is useful.

## Fruit

Fruit tends to mean:
- freshness;
- attention;
- season;
- household intimacy.

A lover who sends out-of-season fruit is displaying money.

A lover who sends the correct in-season fruit from someone's home district is displaying memory.

The second is often more effective.

## Chocolate

Chocolate tends to mean:
- deliberate pleasure;
- intimacy;
- courtship;
- apology;
- indulgence.

Cheap chocolate can be affectionate.

Expensive chocolate can be political.

The recipient must judge the context.

## Pastry

Pastry tends to mean:
- effort;
- comfort;
- mornings;
- visits;
- domestic imagination.

Someone bringing breakfast pastry is often implying a different emotional future than someone sending flowers to a ballroom.

## Ice cream

Frozen desserts suggest:
- leisure;
- summer;
- youthful pleasure;
- private conversation;
- urban sophistication.

Sharing frostcream during a promenade is a common low-stakes courtship act.

## Wine

Wine is socially flexible.

It can mean:
- hospitality;
- celebration;
- seduction;
- status;
- apology;
- negotiation.

Context determines everything.

## Tea

Tea is often safer and more intimate than wine.

Inviting someone for tea can mean:
- friendship;
- private discussion;
- cautious courtship;
- reconciliation.

## Coffee

Coffee implies:
- wakefulness;
- work;
- conspiracy;
- late conversation.

A recurring romantic trope is the person who remembers exactly how another takes their coffee before either admits the relationship has become intimate.

# XIV. Seduction Through Food

Adult seduction in Elaranth commonly relies on:

- privacy;
- remembered preference;
- controlled pacing;
- scent;
- texture;
- temperature;
- finger foods;
- shared plates;
- seasonal rarity;
- meaningful regional dishes;
- presentation;
- the invitation itself.

The most effective seductive meal is rarely the richest.

It is the one demonstrating that the host knows the guest.

Examples:

A person from the Western Marches may be more moved by properly prepared black bread and berry glaze than by an elaborate Crownheart sugar sculpture.

A River Cantons merchant may recognize an imported spice as proof that the giver went to effort.

A Vael noble may notice whether fruit was picked ripe rather than transported for appearance.

An Orthell may prefer a fireside chestnut cake after a ride to formal dining.

The social lesson is:

> **Seduction by food is about being observed accurately.**

# XV. Shared Eating Practices

## Splitting one pastry

Common among established couples or flirtatious adults.

Usually intimate without being scandalous.

## Feeding by hand

More intimate.

Context matters heavily.

At a private picnic, romantic.

At a public diplomatic dinner, potentially explosive.

## Sharing a glass

Varies by region.

Often intimate.

In some rural places entirely ordinary.

## Taking food from another person's plate

Usually indicates familiarity.

A character who does this without permission can communicate entitlement just as easily as affection.

## Saving the best piece

A common domestic signal.

Giving someone the crisp corner, sweetest berry, best slice, or last chocolate is culturally legible affection.

## Remembering dislikes

Important.

Knowing that someone hates rose cream can be more intimate than knowing what they love.

# XVII. Flowers Versus Food

Flowers are beautiful.

Food is useful.

This has created centuries of romantic argument.

Common cultural reading:

Flowers say:

I wanted your morning to be better.

I remember where you are from.

I am letting you inside.

A preserved jar says:
I expect you to still be here later.

This last meaning becomes particularly powerful in autumn.

# XXI. The Etiquette of Refusal

Food gifts create obligations only if accepted.

A recipient may refuse by:

- returning the unopened box;
- sending the gift to the household table rather than keeping it privately;
- accepting publicly rather than privately;
- sharing the gift broadly;
- returning an equivalent neutral gift.

Examples:

A thirteen-piece chocolate box kept in private quarters is meaningful.

The same box placed in the servants' common room is also meaningful.

Just differently.

# XXVIII. Culinary Knowledge as Social Intelligence

A competent diplomat, spy, courtier, servant, or lover watches food carefully.

Useful information includes:

- what someone refuses;
- what they always choose;
- what they miss from home;
- what they eat when frightened;
- what they order when celebrating;
- what they share;
- what they hoard;
- what they send anonymously;
- what their household suddenly buys in quantity.

A household purchasing:

- extra chocolate;
- rare fruit;
- fine wine;
- wedding sugar;
- festival flowers;
- imported spice;

may reveal political activity before formal announcements.

Food orders can indicate:

- engagement;
- affair;
- diplomatic dinner;
- funeral;
- reconciliation;
- secret guest;
- pregnancy celebration;
- major festival preparation.

Provisioners are therefore excellent sources of intelligence.

# XXIX. Automation Rules for Culinary Continuity

Every story run should use food as part of world continuity.

Before writing a meal, gift, market, inn, festival, date, picnic, household breakfast, or formal dinner, consider:

1. current month;
2. region;
3. settlement;
4. House sphere;
5. character class;
6. fresh versus preserved availability;
7. local Little Year customs;
8. religious observance;
9. romantic implications;
10. whether the food is ordinary, luxurious, imported, enchanted, or politically symbolic.

Do not serve rare fresh fruit in winter without explanation.

Do not give every region identical pastries.

Do not make magical food ubiquitous.

Do not use hidden attraction magic as ordinary flirtation.

When a romantic food preference becomes recurring character information, preserve it in the character continuity catalog.

When a regional dish or courtship custom becomes recurring setting information, preserve it in a durable setting file.

# XXX. Canon Principles

The enduring culinary principles are:

- Elaranth has a complete earthlike culinary civilization, not generic fantasy stew.
- Magic enhances preservation, temperature, presentation, texture, and sensory experience.
- Agriculture remains economically real.
- Food follows season, road access, wealth, and regional trade.
- Chocolate culture is mature and socially important.
- Ice cream and frozen desserts exist through both mundane and magical cold technology.
- Pastries, cakes, candies, custards, parfaits, tea, coffee, and drinking chocolate are normal parts of developed urban culture.
- Romantic food gifting possesses a sophisticated social grammar.
- A favorite food remembered correctly can be more intimate than an expensive generic gift.
- Family recipes and preserved foods imply household continuity.
- Seduction through cuisine should depend on attention, hospitality, privacy, symbolism, and taste rather than magical compulsion.
- Secret enchantment intended to manufacture desire or consent is criminal coercion, not romance.
- Illicit culinary magic nevertheless exists in abundance and is a major source of mystery, scandal, poisoning, accidental contamination, blackmail, diplomatic disaster, and romantic catastrophe.
- A potion's magical duration may be brief while the social, legal, political, and emotional consequences remain permanent.
