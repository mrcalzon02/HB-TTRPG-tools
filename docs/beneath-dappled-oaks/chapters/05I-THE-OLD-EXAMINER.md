# Chapter Five I — The Old Examiner

Damaris woke with one silver hairpin still lodged beneath her pillow and Rhyl's kiss lingering far more agreeably in memory than the headache behind her eyes.

The house had already begun without her.

Somewhere below, crockery knocked softly against a tray. A cart went past in the lane. Rainwater dripped from the eaves in the slow, irregular rhythm left behind after a wet night. She lay still for several breaths, warm beneath the coverlet, and remembered the Hall of Seven Lamps in pieces rather than sequence: Rhyl staring at her on the stairs, Thessa barefoot under the east colonnade, Ilyan standing rigid in his uncle's arms, Meris noticing absolutely everything and graciously pretending to notice only most of it.

Then she remembered the note.

Selwyn Arven.

Old Orchard.

She opened her eyes.

The silver pin stabbed her palm when she reached beneath the pillow.

"Appropriate," she muttered.

By breakfast she had changed into a plain dark walking dress, pinned her hair without assistance, and discovered that Meris had arranged the morning post beside the marmalade.

There were six invitations.

Damaris stared at them.

Meris buttered toast.

"No."

"I haven't said anything."

"You have your invitation face."

"I have an invitation face?"

"You do now."

Three envelopes bore House seals. One came from a River Ledger councillor she knew well enough to distrust socially and trust professionally. One came from an elderly widow who hosted musical evenings and collected young people with the same enthusiasm other people collected porcelain. The last was addressed in a hand Damaris did not recognize.

Meris nudged that one toward her.

"Open it."

Damaris did.

Lady Maeryn Caladren-Voss requested the pleasure of Lady Damaris Caladren-Ilyra's company at luncheon on Ledgerday. The invitation included Rhyl by name.

Damaris read it twice.

Meris bit into her toast.

"There. Consequences."

"Of dancing?"

"Of being seen enjoying dancing."

"We danced once."

"You kissed him in a room containing approximately everyone."

"Not everyone."

"An important distinction."

Damaris folded the invitation.

"Is this a marriage ambush?"

"Probably not."

"That is not reassuring."

"If Maeryn intended a marriage ambush, she would invite only you and arrange for Rhyl to arrive accidentally. Including him means she wants both of you aware that she has noticed."

"Why?"

"Ask her at luncheon."

Damaris gave her aunt a long look.

Meris drank tea.

There were mornings when affection for one's family required discipline.

"Vaun is sending a carriage at nine," Meris said.

Damaris's irritation vanished.

"Selwyn?"

"Yes."

"Rhyl?"

"Already downstairs."

That improved the morning considerably.

Meris saw that too.

Damaris refused to reward her by reacting.

---

Old Orchard had once been exactly what its name promised.

The city had swallowed most of the trees two centuries ago, but several ancient pear and apple orchards survived behind stone walls too inconveniently placed for builders to remove cheaply. Houses leaned into lanes that curved around roots older than their foundations. Moss climbed garden steps. Flowering branches reached over walls and dropped white petals into gutters.

Selwyn Arven lived in a narrow brick house beside one of the surviving orchards.

He answered the door himself.

He was old enough that Damaris first noticed the careful way he stood and only afterward the sharpness of his eyes. His white hair had been cut close. One hand rested on a polished cane. The other held the door.

Judge Vaun introduced herself.

Selwyn looked at her tribunal clasp, then at Damaris and Rhyl.

"You found Mara."

Nobody had said Mara's name.

Vaun's expression did not change.

"Why do you think so?"

"Because if you'd found the case first, you would have brought paper. If you'd found me first, you wouldn't have brought young people. Mara always remembered the young people involved in things."

Rhyl glanced at Damaris.

Selwyn noticed.

"Come in. My knees are old and your investigation is unlikely to improve them."

His sitting room smelled of apples and dust. A fire burned despite the mild morning. Shelves covered two walls, but most of the books were gardening manuals, histories and cheap adventure novels rather than professional texts.

Vaun waited until they were seated.

"Do you remember a counterfeit bonded-runner investigation involving damaged sponsor dies?"

Selwyn looked toward the fire.

"Yes."

No performance of surprise.

No miraculous recitation either.

He rubbed his thumb along the head of his cane.

"Not all of it. Enough to have disliked seeing the same trick again in the papers three years later."

Damaris leaned forward.

"Again?"

Vaun glanced at her.

Damaris sat back.

Selwyn smiled faintly.

"Mara's daughter has that habit too."

"You know Talia?"

"I knew a six-year-old who stole sealing wax and pressed flowers into it. I assume she has changed."

"Somewhat."

"Pity."

Vaun brought him back gently.

"The investigation."

Selwyn nodded.

"Credential Review started seeing impressions that were wrong in ways designed to look old. Not crude counterfeits. Someone understood how examiners think. A clean copy of a clean die is suspicious because copied lines lose character. A copy of a damaged die arrives already carrying an explanation for its imperfections."

"Mara borrowed Joren Tal's die."

"Yes."

"Why that one?"

"Because it had four cancellation cuts and a chipped lower border. Useful comparison piece. We borrowed several damaged dies from different offices."

"Did Tal's match a counterfeit?"

Selwyn frowned.

"Not exactly."

Damaris felt Rhyl's attention sharpen beside her.

Selwyn noticed that too.

"There was a sheet," he said. "Three impressions. One genuine cancelled die. One known counterfeit. One questioned credential. We were comparing defect inheritance."

"What happened to it?" Vaun asked.

"Missing when the case came back from joint review."

There it was.

Not a revelation.

A missing piece with a place where it had once been.

"Came back?" Rhyl asked.

Selwyn looked at him.

"The case file came back. The inquiry did not."

Vaun said, "Explain."

"We were told the cross-office portion had been assumed by the Joint Office for Order and Credential Integrity. We sent our working file. Several weeks later our local material was returned with instructions to close the credential-review matter as superseded."

"By whom?"

"The Joint Office."

"Signed?"

"Probably. I don't remember the name."

"Dmitrigical Review?"

Selwyn's eyes moved to Vaun.

"There was a liaison."

"Do you remember who?"

"No."

"Teren Valeric?"

Damaris watched his face.

Nothing useful happened.

Selwyn shook his head.

"Name means nothing to me."

Good.

It should have been disappointing.

Instead Damaris felt relief.

She was becoming suspicious of answers that arrived too neatly.

Vaun asked, "Why was the comparison sheet missing?"

"We asked."

"And?"

"We were told retained exhibits were outside our jurisdiction."

"Was that normal?"

Selwyn considered.

"Possible. Not normal."

"Difference?"

"Joint offices retain things. They also return things. There were rules. The trouble with joint work is that everybody knows a rule belonging to somebody else."

Rhyl's mouth moved.

Selwyn pointed his cane at him.

"You may laugh. I spent forty-two years being defeated by forms designed by people who died before I was born."

Rhyl surrendered and smiled.

Selwyn smiled back.

Damaris said, "Did you believe the case was actually solved?"

"No."

"Did Mara?"

"Absolutely not. Mara believed closure notices were insults written on expensive paper."

That sounded like Mara.

"What did you believe?"

Selwyn's amusement faded.

"That someone had found a way to move credentials through the spaces between offices. River Ledger saw runners. Customs saw seals. Bridge authorities saw passage rights. We each inspected our own little piece. Whoever made the counterfeits understood that nobody owned the whole journey."

Vaun's pencil moved.

"Did you identify a beneficiary?"

"No."

"A maker?"

"No."

"A distribution route?"

"Fragments."

"Do you still have anything?"

Selwyn looked toward the shelves.

For the first time he hesitated.

Then he stood.

Slowly.

"I kept something I should have destroyed."

Vaun did not move to help him.

Neither did Damaris.

He had not asked.

Selwyn crossed to a writing desk and unlocked the bottom drawer.

He removed a thin envelope.

"Not an exhibit," he said. "My notes. Personal notes. Which is why I was permitted to be foolish enough to keep them."

He gave the envelope to Vaun.

Inside were six small pages covered in compact handwriting.

Vaun read silently.

Damaris waited.

Rhyl waited.

Selwyn sat again.

Finally Vaun passed the first page to Damaris.

It was a list of questioned credentials.

Not names.

Routes.

A bridge crossing. A customs clearance. Two bonded deliveries. A warehouse admission. A temporary archive pass.

Beside three entries Selwyn had written the same notation.

**Damage copied after cancellation.**

Beside another:

**Issuer valid. Sponsor authority doubtful.**

And at the bottom:

**Ask Holt whether scrap custody permits comparison removal after return.**

Damaris read that line again.

"You asked Mera Holt?"

Selwyn closed his eyes.

"Yes."

"What did she say?"

"That it shouldn't."

"Shouldn't?"

"A returned comparison die was supposed to go back into its original disposal lot or be logged into retained exemplars. It was not supposed to wander."

"Did Tal's wander?"

"I don't know."

"Why write her name?"

"Because Mara's return receipt said Holt received it, and the sale weights later made no sense."

Damaris's skin prickled.

"You saw the weight discrepancy?"

"Eventually."

"Eleven ounces?"

Selwyn stared at her.

That was recognition.

"Yes."

Vaun stopped writing.

"How did you know the amount?"

"Because I requested the stores ledger after the case was closed."

"Was that authorized?"

"No."

Selwyn looked entirely unashamed.

"I was sixty-eight and angry."

Rhyl coughed into his hand.

Damaris did not look at him.

Vaun said, "What did Holt tell you?"

"Nothing useful. She was frightened."

The room changed.

Damaris kept still.

"Of you?" Vaun asked.

"No. Of the question."

"What did she say?"

Selwyn stared at the fire.

"She told me the lot had been reconciled. I said eleven ounces did not reconcile itself. She told me I was no longer assigned to the case."

"Anything else?"

A long pause.

"She asked whether Mara had kept copies."

Damaris felt the old house around them settle with a small wooden crack.

"What did you tell her?"

"That Mara kept copies of everything."

"Was that true?"

"Mostly."

"What happened then?"

"Nothing."

Selwyn's voice had gone quiet.

"Holt died the following winter. Fever. Ordinary fever, as far as I ever knew. The Joint Office dissolved some years later. I retired. Mara left Credential Review. The world found newer problems."

Vaun asked, "Why didn't you pursue it?"

Selwyn looked at her.

"With what authority?"

There was no self-pity in it.

That made the answer heavier.

"I had a closed file, a frightened stores supervisor, missing comparison paper, and a suspicion that somebody understood administrative seams better than we did. I could have made noise. Noise is not the same thing as evidence."

Vaun nodded once.

Damaris thought of all the times in the last days she had wanted suspicion to become proof simply because waiting was unbearable.

Selwyn looked at her.

"You disagree?"

"No."

"You looked as though you wanted to."

"I often do."

"Good. It keeps judges employed."

Vaun ignored that.

"May I take these notes?"

Selwyn looked at the envelope.

"Copy them here. Originals stay with me unless you issue process."

Vaun's eyebrows rose slightly.

"You remember procedure."

"I remember being young enough to annoy judges."

"You still manage."

"Practice."

For the first time, Vaun smiled.

---

They spent another hour copying Selwyn's notes.

No hidden confession emerged.

No list of conspirators waited on the final page.

There were only fragments: offices, credential numbers too old to mean anything without archive retrieval, defect comparisons, questions never answered, and the name of one clerk who had transferred away before the case closed.

It was enough.

More importantly, it was bounded.

When they left, Selwyn stood in his doorway beneath a branch heavy with white blossom.

"Lady Damaris."

She turned.

"Yes?"

"Tell Mara I was right about keeping notes."

"I suspect she'll be unbearable."

"She already was."

The door closed.

Rhyl waited until they reached the lane.

"I like him."

"You like everyone who insults bureaucracy."

"Not everyone. Some people lack technique."

Vaun handed Damaris the copied page.

"Holt is still the next custody question."

Damaris nodded.

"But she's dead."

"Records aren't."

"And the missing comparison sheet?"

"Separate question."

Rhyl looked at the line naming Holt.

"Selwyn saw the same eleven ounces eighteen years ago."

"Yes," Vaun said.

"And Holt was frightened."

"According to Selwyn's memory."

Rhyl nodded.

He did not need the rest explained.

They had learned enough from Vaun.

Damaris folded the copy.

A carriage waited at the end of the lane.

Talia sat inside.

Damaris stopped.

Talia opened the door before anyone reached it.

"Mother remembered something."

Vaun's gaze sharpened.

Talia held up a folded scrap of paper.

"Not a revelation. Before you make that face."

Damaris looked at Rhyl.

"I have a face too?"

"Several."

Talia continued before Damaris could retaliate.

"She remembered why she kept the return chit. Holt came to see her after the case closed. At home."

Vaun stepped closer.

"When?"

"Mara isn't certain. Weeks later. Maybe two months. She wrote the date down this morning after checking an old household account because she'd bought tea for the visit."

That was exactly the sort of ridiculous mundane anchor Damaris had begun to trust.

"What did Holt want?"

Talia looked at the folded paper.

"To know whether Mother still had her comparison copies."

Damaris and Rhyl looked at one another.

Selwyn had just said the same thing.

Independent memories.

Different witnesses.

Vaun held out her hand.

Talia gave her the note.

"Mother says Holt looked terrified. She wouldn't say why. She only told Mother that if anyone asked about Tal's die, Mother should show the return receipt before she said anything else."

Damaris felt cold despite the mild air.

"Did Mara ask what happened to it?"

"Of course."

"And?"

"Holt said, 'I put it where it was supposed to go.'"

Talia swallowed.

"Then she said, 'That stopped being the same thing as knowing where it was.'"

Nobody spoke for several seconds.

Vaun read the note again.

"Mara will give a statement?"

"Yes."

"Good."

Talia looked at Damaris.

There were shadows under her eyes.

"Is my mother in trouble?"

Damaris could have answered quickly.

She didn't.

"She's part of the evidence now. That isn't the same thing."

Talia studied her face.

Then nodded.

Not relieved.

But steadier.

Damaris reached into the carriage and squeezed her hand.

Talia squeezed back.

"Aereth asked for me this morning," she said.

Damaris waited.

"I went."

"How was he?"

"Embarrassed."

"That sounds promising."

Talia's mouth twitched.

"He asked whether I was leaving Reedglass."

"Are you?"

"I don't know."

There it was too.

An apology had not repaired everything.

It had merely made repair possible.

Damaris nodded.

"You don't have to decide today."

"I know."

Talia looked down at their joined hands.

"I'm learning that."

---

They returned to Reedglass after noon.

Ilyan was waiting in the library.

Not pacing.

That was new.

The certified unrestricted renewal record lay on the table before him.

Damaris stopped just inside the door.

"You have it."

"Yes."

Rhyl shut the door behind them.

Ilyan tapped the page.

"Teren renewed the restriction. We knew that. The unrestricted copy adds the requesting office."

Damaris sat.

"Which?"

"Joint Office for Order and Credential Integrity."

Silence.

Not because the name was new.

Because it had now arrived through another door.

Vaun placed Selwyn's copied notes beside Ilyan's record.

Two documents.

Different archives.

Different purposes.

The same dead office.

Rhyl leaned against the table.

"Still not authorship."

Ilyan looked at him.

"I know."

No anger this time.

Just exhaustion.

Damaris said, "Did you speak to Teren after we left?"

Ilyan looked at the window.

"About my mother."

Damaris waited.

"He asked whether I was sleeping. I told him badly. He said my mother used to sleep badly before examinations and then pretend she hadn't."

His mouth tightened.

"I had forgotten that."

Nobody turned it into evidence.

Nobody asked what Teren had meant.

Ilyan rested both hands on the table.

"I still want to ask him."

"Then ask him when you know what question you're asking," Vaun said.

Ilyan nodded.

Damaris looked at the two documents again.

The old Joint Office had touched the counterfeit credential investigation eighteen years ago.

Years later, after Silver Night, the same office's old authority still appeared in the request chain protecting Revision Seventeen's signatory.

That was a pattern.

A pattern was not yet an answer.

But it was finally something large enough that they could stop pretending each thread belonged to a different room.

Rhyl's hand settled against the back of Damaris's chair.

Warm.

Familiar already.

She leaned into it without thinking.

Ilyan noticed.

For once, he smiled.

"Maeryn invited you both to luncheon."

Damaris stared at him.

"How do you know that?"

"Meris told me."

"Traitor."

"She seemed delighted."

Rhyl said, "Should I be frightened?"

"Yes," Damaris and Ilyan said together.

That made Rhyl laugh.

Outside, the orchard trees around Reedglass moved in a soft afternoon wind.

Tomorrow there would be Holt's records.

There would be Mara's statement.

There would be an old joint office to reconstruct from the bureaucratic bones it had left behind.

And somewhere beyond all of that, an Academy delivery still waited unopened in the history they were trying to understand.

Damaris reached up and caught Rhyl's hand.

For the moment, that was enough.
