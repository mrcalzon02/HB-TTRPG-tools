# Chapter Fourteen — The Invitation That Knows Too Much

Thessa owned three dresses appropriate to supper with Lady Maevra Eris.

This was, according to Thessa, two too many.

Ilyan had been instructed to sit on the edge of her bed and keep his opinions to himself while she decided which of the three she hated least.

He had obeyed for almost four minutes.

"The green one."

Thessa looked at him through the mirror.

"I did not ask."

"I know."

"You were given one job."

"I have failed it with distinction."

She turned back to the wardrobe.

The room belonged to a young Warden officer rather than to a daughter of House Eris, which meant the wardrobe contained more uniform coats than evening clothes, two pairs of boots drying beside the window, a folded road map under a hairbrush, and a small heap of clean laundry that had achieved temporary citizenship on a chair. Bloomtide rain had returned in a thin silver mist. It stippled the glass and softened the roofs beyond it.

Thessa took out the green dress.

"Why this one?"

Ilyan considered lying.

Thessa saw him consider it.

"Careful."

"It makes your shoulders look beautiful."

"My shoulders look the same in all three."

"That is not remotely true."

She held the dress against herself and examined the mirror again.

The cloth was dark green, almost black away from the window, cut simply enough that its quality became obvious only when it moved. The neckline was modest by court standards and catastrophic by Ilyan's present standards. He tried not to stare.

Thessa caught him staring.

Her mouth curved.

"Ah."

"Don't."

"You have made an evidentiary contribution."

"I withdraw it."

"Too late."

She laid the green dress on the bed beside him and leaned down until her hair brushed his cheek.

"You know," she said, "for a man who has spent two weeks learning not to overstate evidence, you remain extremely easy to read."

"Only by you."

"That is not the defense you think it is."

She kissed him once, slowly enough to make the invitation waiting on the dressing table seem very far away.

Ilyan caught her at the waist.

Thessa let him pull her closer for a moment, then put two fingers against his mouth.

"No."

"Cruel."

"My aunt serves supper at the stated hour."

"Your family has no romance in its soul."

"My family has fortresses. We consider punctuality a romantic virtue."

She straightened and picked up the invitation.

The joke left the room without either of them asking it to.

Three nights had passed since Lady Maevra's card arrived. In that time, nothing about the card had become less interesting.

Thessa was invited because she was a Warden officer engaged in bounded Crown-Hall liaison work. Ilyan was invited as her guest. Neither fact was secret. Together they made the invitation something else.

Not necessarily a trap.

Not necessarily friendly.

A door, perhaps.

Doors were worse. Traps at least admitted what they were for.

Thessa sat beside him.

"Before we go."

"I know."

"Say it anyway."

Ilyan rested his hands on his knees.

"I am your guest. I am not attending for the Hall. I am not attending for Teren. I am not going to ask anyone at the table to disclose protected routing records."

"Good."

"If someone asks what Vaun is investigating, I can say there is a lawful historical inquiry into emergency custody and credential procedure connected to Silver Night."

"That's public enough now."

"I can say old offices are being asked for records. I can say nothing about Mara, protected destinations, route geometry, Teren's medical compartment, or the substance of Caelen's Crown information."

Thessa nodded.

"The reserve phrase?"

"We know the authority class appears in an unsealed successor registry. That is evidence we can discuss in general."

"Not how Caelen knew to ask for it."

"No."

"Corren?"

Ilyan looked at the rain.

"His lawful temporary custody is in the opened record. His claim of non-use is in the opened record. His role in Silver Night is not something I am going to minimize because he answered one notice reasonably."

Thessa's shoulder touched his.

"And if Maevra asks whether you think he used the selector?"

"I don't know."

"Good."

He looked at her.

"Your turn."

Thessa sighed.

"I can discuss my assignment as historical retrieval support. I can discuss ordinary Warden transport doctrine. I cannot disclose current patrol patterns, protected movement procedures, live wayhouse security, active reserve arrangements, or anything I saw under Warden restriction while helping Vaun."

"If your aunt asks?"

"Especially if my aunt asks."

"Does she know that?"

Thessa smiled without amusement.

"She taught me half of it."

That answer settled something in Ilyan he had not realized was tense.

He knew Lady Maevra by reputation: western defense strategist, aunt to the senior Eris heir, a woman who measured roads by what could move over them before winter. Thessa knew her as family.

Those were not the same person.

"What do you think she wants?" he asked.

Thessa leaned back on her hands.

"She wants to see me. She wants to know whether the work is good for me. She wants to know whether you are good for me. She wants to know whether the inquiry will touch western transport institutions. She wants to know whether somebody else will learn something before she does. She wants to decide whether I should be given better work."

"All at once?"

"Why waste a supper?"

Ilyan laughed.

Thessa looked at him for a moment.

"And she likes me."

That was the part he had not expected.

Not because he thought Lady Maevra incapable of affection. Because politics had trained him, very recently and very badly, to look for the second purpose before accepting the first.

Thessa saw the thought cross his face.

"You are doing it again."

"What?"

"Turning affection into camouflage because camouflage is easier for you to understand right now."

He opened his mouth.

Closed it.

"That was unpleasantly accurate."

"I learned from professionals."

She stood and lifted the green dress.

"Go wait in the other room."

"Why?"

"Because if you stay, we will be late."

Ilyan went.

---

Lady Maevra's townhouse stood in a quiet western quarter of Aurelith where the streets were broad enough for military carriages and old enough that several trees had been permitted to survive urban planning.

It was not a fortress.

It merely had the manners of one.

The entrance hall was warm, dry and uncluttered. Coats vanished efficiently. A servant took Ilyan's gloves without asking who he was because someone had already told her. The walls displayed three landscapes, one cavalry painting and a framed survey of a mountain road that Thessa stopped to inspect before they had crossed half the room.

"That retaining wall failed," she murmured.

"You can tell from the painting?"

"I can tell because Maevra has complained about it for six years."

A voice behind them said, "Seven."

Thessa turned.

Lady Maevra Eris was perhaps in her middle second century, old enough that authority had ceased to require display and young enough by elven standards to cross the room with an officer's economy. Silver had begun to thread the dark hair at her temples. Her gown was blue-grey, severe until one noticed the embroidery along the cuffs: tiny roads, bridges and mile markers worked in silver thread.

She kissed Thessa on both cheeks.

Then she held her at arm's length.

"You are thinner."

"I am not."

"Your captain is feeding you badly."

"My captain does not feed me."

"A command failure."

Thessa's face had changed.

Not softened exactly. Opened.

Ilyan had seen her with commanders, with the Seven, with him, with old Academy staff. This was another Thessa: still adult, still watchful, but willing to be someone's niece for several seconds without treating it as surrender.

Maevra looked at Ilyan.

Her eyes moved over him once.

Not rudely.

Thoroughly.

"Ilyan Valeric."

"Lady Maevra."

"You are the reason my niece has begun smiling at letters and swearing at filing systems in the same week."

Thessa closed her eyes.

"Aunt."

"I am establishing context."

"You are embarrassing me."

"That is also context."

Ilyan liked her immediately, which seemed unsafe.

Maevra offered him her hand.

"Welcome. You are here as Thessa's guest. I have been told this twice."

"By Thessa?"

"Once. The second time by my own secretary, who apparently believes I require procedural supervision in my own house."

"Do you?"

Thessa made a small sound beside him.

Maevra's eyebrows rose.

Then she laughed.

"Perhaps. Come in."

The supper was smaller than Ilyan had expected.

That made it more dangerous.

There were nine guests besides Maevra. Two were western magistrates in Aurelith for road appropriations. One was an Eris-Daeron engineer with scarred hands and a dislike of decorative bridges. Another was a grain-contract lawyer Ilyan recognized from National Council galleries. There was a retired officer of the Order of the Sundered Shield, a Crown transport clerk, a young caravan factor from Eris-Keral, and an elderly woman introduced simply as Mistress Veyra who had apparently spent sixty years auditing military road stores and regarded retirement as an administrative error.

No one at the table had been chosen accidentally.

Neither had they been chosen solely for Ilyan.

That distinction became obvious before the soup arrived.

The magistrates wanted money for two washed-out culverts. The engineer wanted them rebuilt in stone. The caravan factor wanted the road kept open during construction. Mistress Veyra wanted to know who had authorized last year's timber purchase because the price was absurd. Thessa spent fifteen minutes arguing about drainage with people who had no interest in Silver Night at all.

Ilyan listened.

He had known Thessa wanted serious Warden work. He had known she cared about roads because roads were part of defense. Knowing it as a catalog of facts had not prepared him for watching her lean over a map between courses, hair slipping over one shoulder, while she dismantled a magistrate's assumption about spring wagon loads.

"You are calculating from summer weight," she said.

The magistrate frowned. "The charter standard—"

"Assumes a dry bed. That bend floods in Leafrise. You lose the shoulder first, then every heavy wagon cuts the edge deeper."

The engineer nodded.

"She's right."

The magistrate looked betrayed.

Thessa stole a roasted carrot from Ilyan's plate without looking at him.

He wanted to kiss her.

This was inconvenient in a room containing her aunt and several people who could requisition cavalry.

Maevra noticed anyway.

Of course she did.

She said nothing.

The adults of Elaranth had apparently survived the discovery that young people desired one another.

Supper continued.

The first mention of Silver Night came with the fish.

Not from Maevra.

From the retired Sundered Shield officer.

His name was Sir Othren Vale, and age had thickened him rather than diminished him. He had the broad hands of someone who had spent decades wearing gauntlets and the patient expression of a man accustomed to letting younger officers finish being wrong.

He was discussing old convoy charters with the Crown transport clerk when he said, "Half those emergency categories were abolished after Silver Night anyway. External continuity reserve, temporary witness carriage, sealed road neutrality—every office had a different name for the same handful of people."

Ilyan's fork stopped.

Across the table, Thessa's did not.

That was why she was better at this.

The Crown clerk said, "They were not the same thing."

"They became the same thing at three in the morning."

"That does not make them legally identical."

"No, it makes them useful."

Mistress Veyra snorted.

"There is the entire history of emergency administration."

Several people laughed.

Ilyan did not look at Maevra.

He looked at Thessa.

She was cutting fish.

Only the slight pressure of her knee against his beneath the table told him she had heard exactly what he had heard.

External continuity reserve.

Not whispered in a sealed archive.

Spoken casually by a retired officer over trout.

That changed the question before anyone answered it.

The phrase was not unique enough to identify a secret actor. It belonged to a professional vocabulary wider than the Crown register where they had found it.

Ilyan waited.

Sir Othren had already moved on to a complaint about duplicated bridge escorts.

Maevra did not bring him back.

Neither did Thessa.

Ilyan ate his fish.

It was excellent.

That annoyed him too.

---

The invitation began knowing too much during dessert.

The dish was simple by noble-house standards: warm pear tart, sharp cream and a little dish of sugared nuts passed around the table. Maevra waited until the magistrates had exhausted themselves over culvert financing.

Then she said to Thessa, "Your historical retrieval assignment has caused three separate people to ask whether the Wardens are reopening protected road authorities from the Silver Night emergency."

There it was.

Thessa set down her spoon.

"We are not."

"I assumed not."

"Why?"

"Because if the Wardens were reopening protected road authorities, I would have received a different class of complaint."

The engineer smiled into her wine.

Maevra looked at Ilyan.

"The more interesting rumor says a Hall inquiry found an old paired custody mechanism, one side of which passed through a Valeric hand."

Silence did not fall across the table.

That would have been theatrical.

The Crown clerk kept eating. Mistress Veyra asked the caravan factor for the nuts. One magistrate became very interested in his tart.

But the room listened.

Ilyan felt it happen.

Thessa's knee remained against his.

"That is more specific," he said.

"Yes."

"Where did you hear it?"

Maevra smiled faintly.

"Four places, none of which possessed the whole thing."

"Which four?"

"No."

Ilyan almost laughed.

Chapter Thirteen had followed them to dinner.

Maevra saw his expression.

"You may ask what I can tell you."

"Can you tell me whether any of those sources violated protected custody?"

"Not to my knowledge."

"Did any claim to know a protected person's identity or destination?"

"No."

"Did any claim the selector was exercised?"

"No."

Maevra's eyes sharpened slightly.

He had told her something by asking.

Not the answer.

The shape of what mattered.

Ilyan knew it as soon as the words left him.

He looked at Thessa.

She knew too.

This was the cost of conversation with intelligent people. Silence could disclose. Questions could disclose. Refusing the wrong premise could disclose.

Maevra took a small bite of pear.

"You are both very young," she said, "and doing tolerably well."

Thessa's mouth tightened.

"Do not pat us on the head."

"I would not dare. You bite."

Sir Othren laughed.

Maevra continued, "The rumor exists because several ordinary traces intersect. A Hall request. A Warden retrieval assignment. Crown access questions. River Ledger movement. A Valeric notice. People who know one another notice patterns. Most of them notice the wrong pattern first."

Lyrion had said almost the same thing.

Professional restraint was not professional invisibility.

"What pattern did you notice?" Ilyan asked.

Maevra looked toward Sir Othren.

"That somebody used an old phrase without understanding how many institutions once used it."

Sir Othren wiped his mouth with his napkin.

"External continuity reserve?"

Ilyan did not answer.

The old knight shrugged.

"If that is the phrase, stop treating it as the name of an office. It wasn't."

Thessa spoke for the first time since Maevra began.

"What was it?"

Sir Othren looked at her.

There was no condescension in it.

"A capacity. Sometimes a person. Sometimes a desk. Sometimes an order holding authority in case the ordinary receiving chain failed. The exact instrument mattered."

"Who could hold it?"

"Depends on the year and the emergency. Crown transport. A chartered order. A regional continuity officer. Occasionally a neutral magistrate with the right oath. During the flood years we held it at two Shield houses because half the civil offices were underwater."

The Crown clerk said, "That was under a flood writ."

"Yes."

"Silver Night was not a flood writ."

"Also yes."

Maevra watched Ilyan instead of Sir Othren.

Ilyan kept his face still.

He was learning.

Slowly.

"If the authority was reassigned," Thessa said, "what survives?"

The clerk answered this time.

"Appointment authority. Acceptance, usually. Scope. Sometimes a receiving acknowledgment. Sometimes only an accession reference if the protected record remains sealed."

"Where?"

"Wherever the instrument says."

Thessa gave him a look.

He spread his hands.

"There was no universal archive. That is the point."

Mistress Veyra set down her fork.

"There was often a payment."

Everyone looked at her.

She seemed offended by the attention.

"People imagine secrecy abolishes horses. It does not. If you appoint an external reserve, somebody feeds the rider, houses the escort, replaces a shoe, rents a room, buys lamp oil or pays a wayhouse. The protected record may hide the person. Accounts still complain about oats."

Damaris would have kissed her.

Ilyan nearly did.

Maevra said, "This is why I invited you."

Thessa went very still.

Not because of the information.

Because Maevra had finally named purpose.

"Both of us?" Thessa asked.

"You because you are my niece, a Warden, and ambitious enough that I would prefer you learn how political rooms actually work before one eats you. Ilyan because the questions following him are beginning to touch transport institutions whether he intends them to or not."

"And because we're sleeping together?" Thessa asked.

The magistrate across from her choked on cream.

Maevra did not blink.

"That made the guest line easier."

Sir Othren looked delighted.

Thessa smiled.

Ilyan stared at his plate and accepted that death would eventually come for him.

Maevra went on.

"I do not know your protected witness. I do not want to know. I do not know whether Corren Valeric exercised a selector. I do not know who held the other half. What I know is that people in my professional world have begun asking whether an old reserve authority touched western movement systems. If you continue without understanding the vocabulary, you will mistake recognition for conspiracy and gossip for penetration."

That landed cleanly.

Ilyan had done exactly that when the phrase first appeared.

He had imagined a hidden receiving office because the registry used a title that sounded like one.

"Do you know which instrument created the Silver Night reserve?" he asked.

"No."

"Do you know who would?"

Maevra glanced at Sir Othren.

The old knight shook his head.

"Not who. Where I would start."

Ilyan waited.

"Do not ask the Shield for witness records," Sir Othren said. "If we held any, and I am not saying we did, you would get a very polite refusal and deserve it. Ask for the emergency charter cross-index for reserve appointments in Bloomtide of 264. Appointment classes are not witness identities. If a Shield house or another chartered order accepted external continuity capacity, the cross-index should identify the appointing authority and the receiving institution class even if every person and route remains sealed."

Thessa looked at Maevra.

"You could have sent that in a note."

"Yes."

"So why supper?"

Maevra's expression changed.

Only slightly.

"Because notes travel without context. Because I wanted you to hear Othren tell you the phrase was ordinary professional language before I told you it had become political gossip. Because I wanted the magistrates to see that you can sit at this table as an officer rather than as my niece. Because I wanted to meet the man you are sleeping with."

"Aunt."

"And because the tart is good."

The tart was, infuriatingly, very good.

---

After supper, the guests divided themselves by instinct.

The magistrates cornered the engineer over culverts. The Crown clerk and Sir Othren argued about whether emergency categories had ever been rational. Mistress Veyra found a pencil and began correcting the caravan factor's freight estimate on the back of a menu.

Maevra took Thessa into the small library.

She did not invite Ilyan.

That was appropriate.

It still made him curious.

He stood near the window with a glass of watered wine and watched rain silver the street lamps.

Sir Othren joined him.

"You are trying not to ask me questions."

"Is it obvious?"

"Painfully."

"I'm practicing restraint."

"How modern."

Ilyan smiled.

The old knight leaned one shoulder against the window frame.

"Your father was a bastard."

Ilyan turned.

Sir Othren looked out at the rain.

"Not in the genealogical sense."

"I understood."

"Good."

The words should have angered him.

Instead Ilyan felt tired.

"Did you know him?"

"Enough to dislike him. Not enough to explain him to his son."

"That's unusually disciplined."

"I am retired. I can afford discipline now."

Ilyan waited.

Sir Othren did not fill the silence.

That made Ilyan trust him slightly more than he wanted to.

"Did you know Teren?"

"Professionally. Better than Corren."

"And?"

"And nothing useful to your inquiry that I can give you over wine."

Ilyan exhaled through his nose.

"Everyone has learned this phrase."

"Which phrase?"

"I can't tell you."

Sir Othren laughed.

"Ah. That one."

He finished his wine.

"Ask for the cross-index. If it points at the Shield, come back with a lawful request. If it points elsewhere, leave us alone."

"Fair."

"One more thing."

Ilyan looked at him.

"Reserve authority existed because ordinary chains fail. Fire. Flood. War. Dead clerks. Compromised offices. Broken roads. Sometimes crime. Sometimes stupidity. Do not assume the reserve was dirty because Silver Night was dirty."

"And don't assume it was clean because the mechanism was lawful."

Sir Othren nodded.

"You may survive this after all."

Thessa returned before Ilyan could decide whether that was a compliment.

Her cheeks were slightly flushed.

Maevra followed at a distance.

Ilyan knew better than to ask what had been said.

He also knew Thessa well enough to see that something had.

On the carriage ride back, she lasted six minutes.

"You are not going to ask?"

"You can tell me if you want."

"That's unbearable."

"Apparently restraint is modern."

She looked at him sharply.

"Othren said that."

"I can't tell you."

Thessa hit him with one glove.

He caught it.

She laughed, then leaned against his shoulder.

The carriage rocked over wet paving stones.

For a while they listened to the wheels.

"She offered me a better assignment," Thessa said.

Ilyan turned his head.

"Because of tonight?"

"No. That's why I believe her. She said she'd been considering me for it before the Hall work. Tonight was partly to see whether I could sit in a mixed political room without either performing for the family or pretending I didn't belong to it."

"What assignment?"

"Western transport assessment. Three months. Roads, wayhouses, seasonal defense movement, civilian bottlenecks. Real work."

She sounded pleased.

And afraid of sounding too pleased.

Ilyan took her hand.

"You want it."

"Very much."

"Then take it."

Thessa was quiet.

"It will mean travel."

"Yes."

"Not every week. But enough."

"Yes."

"Ilyan."

He squeezed her fingers.

"Do not make your career smaller so I can feel secure about where you sleep."

She looked at him in the dark carriage.

"That was almost mature."

"I regret it already."

She kissed him.

Not desperately. Not as reassurance.

Warmly, with the easy familiarity they had been acquiring one ordinary evening at a time.

When she drew back, she kept her forehead against his.

"I haven't accepted yet."

"You will."

"Probably."

"Good."

"You may hate the travel."

"Probably."

"Good."

They sat together until the carriage stopped.

---

Damaris received the news about the cross-index the next morning and immediately asked the only question that mattered to her.

"Who paid?"

They were in Vaun's workroom again. Rainwater darkened the shoulders of Ilyan's coat. Thessa had gone directly to Warden duty after breakfast, leaving him with a kiss, half his toast and the information that she intended to speak to her captain about Maevra's offer.

Ilyan repeated Mistress Veyra's observation about external reserves leaving ordinary expenses.

Damaris's eyes brightened.

"That woman is a saint."

"I thought you might like her."

"Protected movement still requires mundane support. If the reserve appointment itself is sealed, the supporting account may only show an authority class, but that could corroborate timing and institutional participation."

Vaun looked at Sael.

"Two requests."

Sael nodded.

"Chartered-order emergency cross-index, Bloomtide 264. And non-identifying expenditure class for external continuity reserve support during the same interval."

"Narrower," Vaun said. "No routes. No names. No destinations."

"Yes."

Ilyan sat down.

The invitation had done what invitations did best.

It had put people in a room where they discovered that their separate knowledge touched.

No confession.

No hidden council lifting its hood.

A retired knight recognized a term. An auditor remembered that horses ate. A strategist knew which rumors had begun to move through her professional world. Thessa knew enough to ask what she could without betraying her work.

And now the inquiry had two new lawful questions.

Sael finished writing.

"Anything else from the supper?"

Ilyan thought of Maevra's four sources. Of the magistrate pretending not to listen. Of Sir Othren calling Corren a bastard and refusing to turn dislike into evidence. Of Thessa leaning over a road map, competent and alive and wanted by a future larger than Ilyan's investigation.

"People know more than we thought," he said.

Vaun looked up.

Ilyan corrected himself.

"No. That's wrong. People know more pieces than we thought."

Vaun nodded once.

That was better.

Outside, somewhere beyond the Hall windows, Aurelith continued doing what cities did with pieces.

It traded them.

It misunderstood them.

It married them to older grudges and newer ambitions.

And occasionally, if one was very lucky, it put two pieces beside each other and revealed where to look next.
