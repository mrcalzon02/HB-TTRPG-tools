# Chapter Nineteen — The Arithmetic Before the Fire

The dead had been counted many times.

Ilyan discovered this at half past second bell, in a room whose windows had been opened to a warm Bloomtide morning and whose long table was covered with documents that had never expected to become evidence of anything.

Enrollment totals. Dormitory allocations. Examination projections. Scholarship forecasts. Temple-service preferences. House affiliations where students had chosen to declare them. Order-interest surveys. Lists of students expected to require another term before graduation. Lists of students likely to finish early. A catering estimate for the graduation supper.

The last one offended him most.

"Why?" Thessa asked.

She had arrived from Warden briefing with her hair still pinned for duty and a rolled road survey tucked beneath one arm. She stood beside him, looking down at the catering sheet.

"There were supposed to be three hundred and twelve honey cakes."

"That seems reasonable."

"It is obscene."

"The cakes?"

"The precision."

Thessa looked at him for a moment, then at the page again.

"You are angry at arithmetic."

"Temporarily."

"Try not to arrest it."

He gave her a look.

She leaned down and kissed his temple.

The clerk across the table did not glance up.

That was one of the minor mercies of spending too much time in archives. Eventually everyone became furniture to everyone else.

Judge Vaun had arranged the comparison exactly as she had promised: no names from the sealed Silver Night circulation ledger, no protected student records, no invitation to infer guilt from the ordinary existence of demographic planning. The Hall had obtained six earlier graduating-cohort circulation histories and three later ones. The Academy had supplied its public administrative manuals from the same period. Damaris had provided a River Ledger planning schedule stripped of household identities. Caelen had obtained a Crown personnel-projection form old enough to be insulting. Thessa had brought the Eris-Vey household review format after her aunt approved a blank historical specimen.

The point was not to find something sinister.

The point was to learn what normal looked like before they decided anything had departed from it.

Examiner Sael sat at the far end of the table with a pencil behind one ear.

"Again," he said.

Ilyan sighed.

"Normal cohort projections were circulated to the Academy bursar, graduation office, chartered orders participating in placement, the Crown education liaison, scholarship trusts with relevant obligations, and—depending on the year—regional institutions expecting licensed graduates."

"And Houses?"

"Not the Academy projection itself. Houses could make their own estimates from their children, kin, public examination honors, social knowledge, marriage planning and whatever else noble families do when bored."

Thessa pulled out a chair.

"We are never bored. We schedule it."

Sael ignored her.

"What did the ordinary Academy projection contain?"

Ilyan looked down at the comparison sheet.

"Total graduating cohort. Broad fields of study. Expected order eligibility. Scholarship obligations. Geographic return intentions where declared. Aggregate House affiliation where disclosure was lawful. No individual marriage prospects. No fertility assumptions. No private medical information. No named succession position unless the student already held a public title that made it unavoidable."

"Good."

"You know I know this."

"I know you know it when annoyed. That is more useful."

Thessa smiled into her hand.

The clerk brought another folder.

This one had a black tie.

Vaun entered behind him.

Conversation stopped.

She placed her gloves beside the folder and sat.

"The Student Safeguard Board has approved release of the non-identifying face of the 264 cohort circulation history. Categories, dates, requesting authority classes, stated purpose, granularity supplied, and disposition. No student names. No family names below Great House aggregate. No protected medical, disciplinary or guardianship information."

Ilyan looked at the black tie.

"Was there a challenge?"

"No."

"Why black?"

"Because the clerk likes black."

The clerk, already halfway to the door, looked back.

"It hides dust."

Then he left.

Thessa waited until the door shut.

"A sound archival doctrine."

Vaun untied the folder.

The first page was dull.

Ilyan had learned to respect dull pages.

The ordinary 264 projection had gone out on the expected Bloomtide schedule, twelve weeks before graduation. Its circulation resembled the surrounding years closely enough that the comparison columns might have been copied: Academy offices, participating orders, two scholarship trusts, Crown education liaison, three regional professional bodies.

No surprise.

A second circulation occurred six weeks later after examination standings changed.

Also normal.

Then there was a third request.

Ilyan stopped.

The date sat in the left column.

Twenty-two days before Silver Night.

Requesting authority class: **Joint Office credential-integrity review liaison, certified through Dmitrigical Review coordination.**

No individual names appeared.

He felt Thessa go still beside him.

Vaun did not.

"Read the purpose."

Ilyan did.

"Future licensed-chair capacity and conflict-of-interest exposure across the graduating cohort."

Sael slid the comparison ledger toward himself.

"Does that purpose exist elsewhere?"

"Not in those words," Ilyan said.

"That wasn't my question."

Ilyan forced himself to look away from the date.

They searched.

It took twenty minutes.

The Academy had received credential-integrity requests in other years. Students moving toward regulated magical chairs could create conflicts. A future examiner might be related to a license holder. A scholarship patron might also sit on a tribunal. A cadet branch could produce several graduates entering the same regulated field. The Joint Office had lawful reasons to ask questions before appointments hardened into conflicts nobody could unwind politely.

The request itself was not extraordinary.

The timing was.

In the nine comparison years, credential-integrity review had occurred after final examination certification or after a named institution began appointment proceedings.

The 264 request came before either.

"Twenty-two days," Ilyan said.

Sael made a mark.

"One anomaly."

Thessa leaned over the table.

"What did they ask to receive?"

That was worse.

The ordinary credential-integrity requests sought aggregate counts by licensing discipline and declared institutional destination.

The 264 request asked for those, then added three supplementary categories:

**Great House affiliation by cadet or senior line where already public or voluntarily declared.**

**Known eligibility for future hereditary or chartered office, expressed as category only.**

**Projected availability for external appointment within ten years of graduation.**

No names.

No secret genealogies.

No private reproductive records.

But the grain had become finer.

Damaris arrived while they were reading the third category.

She came straight from the River Ledger, blue-grey day coat buttoned to the throat, a pencil tucked into her hair and irritation preceding her by several feet.

"I have forty minutes. A barge insurer has decided water is a surprising feature of river commerce. What happened?"

Ilyan pushed the request toward her.

She read it once.

Then again.

"This is a workforce projection."

"Partly," he said.

"No. I mean this line. Availability within ten years. River houses ask that constantly. Who will be free to manage a branch office? Who is tied to an estate? Who has a temple obligation? Who can move?"

Thessa nodded.

"Military households do the same."

Damaris tapped the cadet-line field.

"This is more political. Still not necessarily sinister. If you're screening conflicts around licensed chairs, family branch matters."

Ilyan felt the first hot certainty cooling.

He did not enjoy it.

He trusted it more for that reason.

"So what is abnormal?"

Damaris looked at the comparison years.

"You tell me."

They did.

The 264 request had been made early. It had been more granular than neighboring credential-integrity requests. It combined regulated magical capacity, family branch, future office eligibility and near-term external availability in one product.

Then Sael found the fourth difference.

"Repeated request."

Ilyan looked up.

A follow-up had been filed nine days later.

Thirteen days before Silver Night.

Same authority class.

Same stated purpose.

The follow-up did not ask for a refreshed whole projection. It asked the Academy to resolve discrepancies in three aggregate cells where students' declared destinations had changed after preliminary order interviews.

"How many people?" Ilyan asked.

Vaun's eyes moved to him.

He corrected himself.

"Can the non-identifying face tell us whether those cells represented one student or fifty?"

"No."

"Can it tell us which Houses?"

"Great House aggregate was released for the original request. The discrepancy cells are sealed because combining them with public honors rolls could narrow identity."

Ilyan sat back.

Thessa's knee touched his under the table.

Not comfort exactly.

A reminder that she was there.

Damaris read the follow-up disposition.

"Academy refused one field."

Everyone looked at her.

She pointed.

The request had asked whether any projected office-eligible students had **probable marriage alignment outside their declared House sphere**.

The Academy privacy officer had refused.

The refusal note was terse.

**Not an Academy administrative datum. Student social association does not constitute credential information. Seek lawful source elsewhere if material.**

Thessa's mouth curved without humor.

"Good for them."

"Yes," Vaun said.

Ilyan read the line again.

Someone had asked.

That mattered.

Someone had been told no.

That mattered too.

But neither fact told him why.

Damaris checked the wall clock.

"What happened after the refusal?"

Sael turned the page.

Nothing.

No appeal.

No revised request.

No threat.

No order compelling disclosure.

The circulation ledger showed the supplied projection delivered eleven days before Silver Night and acknowledged by the Joint Office registry the same day.

Ilyan's stomach tightened.

The Joint Office again.

The same institutional family that had later received Mara Venn's damaged credential case. The same broader machinery through which the black selector's custody trail had moved. Not proof that the same people touched both. Not proof that the projection caused anything.

But no longer merely two stories sharing a city.

"Who signed the request?" he asked.

Vaun closed the folder.

"That is identifying authority detail. Not part of this release."

"Can we ask?"

"Yes."

"Will we get it?"

"Possibly."

Damaris stood.

"Ask for office and delegated authority first. Not the person."

Ilyan looked at her.

"Why?"

"Because if the signature belongs to a clerk acting under standing delegation, the name tells you less than the authority. And because if the authority was borrowed, transferred or countersigned, you want that before you decide the hand holding the pen was the mind behind it."

Sael smiled faintly.

"River Ledger has not ruined you yet."

"Give it time."

She bent, kissed Ilyan's cheek, squeezed Thessa's shoulder and left at nearly the same speed she had entered.

Thessa watched the door.

"Forty minutes."

"Thirty-one," Sael said.

"She'll be pleased."

---

They did not spend the afternoon hunting the signature.

Vaun filed the request.

Sael returned to the Hall of Veils.

Ilyan went with Thessa to buy socks.

This was less ridiculous than it sounded.

The western posting required twelve pairs of field socks of a weave Thessa considered acceptable, six undershirts, rain wraps, boot grease, spare glove liners, two sets of formal Warden insignia, a travel writing case, three sealed medicine packets and a list of personal equipment whose author apparently believed western officers could not encounter civilization for three months.

"You own socks," Ilyan said.

"Not enough."

"You have an entire drawer."

"Those are city socks."

He stopped in the middle of the outfitter's shop.

"There are classes of socks?"

The shopkeeper looked offended on Thessa's behalf.

"There are now," she said.

The shop smelled of wool, leather and cedar shavings. Warden green occupied one wall in more shades than Ilyan had believed green possessed. A pair of junior officers argued quietly over waterproof map cases near the door.

Thessa held up a thick grey pair.

"These."

"They look exactly like the last ones."

"You are decorative."

"I have other uses."

Her eyes flicked over him.

"Occasionally."

He stepped closer.

"Eleven days has made you cruel."

"Ten."

He frowned.

She smiled.

"Midnight occurred."

"I dislike your calendar."

"You dislike time."

"At present."

She put the socks into his hands and went to inspect glove liners.

Ilyan watched her move down the aisle, competent and intent, comparing stitching with the seriousness she brought to roads and evidence and most things she considered worth doing properly.

Ten days.

He could spend them counting down, making every meal a rehearsal for absence, every kiss an advance memorial to a separation that had not happened yet.

He could also carry the socks.

He chose the socks.

When Thessa came back, she found him with both arms full of wool.

"You look useful."

"Do not spread it around."

She kissed him between two shelves of boot grease.

A woman choosing riding gloves nearby smiled without looking at them directly.

Young adults, Ilyan thought. Public architecture. Outfitting shops. Apparently the realm had survived worse.

---

The answer to Vaun's narrower request arrived after dusk.

Not the signature.

The authority.

The Seven were not together when it came.

Mireth was at the Green Covenant. Caelen had evening drill. Rhyl had gone beyond the north gate with a Red Hart patrol. Lyrion was at a diplomatic supper he had described as "an armed conflict conducted with fish knives." Damaris was still fighting the insurer.

Ilyan was in Thessa's rooms helping her discover that three months of travel generated an astonishing quantity of folded fabric.

Vaun sent a copy by runner.

He opened it while Thessa sat cross-legged on the floor beside an open trunk.

The released authority line was only four sentences.

The original request had been initiated through the Joint Office for Order and Credential Integrity.

Its lawful basis was a standing pre-appointment conflict review available to regulated magical institutions.

The request had not originated inside the Academy.

It had been **sponsored into the Joint Office by the Dmitrigical Review liaison desk under a temporary cross-institutional review docket**.

Ilyan read the last sentence again.

Thessa did not ask what it meant. She knew enough by now to wait until he had finished deciding what he thought it meant.

"That intersects," he said.

"Yes."

"It doesn't prove purpose."

"No."

"It means the Dmitrigical side didn't merely receive the projection after somebody else asked for it. Its liaison desk sponsored the request."

"Yes."

He sat on the edge of the bed.

The old anger came easily. Too easily.

Valeric involvement in Silver Night was already established. Dmitrigical machinery was already established. The temptation was to pull the new line tight until everything in the room pointed at the same villain.

But the document did not say Valeric.

It did not say massacre.

It did not say which family had been expanding.

It did not say whether the liaison desk wanted the information for a lawful conflict review, a political forecast, an unlawful scheme, or a lawful task later exploited by someone else.

Thessa rose from the floor and stood between his knees.

"What do you actually have?"

He rested his forehead against her sternum.

"A request twenty-two days before Silver Night. Earlier than comparable reviews. More granular than comparable reviews. Repeated nine days later. One overreaching marriage-alignment field refused. Supplied projection acknowledged eleven days before Silver Night. And the request was sponsored by the Dmitrigical Review liaison desk into the Joint Office."

Her fingers moved through his hair.

"That's enough for another question."

"Not an answer."

"You are getting better at this."

"I hate that everyone keeps saying that when I am disappointed."

"Would you prefer me to lie?"

He looked up.

"You are terrible at lying to me."

"I know."

"It's attractive."

"That is a strange defect to fetishize."

"I contain multitudes."

She laughed and bent to kiss him.

The paper remained on the bed beside them.

It did not change while they kissed.

It did not become more damning because Ilyan wanted it to. It did not become harmless because Thessa's hands were warm against his face and he would have preferred, for ten minutes, to live in a world where old records stayed old.

When they parted, she picked it up again.

"Next question?"

"Who held the temporary docket authority. What reason they recorded for opening it. What information actually went back to the liaison desk. Whether anyone outside that desk received the product."

"And the expanding family?"

Ilyan shook his head.

"Still unknown."

"Good."

"You enjoy telling me that."

"Very much."

He caught her around the waist and pulled her closer.

"Cruel woman."

"Ten days."

"Cruel and repetitive."

She kissed him again.

Later, after the trunk had been packed badly and unpacked once because Thessa refused to travel with shirts folded the way Ilyan folded them, he wrote the request in his own hand.

Not **who wanted the students counted?**

That was too broad. Everyone counted students for something.

Not **which House feared the future?**

Every House feared some version of it.

He wrote:

**Identify the delegated authority and recorded purpose for the temporary Dmitrigical cross-institutional review docket that sponsored the 264 graduating-cohort projection request; identify the exact non-protected product returned to that docket and its lawful onward recipients.**

Then he stopped.

There was one more question.

He added it beneath the first.

**Determine whether the request's unusual timing and granularity originated with the liaison desk, the Joint Office reviewer, or an upstream commissioning authority.**

Thessa read over his shoulder.

"That one."

"What?"

"That's the dangerous question."

"Why?"

"Because it lets an innocent clerk stay innocent."

He looked at her.

She shrugged.

"If someone used a lawful office to ask an unlawful question, I would rather know where the question changed."

Ilyan sanded the ink.

Outside, Bloomtide rain began softly against the window.

Somewhere across Aurelith, the JEC-R/6 receiving abstract remained sealed until morning. The commission accounts waited in another archive. The dead students remained dead. Corren's statement remained neither confession enough nor innocence enough to end anything. Teren still remembered corners instead of rooms.

And now there was this: twenty-two days before the fire, someone had asked the Academy to describe the graduating class with unusual precision; eleven days before the fire, the resulting projection had entered the same broad institutional machinery already tangled through the Silver Night inquiry.

That was not a murderer.

It was not yet a motive.

It was a route by which knowledge had moved.

For tonight, that was enough.
