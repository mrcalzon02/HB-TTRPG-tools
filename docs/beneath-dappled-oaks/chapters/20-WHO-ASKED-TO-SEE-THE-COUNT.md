# Chapter Twenty — Who Asked to See the Count

The temporary docket had a purpose so reasonable that Ilyan distrusted his disappointment in it.

He read the line twice.

Then a third time, because there were mornings when repetition was cheaper than self-deception.

**Cross-institutional conflict screening for prospective regulated chairs, emergency substitutions, and succession-contingent appointments.**

Nothing about dynastic extermination. Nothing about future heirs. Nothing about the arithmetic of which family might become too numerous, too well married, too professionally placed, or simply too difficult to exclude from the next generation of government.

The paper lay under Judge Vaun's left hand in a narrow hearing room at the Hall of Seven Lamps. Rain had washed the city before dawn and left the windows pearl-grey. Somewhere in the courtyard below, a porter was arguing with a cart wheel. The wheel had the advantage of being inanimate and therefore immune to persuasion.

Examiner Sael sat opposite Ilyan with a cup of tea he had forgotten to drink.

Damaris had arrived six minutes late, announced that six minutes was a gift wrested from commerce, and immediately stolen half of Ilyan's breakfast roll.

"Well?" she asked.

Ilyan looked again at the purpose line.

"It is lawful."

"That wasn't the question."

He gave her a tired look.

She ate the stolen half of his roll.

Vaun slid the first page toward him.

"The docket was opened twenty-six days before Silver Night. Four days before the Academy request. It was authorized for forty days and would have expired automatically if not renewed. It was not renewed."

Ilyan read the dates.

"Who opened it?"

"The answer depends on what you mean by who."

Damaris pointed at Vaun with the remains of the roll.

"There. That's why I told you not to start with the signature."

"You may be insufferable after I have eaten."

"I have improved the schedule."

Sael finally remembered his tea.

The docket had been created under an old Concord mechanism that allowed two or more regulated institutions to share conflict-screening work when several appointment systems overlapped. The Joint Office for Order and Credential Integrity administered the process. Participating bodies could submit questions through accredited liaison desks. The Joint Office could request non-protected information from schools, orders, licensing bodies, charter registries and public succession offices, then return a bounded product to the commissioning participants.

The machinery was dull.

That mattered.

It had existed before the 264 cohort. It had been used for judges whose cousins held magical monopolies, for emergency tribunal substitutes, for healers who also sat on licensing boards, for military officers inheriting chartered estates, for scholars moving between institutions whose rules did not agree on what constituted a conflict.

Ilyan traced the authorization chain with one finger.

"The temporary docket was commissioned by the Dmitrigical Review liaison desk and the Crown Appointments Conflict Secretariat."

"Yes," Vaun said.

That was new.

Not a Great House. Not the Academy. Not a mysterious committee assembled for the purpose of being sinister in retrospect.

A Crown office.

Ilyan looked at Sael.

"Did we know the Crown was in this?"

"In this docket? No. In appointment screening? Obviously."

"Caelen will love that distinction."

"Caelen is capable of surviving facts about the Crown."

"He'll complain beautifully."

"Also true."

The Crown secretariat's participation had a recorded reason. Two regulated chairs were expected to become vacant within the following year. A third could become vacant if an elderly holder retired. Several emergency substitutes were being reviewed because one of the offices could not legally remain empty during an active licensing cycle.

The docket therefore asked a practical question: who might soon be eligible to hold, substitute for, advise, inherit influence over, or become conflicted with those offices?

Ilyan felt the shape of the problem change under his hands.

Not disappear.

Change.

"Were any of the chairs Valeric?"

Vaun's expression did not move.

"One was administered through a Dmitrigical institution in which Valeric interests had representation. That is not the same as saying the chair belonged to House Valeric."

"And the other two?"

"Different institutions. One Crown-chartered. One mixed civic-magical."

Damaris had stopped eating.

"So the docket itself has a normal reason to care about future licensed capacity."

"Yes."

"And a normal reason to care about conflicts created by inheritance."

"Yes."

"And maybe cadet branch."

"Yes."

Ilyan leaned back.

The temptation to resent the record for refusing to be simple was childish. He resented it anyway.

Sael watched him.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"You're doing the thing."

"What thing?"

"Where your face attempts to prosecute a document."

Damaris laughed.

Ilyan folded his arms.

"I am thinking."

"Hostilely."

"Thinking does not require warmth."

Vaun turned the page.

"Then think about this."

The original commissioning memorandum did not mention the Academy.

It did not mention the 264 graduating cohort.

It did not ask for marriage alignment, ten-year external availability, or Great House branch detail.

Ilyan sat forward.

The docket's first request template was attached.

It contained seven fields.

Licensing discipline. Current institutional obligation. Declared prospective employer where public. Existing regulated-office conflict. Public hereditary office already held. Scholarship or service obligation. Geographic restriction affecting appointment.

That was all.

Damaris reached for the Academy request from the previous day.

She laid the two forms side by side.

The difference was immediate.

The Academy request had retained licensing discipline and institutional destination, then added cadet-or-senior Great House affiliation, future hereditary or chartered-office eligibility, and projected availability for external appointment within ten years.

The marriage-alignment question had appeared only in the follow-up.

"Not the template," Ilyan said.

"No," Vaun replied.

For the first time that morning, disappointment vanished.

In its place came something colder.

"Who changed it?"

"That is the next layer."

Damaris tapped the commissioning memorandum.

"The Crown secretariat?"

"Not according to the surviving draft history."

"The Joint Office reviewer?"

"Partly."

Ilyan looked up.

Vaun placed three sheets on the table.

They were not the original handwritten drafts. Those had not survived. The Joint Office had, however, maintained a revision register for outgoing institutional requests. It recorded which office introduced a field, which reviewer approved it, and whether the field came from a standard template, a commissioning note, or reviewer supplementation.

The first additional field—Great House branch—had been proposed by the assigned Joint Office reviewer.

Ilyan's pulse quickened.

Then he read the reason.

**Necessary to distinguish apparent kinship conflict from legally separate cadet-line appointment eligibility.**

Damaris nodded before anyone asked her.

"That makes sense."

"It does," Ilyan said.

He disliked how much sense it made.

The second field—future hereditary or chartered-office eligibility—had also been proposed by the Joint Office reviewer, after the Crown Appointments Conflict Secretariat asked whether the screening could capture foreseeable public-office conflicts not yet active.

Again, ordinary.

Again, defensible.

The third field was different.

**Projected availability for external appointment within ten years of graduation.**

Origin: **commissioning supplementation received through Dmitrigical Review liaison desk.**

Ilyan did not speak.

Damaris's eyes narrowed.

Sael put down his tea.

Vaun let them read.

The supplementation had arrived nineteen days before Silver Night, three days after the first Academy-facing request was prepared but before it was sent. The liaison desk had asked the Joint Office to add a future-availability measure because, according to the recorded note, several relevant offices had unusually long training and succession horizons.

That explanation was plausible.

The next line was not proof of anything worse.

But it was the first place the question had changed.

"Who supplied the supplementation to the liaison desk?" Ilyan asked.

Vaun looked at him.

"The released register does not say."

"Does the sealed register?"

"The register references an upstream commissioning memorandum by number. It does not reproduce it."

"Can we request it?"

"Yes."

Damaris was already reading ahead.

"The marriage field."

Ilyan followed her finger.

The follow-up request for probable marriage alignment had not come from the Joint Office reviewer.

It had not come from the Crown secretariat.

It had been submitted as a liaison-desk supplementation after the first projection was returned and reviewed.

The Joint Office reviewer had forwarded the question to the Academy with a notation that its relevance was uncertain.

The Academy had refused it.

The Joint Office had accepted the refusal without challenge.

Ilyan stared at the entry.

"So the Joint Office wasn't trying to force it."

"The surviving record does not show that," Vaun said.

"And the Crown office?"

"No recorded participation in the marriage question."

"Which leaves the liaison desk."

Sael shook his head once.

"It leaves the liaison desk as the route. Not necessarily the origin."

Ilyan closed his mouth.

Damaris glanced at Sael, then at Ilyan.

"He's right. Annoyingly."

"I know."

"You sounded as though you wanted to argue."

"I wanted to."

"Growth."

"I liked you better when you were stealing breakfast."

"I can do both."

Vaun turned another page.

The product returned to the temporary docket was less expansive than the request.

The Academy had supplied aggregate licensing discipline, declared destination, voluntarily declared Great House branch, public office eligibility category, and a bounded availability category based only on declared service obligations and known public commitments.

It had not predicted private marriage choices.

It had not supplied fertility information.

It had not identified students by name in the released projection.

The Joint Office then combined that product with public appointment data and returned a conflict-screening summary to both commissioning offices.

"Both?" Ilyan asked.

"Yes."

The Crown Appointments Conflict Secretariat received the final summary.

The Dmitrigical Review liaison desk received the same final summary.

Neither office received the Academy's underlying protected working material through this docket.

The final summary was then lawfully copied to three recipient classes: the chair-secretariat for each affected regulated office, the Joint Office retention registry, and a sealed contingency appointments packet held for emergency use.

Ilyan read the recipient line twice.

"No Valeric household."

"Not through this circulation," Vaun said.

"No Great House household at all."

"Correct."

"No Academy administrator outside the responding offices."

"Correct."

Damaris leaned back and exhaled.

"That cuts away quite a lot."

It did.

It did not cut away the Dmitrigical liaison desk.

It did not cut away the upstream memorandum that had introduced the future-availability question and later produced the marriage-alignment question.

But it cut away a version of the story in which the Joint Office itself had invented the entire demographic inquiry. It cut away a version in which the Crown secretariat had demanded marriage forecasts. It cut away a version in which the Academy had handed over a secret list of named heirs.

The truth was becoming narrower.

Ilyan had once imagined that truth became larger as one approached it.

Lately it seemed to do the opposite.

---

Caelen received the Crown's involvement badly, which was to say with perfect manners and an expression that made Mireth laugh into her wine.

They met that evening in the back room of a little eating house near the east market, one of the few places that had learned the Seven preferred doors that closed and staff who did not hover. The proprietor knew them well enough to bring two extra baskets of bread without being asked and old enough not to mistake their political anxiety for an excuse to let the stew cool.

Caelen had come directly from drill. His dark training coat was open at the throat, and a shallow scrape crossed one knuckle.

Mireth noticed it before he sat down.

"What did you hit?"

"Someone else's sword."

"With your hand?"

"Eventually."

She caught his wrist and turned the hand over.

Caelen let her.

Ilyan watched Mireth's thumb pass carefully beside the scrape. Caelen watched Mireth rather than his own hand.

Some things did not require investigation.

"The Crown office was a lawful commissioner," Ilyan said.

Caelen looked at him.

"You waited until she had hold of me."

"I am learning strategy."

Mireth did not release his wrist.

"Continue."

Ilyan explained the docket.

Not every field. Not every procedural turn. They had all learned enough in the last weeks to stop inflicting complete archival reproductions on one another over dinner.

The Crown had wanted conflict screening for prospective appointments. The ordinary template was ordinary. The Joint Office reviewer had added House-branch detail for a defensible reason and future-office eligibility after the Crown asked about foreseeable conflicts. The unusual ten-year availability field had entered through the Dmitrigical liaison desk from an upstream memorandum. The later marriage-alignment question had come through the same route. The Joint Office had forwarded it skeptically. The Academy had refused it. The Crown office had not joined that question.

Caelen listened.

"So the Crown secretariat gets to be boring."

"At present."

"How disappointing."

"I thought you might be relieved."

"I am. I can be relieved and insulted by the quality of the scandal at the same time."

Mireth finally released his hand.

"Your family will be devastated."

"We have survived worse."

Lyrion arrived as Caelen said it, kissed Thessa's cheek, stole Rhyl's chair, was made to return it, and finally sat between Damaris and Mireth with the air of a man who had spent all afternoon making other people believe compromise had been their idea.

"What did I miss?"

"The Crown is boring," Mireth said.

"Constitutional crisis."

Thessa slid a bowl toward him.

"Eat."

He obeyed.

Rhyl came in last, smelling faintly of wet leaves and horse.

Damaris looked up before anyone else did.

Her face changed in a way so small that Ilyan suspected he noticed only because he had begun paying attention to how all of them changed around particular people.

Rhyl bent to kiss her.

Not theatrically. Not furtively either.

Damaris touched two fingers to the back of his neck and kept him there a heartbeat longer than necessary.

The proprietor passed the open doorway, saw them, and continued toward the kitchen without visible surprise.

The world, again, declined to end.

Once Rhyl had food, Ilyan gave the short version a second time.

Damaris corrected one phrase.

Caelen corrected nothing, which Ilyan considered evidence of emotional maturity.

Then Mireth asked the question that had been bothering him since morning.

"Why ten years?"

Silence settled around the table.

Ilyan looked at her.

"The note says long training and succession horizons."

"I heard that. Why ten? Why not five? Why not twelve? Why not until the next appointment cycle?"

Damaris put down her spoon.

"Because somebody wanted a useful planning horizon."

"Obviously."

"No, I mean that may be all it is. Ten is common. Estate plans use it. Credit forecasts use it. Workforce plans use it."

Thessa nodded.

"Road and fortress planning too."

Mireth looked around the table.

"Then it isn't useful."

"Not by itself," Damaris said.

"But if the upstream memorandum explains why ten, that tells us what problem the person writing it believed they were solving."

Ilyan felt the question settle into place.

Not who wanted the students counted.

Why did the author of the supplementation need *that* horizon?

Caelen tore bread in half.

"Can the memorandum be opened?"

"Vaun requested it this afternoon."

"And?"

"The liaison archive says it exists. Release requires review because the memorandum identifies a commissioning authority."

Lyrion swallowed.

"There it is."

"What?" Rhyl asked.

"The first point at which a person with enough status to be embarrassed has appeared."

Damaris smiled.

"You are assuming status."

"A sealed commissioning authority in an eighteen-year-old Dmitrigical liaison file? If it turns out to be a junior clerk named Pell who was worried about scheduling, I will buy everyone dinner."

"You are already eating dinner someone else bought."

"Then I am risking very little."

Thessa's boot touched Ilyan's under the table.

He looked at her.

She was smiling, but there was fatigue under it. Warden briefing had begun before dawn. Her departure was now nine days away. Two folded route maps protruded from her satchel, and a new brass baggage token hung from one strap.

He had spent most of the day inside an old administrative chain and had nearly missed that she had acquired the token.

"When did you get that?"

She followed his gaze.

"This afternoon."

"For the western coach?"

"For the equipment wagon. I ride with the survey party."

He knew this.

He had known it for days.

Still, the small brass disk made the journey more real than the trunk had.

Thessa saw the recognition on his face.

"Don't."

"I didn't say anything."

"Your eyebrows did."

"Treacherous things."

"Nine days."

He put his hand over hers beneath the table.

"I know."

She turned her palm upward and laced their fingers together.

No speech followed.

None was needed.

Across from them, Caelen had leaned close to Mireth to hear something she was saying. Damaris was stealing mushrooms from Rhyl's bowl. Lyrion was explaining to the proprietor why the fish knives at yesterday's diplomatic supper had constituted a declaration of war.

They were becoming adults in the irritating way adulthood usually happened: not in one ceremony, but in accumulating obligations that refused to wait for one another.

---

The upstream memorandum was not released that night.

The next morning brought something almost as useful.

A registry abstract.

Judge Vaun sent it to Ilyan at the Hall of Veils, where Sael had him sorting unrelated oath disputes because apparently an investigation did not excuse him from learning his actual profession.

The abstract identified the memorandum's authority class without naming the person or officeholder.

**Commissioning source: temporary advisory authority attached to Dmitrigical succession and chair-continuity review.**

Ilyan read it twice.

Sael kept working.

"You are making the face again."

"This one deserves it."

"Perhaps."

The abstract also gave the memorandum's stated objective.

**Assess medium-term concentration risk in regulated magical offices arising from cohort-linked kinship, inheritance, appointment eligibility, and external placement.**

Ilyan stopped breathing for a moment.

Not because the sentence proved murder.

It did not.

Not because it named the expanding family.

It did not.

But the concern was no longer merely whether individual graduates might conflict with individual chairs.

**Concentration risk.**

Kinship. Inheritance. Appointment eligibility. External placement.

The political shape of a generation.

Sael came around the desk and read over his shoulder.

"That is closer."

"Yes."

"To what?"

Ilyan looked at him.

Sael waited.

He hated when Sael did that.

"To a person asking how much institutional capacity one cohort might accumulate."

"Better."

"Still not murder."

"Obviously."

"Still not a family."

"Correct."

"Still not Valeric."

"Correct."

Ilyan looked back at the abstract.

There was one final line.

The temporary advisory authority had not possessed independent power to demand Academy records. It had sent its concern to the Dmitrigical Review liaison desk. The liaison desk had translated that concern into a supplementation to the lawful Joint Office docket.

That was the seam.

An upstream advisory body had asked about concentration risk.

The liaison desk had turned that concern into a request for ten-year availability.

After receiving the first product, the same route had produced the attempted marriage-alignment question.

The Joint Office had processed the requests within its rules.

The Academy had answered what it considered lawful and refused what it did not.

No one institution in the chain needed to understand the whole political use to which the information might be put.

Ilyan sat very still.

Eighteen years earlier, a set of competent people could have performed individually defensible tasks and still built something useful to someone with a much uglier purpose.

That possibility frightened him more than discovering a room full of obvious conspirators would have.

Obvious conspirators were easy to hate.

Systems were harder.

"What now?" Sael asked.

Ilyan folded the abstract carefully.

"We ask for the memorandum."

"Yes."

"We ask who constituted the temporary advisory authority, what information it had before it asked about concentration risk, and whether its concern was general or tied to a particular House, branch, office, or projected coalition."

Sael nodded.

"And?"

Ilyan thought of the Crown secretariat, boring and lawful. The Joint Office reviewer, adding two fields for reasons that survived scrutiny. The Academy privacy officer saying no to marriage gossip. The liaison desk carrying a question from somewhere upstream and turning it into administrative language.

"We do not accuse the people who carried the question merely because they carried it."

Sael's mouth moved slightly.

"You really are getting better."

"I continue to hate hearing that."

"Good."

Ilyan reached for a clean request sheet.

Outside the high windows, Bloomtide sunlight had finally broken through the rain. The wet roofs of Aurelith shone pale gold. Somewhere west of the city, Thessa's survey route already existed whether he liked it or not. Somewhere under Commonwealth seal, the JEC-R/6 receiving abstract was moving through its own lawful review. Somewhere in an archive that had outlived the people who created it, an old memorandum waited behind a seal.

Twenty-two days before Silver Night, the Academy had been asked to describe the future capacity of its graduating class with unusual precision.

Now Ilyan knew more about how that happened.

The Joint Office had not begun with that question.

The Crown secretariat had not begun with that question.

The Academy had not begun with that question.

The question had arrived through the Dmitrigical liaison desk from a temporary advisory authority concerned with **medium-term concentration risk** in regulated magical offices.

That did not tell him who had decided the future contained too many of the wrong people.

But at last, he knew where to look for the mind that had asked.
