# Chapter Five J — Where It Was Supposed to Go

Mara Venn arrived at Reedglass carrying the green tin box under one arm and a cake under the other.

Damaris stared at the cake.

Mara stared back.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"You have the face again."

Talia, behind her mother, closed her eyes.

"Please don't encourage her."

"I brought documents for a tribunal statement," Mara said, stepping into the morning room, "and almond cake because your cook sent my daughter home with broth twice this week and I was raised properly."

Judge Vaun looked up from the table.

"Put the documents here."

"The cake?"

"Anywhere else."

Mara nodded approvingly. "A serious woman."

Talia took the cake away before the law could contaminate it.

Aereth was already seated near the window. He had dressed himself that morning and looked offended by how much satisfaction everyone had taken in the accomplishment. The hollows beneath his eyes remained, but the strange exhausted looseness Damaris had seen after the missing hour was gone.

He and Talia had spoken again before breakfast.

Damaris knew because she had passed them in the corridor, standing several feet apart and speaking quietly.

She did not know what they had said.

Talia had not told her.

That was allowed.

Mara sat opposite Vaun and unfolded her hands.

"Where do you want me to begin?"

"With what you remember. Not what Talia told you Selwyn remembered."

Mara's eyebrows rose.

Talia leaned against the mantel.

"I told her not to compare notes."

"And did you?"

"No."

Mara looked offended. "My daughter has become very authoritarian."

"You taught me."

"Poorly, apparently. I was subtler."

Vaun waited.

Mara sighed and became serious.

She gave the date from the household account. She remembered buying black tea because Mera Holt disliked the green kind. She remembered Holt arriving after dusk without a work coat and refusing wine. She remembered the question about comparison copies.

"Did she say Tal's name?"

"Yes."

"Exactly?"

Mara thought.

"She asked whether I still had the return receipt for Tal's die."

"Then?"

"I said yes. She told me to keep it."

"Why?"

"She wouldn't say."

"Did she tell you the die was missing?"

"No."

"Did she tell you it had been stolen?"

"No."

"Did she tell you anyone had ordered her to move it?"

"No."

Vaun's pencil moved.

Mara watched it.

"She was frightened."

"I'll record that as your impression."

"It was not subtle."

"Still your impression."

Mara's mouth tightened.

Then she nodded.

"Fair."

Damaris sat beside Rhyl on the other side of the table. Their knees touched beneath it. Neither moved.

Mara continued.

"She said she'd put it where it belonged. I asked where. She said the cancelled lot. Then she said that had stopped being the same thing as knowing where it was."

"Did you ask what that meant?"

"Of course."

"And?"

"She told me to forget she'd come."

Vaun looked up.

"Did you?"

Mara glanced at the green box.

"No."

Rhyl's knee pressed lightly against Damaris's.

Mara saw that too.

She said nothing.

Older adults were becoming unbearable.

Vaun finished the statement, read the relevant portion back, and let Mara correct two words. Then she asked Mara to sign.

No drama.

No hidden confession.

Just ink drying beside an eighteen-year-old memory that now had a date.

When it was done, Aereth spoke for the first time.

"Mera Holt came to Reedglass once."

Every head turned.

Aereth frowned at the attention.

"Years ago. I don't know when."

Damaris felt the room sharpen.

Vaun did not.

She merely turned a fresh page.

"Why?"

"I don't remember."

"Did you know her?"

"No."

"Then why do you remember her?"

Aereth looked irritated.

"Because Meris was angry."

Damaris looked toward the door.

Her aunt was not in the room.

"About Holt?"

"About someone sending a customs supervisor to the house without warning. I was visiting. Mera waited in the blue sitting room."

"Did you speak with her?"

"No."

"Did you see what she carried?"

Aereth rubbed his forehead.

"A document wallet. Brown. Maybe."

"Maybe?"

"It was years ago."

Vaun nodded.

"Good."

Aereth gave her a suspicious look.

"Good?"

"Good that you said maybe."

That ended it.

Damaris did not ask the obvious question.

Not yet.

Meris could answer for herself.

---

North Quay Stores had changed buildings twice since Mera Holt died.

Its records had not enjoyed the journey.

Damaris stood in a basement archive between shelves of tied bundles while a records clerk named Pel Orin explained, with the wounded dignity of a man personally betrayed by municipal architecture, that water had entered the old east storehouse during the Flood Year.

"Not enough to destroy everything," he said. "Enough to make everything unpleasant."

Rhyl lifted a warped ledger.

"Unpleasant seems generous."

"It was worse before the mold treatment."

Damaris put the ledger down.

Vaun had sent them with a written request narrow enough to prevent the archive staff from responding with six carts of irrelevant customs history. They wanted the retained-exemplar register, disposal-correction ledger and any correspondence attached to Mera Holt's stores office during the month after Tal's die returned.

Pel found the exemplar register first.

Tal's die was not in it.

That was useful mostly because it removed one possibility.

The disposal correction ledger took longer.

Rhyl found the entry.

He did not announce it.

He simply touched Damaris's sleeve and turned the book toward her.

**Lot 44-C. Returned comparison exemplar restored to cancelled disposal lot. Supervisor M. Holt.**

Below it, three days later:

**Lot reopened by external comparison warrant. One exemplar removed under receipt.**

Damaris read the line twice.

"Receipt number?"

Rhyl pointed.

There was one.

Pel disappeared upstairs to find the receipt book.

Damaris stayed very still.

External comparison warrant.

Not theft.

Not proof of wrongdoing.

A documented removal.

The kind of thing that could vanish from ordinary memory because it had been legal enough to acquire a number.

Rhyl leaned against the table.

"You hate that."

"Immensely."

"Because?"

"Because I wanted someone to have done something obviously criminal."

"Convenient."

"Yes."

He smiled.

She bumped her shoulder against his.

Pel returned with a narrow volume and dust on his sleeve.

"Found it."

The receipt number matched.

The receiving office was abbreviated.

**JOCI — retained comparative custody.**

Joint Office for Order and Credential Integrity.

Damaris felt the bottom of her stomach drop.

Rhyl did not say anything.

She was grateful.

Pel ran one finger beneath the next line.

"There's a return field."

Blank.

"Was a return required?" Damaris asked.

"For an exemplar? Yes."

"Could the office retain it?"

"With conversion paperwork."

"Is there conversion paperwork?"

Pel looked at the receipt.

Then at the ledger.

"Not attached here."

"Would it be elsewhere?"

"Possibly."

"Where?"

"If the Joint Office retained it, their property register. If they destroyed it, disposal certification. If they transferred it again, receiving receipt."

"Do those records survive?"

Pel gave her the expression of an archivist asked whether the dead had kept appointments.

"Some."

Rhyl said, "We'll take some."

Pel almost smiled.

They copied the receipt.

Then Damaris noticed the witness signature.

Not Mera Holt.

**A. Saren.**

"Who was Saren?"

Pel looked at the old staff index.

"Aric Saren. Deputy stores clerk."

"Alive?"

Pel checked another book.

"Retired. Twelve years ago."

"Address?"

"That I cannot give you."

Vaun could ask.

Good.

Another bounded step.

Damaris was beginning to understand why judges lived longer than impatient heiresses.

---

Meris was in the garden when they returned.

She had removed her shoes and was walking barefoot along the wet grass with a basket over one arm, cutting late herbs before the afternoon rain returned.

Damaris stopped at the terrace.

Rhyl stopped beside her.

"You want me to stay?"

Damaris considered.

"No."

He nodded.

No wounded pride.

No insistence.

He kissed her temple and went inside.

Meris watched him go.

"He learns quickly."

Damaris came down the steps.

"Do not."

"I said four words."

"All dangerous."

Meris clipped rosemary.

Damaris waited until they were out of earshot of the house.

"Did you know Mera Holt?"

The shears stopped.

Not dramatically.

Enough.

Meris looked at her.

"Yes."

"When?"

"A long time ago."

"Aereth remembers her waiting here."

Meris put the rosemary in the basket.

"I wondered whether he would."

"Why was she here?"

Meris's face had gone careful.

Damaris knew that face.

It did not mean Meris was lying.

It meant she was deciding how much truth belonged to whom.

Damaris disliked it more now that she could recognize it.

"Please don't make me drag this out of you."

Meris looked toward the house.

"She came because she was frightened."

"Of what?"

"She didn't know."

Damaris almost laughed.

"That is becoming a family tradition."

Meris ignored that.

"Holt had been ordered to release several cancelled dies to the Joint Office for comparison work. Proper warrants. Proper receipts. Then the office asked her to amend an old disposal reconciliation because the exemplars were no longer physically in the lot."

"Tal's?"

"I don't know which dies."

"You never asked?"

"I did. She refused to tell me."

"Why come to you?"

"Because she knew I had worked with joint offices."

There it was.

Not a revelation.

A piece of Meris's adult life Damaris had always known in outline and never thought to apply here.

"What did she want?"

"To know whether a lawful order could still be dangerous."

Damaris stared at her aunt.

"What did you tell her?"

"Of course."

The answer was immediate.

Meris looked down at the rosemary in her hand.

"Then I asked what she had done."

"And?"

"She said she had obeyed the warrants."

"Did you tell anyone?"

"She had not alleged a crime."

"She was frightened."

"Yes."

"That wasn't enough?"

Meris met her eyes.

"No."

Damaris hated the answer.

Not because it was obviously wrong.

Because she understood it.

Meris saw that.

"I told her to preserve every receipt and not alter anything else without written authority."

"Did she?"

"I don't know."

"She died that winter."

"Yes."

"Did you ever follow up?"

A pause.

"No."

That hurt more than Damaris expected.

"Why?"

Meris's expression changed.

Not defensive.

Tired.

"Because I had three active assignments, your mother was ill, Aereth was making a spectacular mess of a council dispute, and Holt had brought me a concern rather than an accusation. I intended to ask again."

"But you didn't."

"No."

Rain began softly in the leaves.

Meris did not reach for shelter.

Neither did Damaris.

"Do you regret it?"

"Yes."

No qualification.

That helped.

A little.

Damaris looked toward the windows of Reedglass.

"Tal's die was removed from the cancelled lot under a Joint Office comparison warrant. The return field is blank."

Meris closed her eyes.

Only for a moment.

"When?"

Damaris told her.

Meris opened them again.

"After Holt came here?"

"Before, if Mara's household date is right."

"Then that may be why she came."

"May."

Meris gave her a faint, unhappy smile.

"You have learned something."

"I have learned to hate that word."

"Useful words are often unpleasant."

Damaris folded her arms.

"Do you remember the Joint Office liaison?"

"No."

"Teren?"

"No."

"Selwyn?"

"No."

"Mara?"

"Not then."

Damaris believed her.

That did not make the omission disappear.

"You should have told me Holt had come here when her name first appeared."

"Yes."

Damaris had expected resistance.

The agreement left her without the anger she had prepared.

Meris set the shears in the basket.

"I did not connect her visit to your investigation until you asked. That is explanation, not excuse."

Damaris looked at her.

Meris stepped closer.

"I am sorry."

An apology.

Not repair.

Not erasure.

Something between them changed anyway.

Damaris let out a breath.

"Vaun will want your statement."

"I assumed."

"She'll be annoying."

"She is a judge."

"Professional requirement?"

"Possibly."

Damaris almost smiled.

Meris touched her cheek.

Damaris let her.

Then, because affection and anger had never respected one another's boundaries, she leaned briefly into her aunt's hand.

"Come inside," Meris said. "You're getting wet."

"So are you."

"I am older. We become waterproof."

"That explains a great deal."

They went in together.

---

Vaun read the Joint Office receipt in silence.

Ilyan stood by the library fire.

Talia sat near Aereth, not beside him.

Rhyl had found the almond cake.

Mara had apparently left half of it behind deliberately, which Damaris suspected was a maternal strategy rather than generosity.

"The die left North Quay lawfully," Ilyan said.

"Under a lawful-looking warrant," Vaun corrected.

He nodded.

Damaris sat across from him.

"The return field is blank."

"Which means?"

"That the return field is blank."

Rhyl pointed his fork at her.

"Vaun has infected you."

"Put that down."

He ate the cake from it.

Talia smiled.

Aereth noticed her smiling and then very carefully did not behave as though the smile meant anything about whether she would stay.

Damaris noticed that too.

Perhaps embarrassment could teach.

Vaun placed Selwyn's copied note beside the receipt.

The old examiner had asked Holt whether scrap custody permitted comparison removal after return.

Now they knew why the question existed.

It had happened.

The Joint Office had removed at least one exemplar from the restored disposal lot.

Tal's.

Documented.

Receipted.

Never documented as returned in the North Quay book.

"Aric Saren," Vaun said. "I'll request his retirement address."

Ilyan looked at the receipt.

"Does this change whether I speak to Teren?"

Damaris watched him.

Vaun did not answer.

Neither did Meris.

Good.

It belonged to Ilyan.

He rubbed one thumb across the edge of the certified renewal record.

"The Joint Office touched the die eighteen years ago. The Joint Office later requested the restriction renewal. Teren executed that request."

Rhyl said, "And we still don't know whether he knew anything about the die."

"I know."

Ilyan sounded irritated.

Not at Rhyl.

At reality.

Damaris understood.

"What do you want from him now?"

Ilyan looked at her.

The same question she had asked before Seven Lamps.

This time the answer came faster.

"I want to know what the Joint Office told him when they asked for the renewal."

"Then ask that."

"Tonight?"

"No."

He frowned.

"Why?"

"Because Maeryn is expecting Rhyl and me tomorrow and I refuse to be the only person in this room whose family gets to terrify them on a schedule."

Ilyan stared.

Then laughed.

So did Talia.

Even Aereth.

The tension broke just enough to breathe through.

Ilyan folded the renewal record.

"Tomorrow afternoon."

"Good."

Vaun said nothing.

Which Damaris was learning sometimes meant approval.

Outside, rain thickened against the windows.

Tomorrow would bring Maeryn's luncheon.

Then Teren.

Then Aric Saren, if he was alive and willing to remember a receipt signed eighteen years ago.

The Academy delivery remained where it had been all week: four days before Silver Night, waiting in a route ledger, important because they did not yet know why.

Damaris looked at Rhyl.

He had icing on his thumb.

She reached across the table, caught his wrist and wiped it away with her napkin.

His eyes warmed.

Meris watched from beside the fire and had the decency not to say a word.

For once.
