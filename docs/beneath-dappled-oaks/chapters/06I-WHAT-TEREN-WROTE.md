# Chapter Six I — What Teren Wrote

The order cleared at eleven minutes past noon.

Ilyan knew because he had watched the clock for the previous forty-three minutes and had begun to dislike it personally.

Examiner Sael noticed.

He said nothing.

That was worse.

Judge Vaun came out of Serrat's office carrying two sheets folded beneath her hand. Serrat followed her as far as the outer desk, where he stopped and looked at Ilyan with the expression of a man who had already decided which arguments he would not entertain.

"The tribunal presiding examiner signed," Vaun said. "The Hall restriction is satisfied for inspection under continuous custody. Nothing leaves SV-12. No rubbing, no impression work, no reagent testing, no destructive sampling."

Ilyan stood.

"And the designator key?"

"Included."

"Teren's disposition?"

"Included."

Serrat added, "If the compartment contains material outside the order, you stop."

"I know."

"Good."

Ilyan took his coat from the chair.

Thessa, who had been sitting on the window ledge with one boot braced against the wall, dropped lightly to the floor.

"That was almost affectionate."

Serrat looked at her.

"No."

"I stand corrected."

They left before she could improve the exchange.

Bloomtide had spent the morning undecided between rain and sunlight and had settled, by noon, on doing both. The paving stones outside the Hall shone black beneath a pale sky. Water dripped from the carved faces over the archive doors. Market awnings along the next street sagged under the last shower while vendors reopened trays of sugared nuts, ribbons, cheap devotional charms and early flowers.

Ilyan noticed all of it because his mind had become very good at noticing irrelevant things when the relevant thing was unbearable.

Thessa walked beside him without trying to talk him out of it.

Vaun walked ahead.

Sael remained at the Hall to preserve the restricted-copy chain.

"You don't have to come," Ilyan said.

Thessa looked at him.

"I know."

That ended the matter.

---

Master Enna Ro had prepared the vault room before they arrived.

Prepared, in this case, meant she had placed three clean cloths on a table, brought in a second lamp, and stationed two tribunal witnesses by the door. She had also arranged six sharpened pencils in a line so straight that Thessa stared at them for several seconds and then deliberately did not touch any.

Ro approved of this restraint by not acknowledging it.

The compartment-opening order passed from Vaun to Ro, from Ro to the senior witness, and back again. Seals were checked against the register. The old compartment key came from one cabinet; the second came from another. Ro used neither until both witnesses had signed the opening line.

Ilyan had spent enough of the last week learning how records survived to understand that this was not ceremony for ceremony's sake.

People forgot.

People died.

People lied.

Paper did all three less often when several irritated clerks had signed it.

SV-12 occupied the lower half of a steel-fronted cabinet set into the stone wall. Its wax seal had darkened almost brown with age. The cord beneath it was dusty but intact.

Ro showed the seal to Vaun.

"No visible breach."

Vaun examined it.

"Agreed."

The witnesses agreed separately.

Only then did Ro cut the cord.

The sound was tiny.

Ilyan hated it anyway.

The lock resisted the first key. Ro frowned, withdrew it, turned it over, and tried again.

Thessa glanced at Ilyan.

He nearly laughed.

It came out as air through his nose.

The second lock opened cleanly.

Ro pulled the compartment door outward.

Inside were two document boxes, a narrow fitted case, and a flat packet wrapped in grey archive cloth.

Nothing glowed.

Nothing whispered.

No dead conspirator had possessed the courtesy to leave a confession on top.

Ro checked the receiving list before removing anything.

"Box one. ES-17-W return set."

She placed it on the first cloth.

"Box two. W-5 file and designator key."

Second cloth.

The fitted case was Tal's die.

Ro did not open it yet.

The grey packet carried Teren's name.

Ilyan looked away from it.

"Credentials first," Vaun said.

He was grateful to her for making the choice unnecessary.

Box one contained five narrow credential folders marked W-1, W-2, W-3, W-4 and W-6. Each held the returned paper credential between protective sheets, along with the post-incident physical examination slip.

They looked ordinary.

That was becoming the most frightening property an object could possess.

Veyr's technical work had done exactly what the emergency review had asked it to do. The sponsor lineage marks were clean. The inherited defect appeared where it should. The five documents differed in handwriting, bearer description and destination authority, but the sponsor impressions shared a consistency that had bothered Pel Ardis only after the fact.

Vaun read the variation report while Ro opened box two.

"Here."

The designator key was a single folded sheet sealed inside a smaller envelope.

Ro checked the seal number.

Then she opened it.

Ilyan stood very still.

W-1 belonged to a tribunal records porter.

W-2 to a contracted locksmith.

W-3 to an Academy receiving clerk temporarily transferred from the south stores.

W-4 to a ward inspector.

W-6 to a Contingency Secretariat witness examiner.

W-5 belonged to **Mara Venn**.

Thessa's breath caught.

Ilyan read the line again.

Mara Venn.

West tower third-floor restricted receiving.

WT-3.

The credential had been issued to her four days before Silver Night and had never been returned.

The notation in the issuance book had presumed it lost with receiving staff because Mara Venn had been listed among the dead.

Except Mara Venn had not died on Silver Night.

They knew that now.

They had spoken to people who remembered her afterward.

Afraid later.

Angry earlier.

Following returned lots that came up light.

Ilyan put a hand flat on the table.

"She survived."

Vaun did not tell him to slow down.

She was already reading the W-5 file.

"Yes."

"Then the credential wasn't necessarily lost in the tower."

"No."

"She could have kept it."

"Could have."

"Or somebody could have taken it from her."

"Could have."

Thessa leaned over the table without touching the file.

"Does the file say why she needed WT-3?"

Ro turned the next leaf.

The request purpose was painfully dull.

**Receiving continuity support during restricted witness intake.**

The attached duty assignment put Mara on temporary third-floor receiving because two regular clerks had been reassigned to graduation security logistics.

Not a secret mission.

Not a conspirator's privilege.

A staffing problem.

"So she was supposed to be there," Thessa said.

"For two shifts," Vaun said.

"Including Silver Night?"

Vaun checked the dates.

"Including Silver Night."

Ilyan thought of Mara's later questions about comparison releases.

A receipt proved who had permission to take something. It did not prove where it went afterward.

He looked at the missing credential's empty return field.

"Teren knew W-5 was hers."

Vaun looked toward the grey packet.

"We are about to find out what Teren wrote."

---

The report was twenty-seven pages long.

Teren's handwriting occupied only the margins and signature lines. The body had been dictated to a tribunal clerk in the dry formal prose of an examiner who expected strangers to argue with every sentence.

Ilyan recognized his uncle anyway.

Not in the phrasing.

In the order.

Teren had always sorted a problem by removing the things that could be answered immediately. When Ilyan was twelve and had broken a practice ward in the west garden, Teren had made him reconstruct the afternoon from the moment he woke until the ward failed. Breakfast first. Then weather. Then who had entered the garden. Then which chalk Ilyan had used.

At twelve, Ilyan had considered this maddening.

At twenty-three, he read the same habit applied to six emergency credentials and wanted to be sick.

The report began with the five returned documents.

All five were physically valid under the rules then in force.

All five were issued through an authorized emergency procedure.

All five had passed sponsor-lineage inspection.

Then Teren reached the variation problem.

The inherited sponsor defect was not merely consistent across the five credentials. Its secondary characteristics were too consistent for five independently prepared emergency documents.

Teren cited Veyr's method note.

He cited CR-88.

He cited Ardis's post-incident concern.

Then he wrote the sentence Ilyan had been waiting eighteen years to read without knowing it existed.

**The documents are individually valid by enacted procedure and collectively inconsistent with independent preparation.**

Thessa whispered, "Fuck."

Ro did not look up.

Vaun continued reading.

Teren had ordered the designator key opened.

He had identified Mara as W-5.

He had checked the Academy casualty roll.

Then he had found the contradiction.

Mara Venn was recorded dead in the first west-tower casualty compilation.

Three days later, a corrected receiving-office payroll return showed her alive and absent on medical leave.

Teren attached both records.

Ilyan's fingers went cold.

"He knew she survived."

"Yes," Vaun said.

The report continued.

Teren requested Mara for interview.

The request was denied.

Not because she was dead.

Because she had been transferred into **protected witness reassignment under Contingency authority**.

The destination was withheld from him.

Teren objected.

He objected twice.

The second objection was sharper than the first.

**A review of an outstanding credential cannot be completed while the assigned bearer is unavailable by order of the same authority whose procedure is under review.**

Ilyan closed his eyes.

His uncle had seen it.

Not everything.

Enough.

"Keep reading," Vaun said.

He opened them.

Teren had then requested the original personal countersignature for the Emergency Substitution Review.

Denied: outside review scope.

He requested the request-entry clerk's desk notes.

Unavailable.

He requested the west-tower access ledger for the night of Silver Night.

Partially destroyed in the fire.

He requested the surviving upper-corridor guard sheet.

That request was granted.

The guard sheet recorded five ES-17-W designators entering the west tower that evening.

W-1.

W-2.

W-3.

W-5.

W-6.

No W-4.

W-5 entered at the third-floor receiving checkpoint forty-one minutes before the first alarm.

There was no recorded exit.

Ilyan stared at the line until the ink blurred.

Mara had been there.

Or her credential had.

He corrected himself before Vaun had to.

"W-5 was used to enter."

Vaun glanced at him.

"Yes."

"We don't know by whom."

"Correct."

Thessa's hand found his wrist under the edge of the table.

Her fingers were warm.

He did not move away.

Teren's next section concerned Tal's die.

The die had been introduced into the post-incident review because its defect geometry matched the class of sponsor-lineage irregularity Teren was examining. Teren found no evidence that the die itself had been used to manufacture the ES-17-W credentials. Instead, he treated it as a comparison object for determining whether the repeated defect could be produced without the parent authorization die.

His conclusion was careful.

**It can.**

Then, beneath it:

**Therefore possession of the parent die cannot be inferred from a credential bearing its inherited defect.**

That was CR-88 stripped of every polite administrative layer.

A lineage check could be made to recognize ancestry that had never physically touched the document.

Teren had understood.

The final section was titled **Disposition and Unresolved Matters**.

Ilyan read it once.

Then again.

Teren recommended immediate suspension of Revision Seventeen pending independent review.

He recommended locating Mara Venn and interviewing her outside Contingency supervision.

He recommended identifying the person who used W-5 at the west-tower checkpoint through surviving witness testimony.

He recommended preserving Tal's die, the returned credentials, W-5's file, CR-88 and all related request records together.

And he recommended referral to Truth Beyond the Veil because the same administrative authority appeared in both the authorization chain and the subsequent restriction of witnesses and records.

None of those recommendations had happened.

Instead, attached behind the report was a one-page disposition order.

**Review held incomplete. No adverse finding entered. Materials sealed pending security necessity determination.**

Signed by the Contingency Secretariat.

Personal countersignature redacted on the vault copy.

Below that was Teren's handwritten objection.

Only four words.

**I do not concur.**

Ilyan sat down.

He had not meant to.

The chair simply happened beneath him.

For several seconds nobody spoke.

The report did not absolve Teren.

It did something more inconvenient.

It proved that eighteen years ago Teren had identified the central procedural failure, demanded the witness, challenged the authority restricting her, recommended suspension, and objected when the review was buried.

And then, years later, he had renewed the restriction.

Both things were true.

Ilyan pressed his thumb into his palm.

"Why renew it?"

Vaun closed the report.

"Ask him."

"He told me he renewed the restriction. He didn't tell me he'd written this."

"Ask him that too."

Thessa still held his wrist.

"After we finish."

Ilyan looked at her.

She nodded toward the fitted case.

Tal's die.

He almost laughed again.

"Right."

The world remained offensively committed to having more than one problem at a time.

---

The fitted case contained the brass die.

Ro lifted it only far enough for Vaun to verify the inventory mark without disturbing the old packing beneath it.

LOT 44-C.

08-D.

The edge carried the same tiny filed notch described in the North Quay records.

Physical continuity, at last, was boring.

Ilyan found the boredom beautiful.

No hidden compartment lay beneath it. No note from Mara. No confession from his father. The die was simply where the register said it had been for sixteen years.

Vaun had Ro close the case.

Then she dictated the inspection record while both tribunal witnesses listened.

SV-12 contained what the receiving register said it contained.

Five returned credentials.

W-5 file.

Designator key.

Variation report.

Tal's die.

Teren's disposition and attachments.

No visible inventory discrepancy.

When Ro resealed the compartment, Ilyan watched the new wax cool.

"Now?" he asked.

Vaun signed the closing line.

"Now you can speak to your uncle."

---

Teren was at home.

Ilyan had expected, irrationally, that he would not be.

The house servant showed them into the back sitting room where Teren kept the windows open even in rain. The room smelled of wet leaves and tea. A half-finished letter lay on the writing desk. Teren stood when Ilyan entered.

His eyes went first to Ilyan.

Then Thessa.

Then Vaun.

He understood immediately that this was not a family visit.

"You opened it," he said.

Ilyan stopped three paces inside the room.

"Yes."

Teren looked at Vaun.

"SV-12?"

"Yes."

He sat again.

Not dramatically.

His knees seemed to give him the same practical advice Ilyan's had given earlier.

"Then you read my report."

Ilyan had imagined this conversation several times since leaving the vault.

In none of those versions had his uncle looked relieved.

Not happy.

Not frightened.

Relieved.

That made Ilyan angrier than fear would have.

"Why didn't you tell me?"

Teren looked at him for a long moment.

"Because when you first came to me, I didn't know what you had."

"You knew what *you* had."

"Eighteen years ago."

"You remembered enough to renew the restriction."

Teren flinched.

Small.

Real.

"Yes."

"Why?"

Vaun did not interrupt.

Thessa stood near the door.

Teren looked toward the rain-dark garden.

"Because Mara was alive."

Ilyan said nothing.

"The restriction order I renewed wasn't only over the technical file," Teren said. "It covered the witness reassignment appendix. I was told opening the compartment would expose her location chain."

"By whom?"

"Contingency successor counsel."

"Name."

"Serel Dain. He's dead. Twelve years."

"And you believed him?"

"I believed there was a living witness who had already disappeared once because people with authority wanted her unavailable."

Ilyan stepped closer.

"You also knew the same authority had blocked you from interviewing her."

"Yes."

"And that didn't bother you?"

Teren's expression changed.

"It terrified me."

The answer landed badly because Ilyan had wanted something easier to hate.

Teren rubbed both hands over his face.

"I objected. I referred it. I sent a sealed copy to Truth Beyond the Veil. Nothing moved. Then your father came to me."

There it was.

Ilyan felt Thessa straighten behind him.

"What did he say?"

"That I had misunderstood what the emergency credentials were protecting. That the west tower had contained material and witnesses whose exposure would have broken the Concord after Silver Night."

"Did you believe him?"

"No."

"Then what?"

Teren looked directly at him.

"Then he told me Mara was still alive and that if I kept forcing the review into open jurisdiction, people would find her."

Rain ticked against the sill.

Ilyan's mouth had gone dry.

"Did he prove it?"

"He gave me a phrase."

"What phrase?"

Teren hesitated.

"She had used it during my first attempted interview request. A private authentication line. It wasn't in the file I had."

Vaun spoke for the first time.

"What line?"

Teren looked at her.

"Ask for the blue pears before frost."

Thessa frowned.

Ilyan did not know it.

Vaun wrote it down.

"And after that?"

"I stopped trying to force Mara's location into the ordinary record. I kept objecting to closure. I kept the compartment sealed. When the successor restriction came up, I renewed it."

Ilyan's voice was quieter now.

"And you never told me any of this."

"You were five."

"I'm not five now."

"No."

Teren looked older than he had in the service-chest room.

"Now you are the one person I was most afraid would go looking for her if I told you she might still be alive."

"You don't get to decide that for me."

"No."

The immediate agreement took some of the force out of Ilyan's anger and left nowhere comfortable for it to go.

Teren continued.

"I made a choice. I thought preserving the restriction protected a witness. I also knew it protected your father from questions he deserved to answer. I told myself the first fact outweighed the second. Some years I believed that more easily than others."

Ilyan stared at him.

There was the thing he had wanted.

Not innocence.

Not confession.

A choice.

A compromised, frightened, consequential choice made by someone who had understood enough to know it was compromised.

"Did you ever speak to Mara?"

"No."

"Did you ever learn where she went?"

"No."

"Did my father?"

Teren's eyes dropped.

"I don't know."

Ilyan believed that he did not know.

He did not yet know whether he believed anything else.

Vaun closed her notebook.

"I will need a formal statement."

"I assumed so."

"Today."

Teren nodded.

Then he looked at Ilyan.

"You should be angry."

Ilyan almost laughed.

"Thank you for the permission."

Teren winced again.

Good.

Ilyan turned toward the door.

"Ilyan."

He stopped.

"There was one other thing," Teren said.

Ilyan looked back.

"When your father gave me Mara's authentication phrase, he said I should stop searching for the woman who carried W-5 and start asking why W-5 had been issued at all."

"Meaning?"

"I don't know. At the time I thought he was deflecting me. I still might."

Teren's gaze shifted to Vaun.

"But the original request named six credentials before the Academy staffing list named six temporary recipients."

Vaun went still.

"How much earlier?"

"Two days."

Ilyan understood a heartbeat later.

The procedure had not merely answered a staffing shortage.

Someone had requested six substitution credentials before the final six bearers had been selected.

That did not prove murder.

It did mean the number came first.

Vaun opened her notebook again.

"That belongs in the statement."

"Yes."

Ilyan left before his uncle could say anything else.

---

Thessa caught up with him halfway down the garden walk.

Rain had started again, soft enough that neither bothered with a hood.

"Where are you going?" she asked.

"I don't know."

"Good."

He stopped.

"Good?"

"Better than pretending you do."

Water gathered on the dark leaves above them and fell in heavy drops.

Ilyan looked back toward Teren's house.

"He protected her."

"Maybe."

"He protected my father."

"Also maybe."

"Both."

"Probably both."

Ilyan wiped rain from his face.

"I hate that."

"I know."

She stood close enough that their sleeves touched.

Not an embrace.

Not distance either.

"You don't have to decide what he is today," she said.

Ilyan looked at her.

"Sael has infected you."

"Don't say that."

He laughed then.

A real one, brief and miserable.

Thessa smiled.

"There. Better."

"No."

"Slightly less terrible."

"I'll allow it."

They started walking again.

Behind them, Vaun remained in the house taking Teren's formal statement.

Ahead waited a phrase about blue pears, a witness reassignment chain, an original request for six credentials made before six bearers had been chosen, and a father who had apparently known exactly which truth would make his brother stop digging.

Ilyan had wanted the vault to tell him whether Teren had been lying.

It had answered a harder question instead.

Teren had told him less than the truth because he believed silence protected someone.

Whether Mara Venn had ever asked for that protection was another matter entirely.
