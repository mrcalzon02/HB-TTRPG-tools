# Chapter Five C — The Dead Man's Seal

By the time Damaris and Rhyl returned upstairs, the rain had softened to a fine silver mist against the green glass.

Aereth was no longer at the window.

He sat at the writing desk with both hands around a cup he had forgotten to drink from. Talia had moved the council packet to the cleared side table under Judge Vaun's eye. Mireth stood near the washstand with the sealed tincture bottle in a padded case.

Damaris put the copied registry result in front of Vaun.

The judge read it once.

Then again.

Her finger stopped on the suspension note.

"Duplicate sponsor seal."

"This morning," Damaris said.

Vaun looked at the audit number, then at her.

"Your query is preserved?"

"Yes."

That was enough.

The judge turned to Rhyl.

"Seven deliveries?"

"Reedglass and six others."

"One Academy."

"Four days before Silver Night."

Aereth closed his eyes.

No one said what that might mean.

Vaun folded the copy and gave it to her clerk.

"Request the sponsor record. Not the route history yet."

Damaris frowned. "Why not both?"

"Because I want to know how the credential came into existence before we frighten every place it visited."

She glanced at the packet.

"And because whoever used it may still believe nobody has noticed."

That made the room quieter.

Talia looked toward the door as though the young courier might be standing outside it with another sealed packet.

Rhyl noticed.

"He's not in the house."

She gave him a look.

"You don't know that."

"No."

He did not add anything.

Talia relaxed anyway.

Mireth lifted the padded case.

"I should take this to the tribunal laboratory."

Aereth looked at the tincture.

"You trust them?"

Mireth paused.

It was a fair question now.

"I trust the method more if more than one person signs it."

Vaun nodded toward her clerk.

"He goes with you. Two samples from the bottle. One retained sealed. One tested."

Mireth's expression sharpened with interest.

"And the powder?"

"Same handling."

"Can I observe?"

"Yes."

That answer pleased her more than it should have.

Damaris knew because Mireth tried not to smile.

Caelen would have noticed too.

The thought came with such certainty that Damaris nearly laughed.

Instead she watched Mireth leave with the clerk and two evidence cases, looking very young beneath a field mantle that had already begun to acquire the permanent creases of work.

Aereth stared into his untouched tea.

"What do I do?"

Vaun looked at him.

"Today?"

"Yes."

"Eat. Sleep if you can. Do not sign anything important."

"That sounds remarkably like being declared incompetent."

"No. It sounds like being frightened and tired while an investigation is occurring around you."

Aereth's mouth tightened.

Damaris expected an argument.

He surprised her.

"All right."

Talia looked at him.

So did Meris, who had entered quietly enough that nobody but Damaris seemed to notice until she spoke.

"I will send your council office a postponement."

Aereth turned.

"Reason?"

"Family illness."

"Technically true."

"The best kind."

He almost smiled.

Meris came farther into the room.

Her gaze touched Damaris, then Rhyl, then the registry copy in Vaun's clerk's hand.

She knew.

Not the contents.

The query.

Damaris had expected the knowledge to feel like a trap closing.

Instead it felt like standing in an open doorway and waiting to see who moved first.

Meris said nothing.

Damaris loved her for that.

Which was irritating.

---

The sponsor record arrived before the laboratory result.

It came by court runner rather than River Ledger courier.

Nobody commented on the choice.

Vaun opened it in the Reedglass accounting room while Damaris, Rhyl and Meris stood around the long table.

The temporary credential for Eren Vale had been sponsored six months earlier under authorization seal **LR-18-44**.

The seal belonged to a freight assessor named Joren Tal.

Damaris read the next line.

Then read it again.

"He's dead."

Meris leaned closer.

Joren Tal had died eighteen months earlier after a winter fever.

His office had closed the credential authority attached to his name nine days after his death.

Rhyl looked at Vaun.

"But Vale's credential is six months old."

"Yes."

"So someone used a dead man's authorization."

Vaun tapped the page.

"Someone used his seal number."

Rhyl nodded.

He was learning.

Damaris turned the page.

There was a destruction receipt for Tal's physical sponsor stamp.

Date: seventeen months ago.

Witnessed by two clerks.

One name meant nothing to her.

The other did.

"Sera Pell."

Meris's eyebrows rose.

"Pell?"

"No relation to the gardener that I know of."

Damaris looked at the office designation.

Lower Reed Transfer Office, stores clerk.

Vaun took the page from her.

"Alive?"

Meris answered before Damaris could.

"Almost certainly."

"Almost?"

"I don't maintain a daily census of minor freight clerks."

"A regrettable failure."

Meris looked at her.

Vaun's face did not change.

Rhyl stared very hard at the table.

Damaris failed first.

A laugh escaped her.

Meris looked betrayed.

"You are enjoying this judge far too much."

"Immensely."

Vaun handed the sponsor record to her clerk.

"We speak to Sera Pell."

Rhyl straightened.

"Now?"

"Before the office closes."

"Do we tell her why?"

"We ask about Joren Tal's stamp."

"And if she lies?"

"Then she lies."

Rhyl waited.

Vaun looked at him.

"What?"

"I thought there would be more."

"There usually is after someone lies."

He accepted that.

Damaris reached for her coat.

Meris said, "No."

She stopped.

"Again?"

"Again."

"Why?"

Meris pointed at the registry copy.

"Because you have already put our family name into the audit trail today. Let the judge interview a freight clerk without arriving with a Caladren niece at her shoulder."

Damaris hated that the argument was good.

Rhyl saw it on her face.

"I'll go."

She looked at him.

"I know."

He came close enough that Meris's attention sharpened by a degree.

Damaris noticed.

Rhyl probably did too.

He still touched two fingers to the inside of Damaris's wrist.

Briefly.

Warm skin beneath her cuff.

"I'll come back."

The words were ordinary.

Her body treated them as something else entirely.

"You had better."

Meris waited until Rhyl and Vaun had gone.

Then she looked at Damaris.

"Oh, dear."

Damaris closed her eyes.

"Do not."

"I said two words."

"They were malicious words."

"They were experienced words."

Damaris turned toward the window.

Meris came beside her.

For several breaths they watched the rain on the canal.

"You queried the runner registry," Meris said.

There it was.

"Yes."

"And found something."

"Yes."

Meris waited.

Damaris felt the shape of the choice.

Her aunt had not ordered her to report. Had not asked what she had found. Had not reached for the family authority she could have used.

That restraint made the question harder, not easier.

"A credential problem," Damaris said.

Meris looked at her.

"How large?"

"Large enough that Vaun is following it."

"Silver Night?"

Damaris did not answer immediately.

Meris understood anyway.

"Possibly," she said.

"Possibly."

Her aunt nodded.

No demand followed.

Damaris looked at her.

"That's all?"

"Would you prefer interrogation?"

"No."

"Then stop looking disappointed."

Damaris laughed despite herself.

Meris's expression softened.

"I gave you access because I believed you could decide when to use it."

"And because you wanted to know what I would search."

"Both things can be true."

There it was.

Love and surveillance occupying the same sentence without cancelling each other.

Damaris looked back at the canal.

"I hate that answer."

"You are becoming an adult. You will hate many accurate answers."

Damaris leaned her shoulder against Meris's.

Her aunt accepted the contact without comment.

For a while they stood that way.

---

The laboratory result arrived near dusk.

Mireth brought it herself.

Her hair had escaped its pins in three places. There was a faint grey streak across one cuff where somebody had apparently introduced her to laboratory dust by force.

Damaris opened the door before the footman could.

"Well?"

Mireth handed the report to Vaun, who had returned ten minutes earlier with Rhyl and no Sera Pell.

The clerk had left work early to collect a child from a temple school.

They had an address.

They also had enough discipline not to turn collecting a child into an ambush.

Vaun read the laboratory report.

Aereth stood at the far end of the room.

He had changed clothes.

That small act of normality made Damaris's chest ache.

"The tincture," Vaun said, "contains the ingredients on its label in the expected proportions. No unexpected sedative. No mnemonic compound detected in the preliminary screen."

Aereth sat down.

Not dramatically.

His knees simply seemed to decide before the rest of him.

Damaris crossed the room.

He held up a hand.

She stopped.

"So it isn't the medicine."

Mireth answered carefully.

"The laboratory did not find an unexpected compound in that bottle."

Aereth gave her a tired look.

"You have been spending time with the judge too."

"Yes."

"Terrible."

"Apparently."

His mouth moved faintly.

Vaun turned to the second page.

The room changed before she spoke.

Damaris saw it in the judge's face.

"The grey powder contains refined mnemonic salt."

No one breathed for a moment.

Mireth looked at the floor.

Rhyl looked at Damaris.

Aereth looked at nobody.

Damaris remembered Ilyan beneath the oak.

Necessary.

The west tower.

Witness materials.

Memory salt.

She forced herself not to run ahead of what they had.

Vaun continued.

"It is compounded with ash stabilizer suitable for damp storage."

Rhyl said, "River warehouse?"

"Possibly. Cellar. Canal store. Apothecary chest. Any damp place where someone wanted the material to keep."

He nodded.

Aereth stared at his hands.

"Can that make someone remember something that didn't happen?"

Mireth looked at Vaun.

The judge answered.

"Mnemonic salt can be used in workings involving memory. The presence of it in the buried object does not tell me what working was attempted, whether it succeeded, or whether it caused what is happening to you."

Aereth's jaw tightened.

Damaris expected him to demand certainty.

He did not.

"But now there is something."

Vaun nodded.

"Yes."

That was enough to frighten him properly.

Damaris sat beside him.

This time he did not raise a hand.

Rhyl stood across the room with rain still darkening the shoulders of his coat.

She wanted him beside her too.

There was no sensible arrangement of furniture that solved that problem.

Mireth noticed.

Of course she did.

Her mouth twitched.

Damaris gave her a murderous look.

Mireth became fascinated by the laboratory report.

Vaun spread the sponsor record beside it.

A dead assessor's seal number.

A suspended courier credential.

Seven deliveries.

One to the Academy four days before Silver Night.

Mnemonic salt beneath Aereth's window.

A council packet he remembered opening twice.

Nothing in the room joined those facts for them.

They simply sat on the table.

Damaris looked at them until the urge to make them into one clean story became almost physical.

Then she remembered Aereth accusing Talia because a missing signet had demanded an explanation.

She let the story remain unfinished.

Vaun looked at the fading light.

"Tomorrow we speak to Sera Pell."

Rhyl nodded.

"And the other six deliveries?"

"Preserve them tonight. Quietly."

Damaris looked at the Academy line.

"All six?"

"All six."

"Without contacting the recipients?"

"For now."

The judge gathered the papers.

"I want the records before I want frightened people improving their memories for us."

Aereth leaned back in his chair.

"And me?"

Vaun looked at him.

"You stay here tonight."

"As a prisoner?"

"As a man whose family has excellent wine and enough spare bedrooms to survive one evening without governing the river."

Aereth considered.

"That is offensively persuasive."

"Good."

Meris called from the doorway, "The red is better than the white."

Aereth looked at her.

"Traitor."

"Family."

He laughed.

Quietly.

It was the first time Damaris had heard him laugh all day without anger inside it.

Outside, the rain finally stopped.

The canal held the last grey light between the houses.

Somewhere in Lower Reed, a freight clerk was collecting a child from school, unaware that a dead man's seal had placed her name on Judge Vaun's desk.

And somewhere else in Aurelith, six old delivery records were about to become very difficult to lose.
