# Chapter Fifteen — The Things Families Count

The first thing Thessa did after Lady Maevra offered her three months in the west was ask how many bridges had failed.

The second was to ask whether the assignment came with authority to tell local commanders when their roads were lying to them.

The third was to ask Ilyan whether he would mind.

He disliked the order only because the third question had been asked in bed, with Thessa warm against him and the rain ticking softly at the window, which gave it an unfair advantage over bridges.

"Three months," he said.

"Possibly less."

"That is not what your aunt said."

"My aunt estimates travel the way siege engineers estimate stone. She assumes someone will steal ten percent."

Ilyan smiled into her hair.

"And will they?"

"Almost certainly."

She had one bare foot hooked around his ankle beneath the blanket. Her uniform shirt hung over the back of a chair. The green dress from Maevra's supper had been folded with rather more care than either of them had shown it while removing it.

There were nights when Ilyan still woke with Silver Night in his throat. There were mornings when he could not look at his father's handwriting without feeling something cold and furious settle behind his ribs. This morning there was Thessa, the smell of rain, the faint ache in his shoulder from sleeping badly because she had stolen most of the pillow, and a professional decision that had nothing whatsoever to do with murder.

He had come to value problems that could be solved by timetables.

"You should take it," he said.

Thessa was quiet.

"You answered too quickly."

"Would you prefer a performance?"

"A small one. For dignity."

Ilyan rolled onto his back and stared at the ceiling.

"My heart is shattered. The western roads have stolen you from me. I shall stand at the city gate in black silk until the first snow."

"Better."

"I will compose bad poetry."

"Now you're being cruel."

She shifted up on one elbow.

Her hair was loose, tangled from sleep, and he had the familiar inconvenient thought that he could spend an hour looking at the line of her shoulder and still fail to learn anything useful about road appropriations.

"I mean it," she said. "Would you mind?"

"Yes."

That made her blink.

Ilyan touched the inside of her wrist.

"I would miss you. I would mind that. I would mind the distance and the letters arriving three days late and knowing some washed-out road has you riding in weather sensible people avoid. I would not mind you taking the work."

Thessa looked at him for a long moment.

Then she kissed his chest, just below the collarbone.

"Good answer."

"It was the true one."

"Those occasionally overlap."

She got out of bed before he could catch her again.

By breakfast, she had turned three months of separation into a sheet of dates.

That was how Ilyan learned that House Eris romance, when healthy, apparently involved calendars.

Bloomtide had nine days remaining. Thessa's preliminary briefing would begin in Sunsreach. The western assessment party would leave Aurelith no earlier than Sunsreach Fourthday of the first week, spend twelve to sixteen days on the south military road, cross into the Gateward uplands, inspect three Warden supply corridors, two civilian caravan alternatives and at least one bridge Maevra had described as "an insult built from timber."

"You have already decided," Ilyan said.

Thessa buttered bread.

"I decided I wanted it before I asked you."

"Good."

"That doesn't bother you?"

"No."

"Why?"

"Because you asked whether I would mind. You did not ask permission."

She smiled.

"Very good answer."

"Again?"

"Do not become vain."

A knock came at the door.

Thessa frowned.

No one who knew her well knocked that politely before the second morning bell.

She pulled on a robe and opened it.

A House Eris courier stood in the corridor with a narrow leather folio under one arm.

He bowed.

"Lady Thessa. From Lady Maevra's secretary. No reply required today."

Thessa took the folio.

"Thank you."

The courier's eyes did not move past her shoulder.

Professional excellence, Ilyan thought, sometimes consisted of knowing precisely where not to look.

When the door closed, Thessa set the folio beside the bread.

"That will be the posting packet."

"Already?"

"Maevra offered it last night. She began the paperwork three days ago."

"Of course she did."

Thessa broke the seal.

It was not the posting packet.

She read the first page, then sat down.

"What?"

"Nothing bad."

Ilyan waited.

She gave him the paper.

It was headed HOUSE ERIS-VEY — BLOOMTIDE HOUSEHOLD REVIEW.

He stopped after the first paragraph.

"Is this normal?"

"Painfully."

"It has my name in it."

"Yes."

"Why does it have my name in it?"

"Because you have been kissing me where people can see."

"I thought your family was civilized."

"We are. That is why the document is boring."

It was, in fact, extremely boring.

The first page concerned Thessa's projected service income if she accepted the western posting. The second listed current Eris-Vey property obligations that might eventually pass through her generation. The third recorded which cousins presently had acknowledged heirs, which marriages had produced children, which cadet lines were likely to divide estates, and which inheritance contingencies would become relevant if particular elderly relatives died before particular younger ones married.

No one was being ordered to marry anyone.

No one was being threatened.

There were no secret lovers marked in red ink or ominous instructions concerning bloodlines.

There were columns.

That was somehow worse.

Ilyan found Thessa's line.

THESSA ERIS-VEY — adult, Warden service, unmarried, no acknowledged children, no current succession compact, current intimate association: Ilyan Valeric, adult, Truth Beyond the Veil affiliation, Valeric line.

Below that, in smaller writing:

No recommendation. Monitor only for material change affecting household succession, residence, service obligations or dependent issue.

Ilyan read it twice.

"Dependent issue?"

Thessa drank tea.

"Children."

"I know what the words mean."

"You asked."

"I object to being grammatically converted into a possible source of dependent issue before breakfast."

"You should see what they call horses."

He looked at her.

She was amused, but not entirely.

"Does this bother you?"

"The existence of it? No. The fact that Maevra sent it this morning? A little."

"Why?"

"Because she knew exactly what we were going to discuss last night. She also knew this would make the point better than she could."

Ilyan looked down again.

There were no moral judgments in the document. A cousin with a male husband had a donor compact noted in one column. An elderly aunt's acknowledged daughter by a long-term companion had been assigned to a cadet succession line. Another branch had three adult children and no grandchildren. One marriage had produced two heirs in six years; another had produced none in seventy and had apparently arranged a surrogate agreement without scandal.

The arithmetic was not about purity.

It was about continuity.

Who would exist in twenty years. Who would be old enough to hold office in forty. Which estates would split. Which names would acquire voting weight in regional councils. Which cadet branches might need patronage. Which households would have spare adults available for military, temple, guild or Crown service instead of needing every child at home.

Thessa tapped one line with her fingernail.

"My cousin Sareth's household has four children under thirty."

"Is that unusual?"

"Not biologically. Politically, in our branch, yes."

"Because?"

"Because there are enough of them that nobody has to make the same child be mine heir, estate manager, military officer and marriage piece."

She turned the page.

"Look here."

A small projection table showed the Eris-Vey branch under three assumptions: current births only; expected acknowledged household additions; and a broader estimate including pending marriage and donor agreements.

The numbers were not large.

That made them feel more real.

Two additional adults of the right age in a generation could determine whether a House branch filled a regional seat from within its own family or depended on an allied cadet line. Three could mean a spare officer for a fortress command. One magically gifted child could alter which branch expected to nominate a future chair in a licensed order.

Ilyan put down the page.

"They count us before we exist."

Thessa's expression changed.

"Yes."

He heard himself and corrected it.

"No. That's not fair. They estimate."

"Also yes."

"And most of the time it's ordinary."

"Most of the time it is how you avoid discovering at age two hundred and forty that your entire estate depends on one exhausted niece who hates you."

Ilyan laughed despite himself.

Thessa folded her hands around the tea cup.

"You are thinking about Silver Night."

"I am trying not to make this guilty because something else was guilty."

"Good."

He looked at the columns again.

Eighteen years earlier, the dead students of Concord Academy had not yet entered adult House politics. That had been the point of the shield around them. They were not supposed to be courted as voting blocs, deployed as officers, traded through marriage negotiations or counted as instruments in the adult contest between Houses.

But someone could still have counted what they would become.

Not names on a murder list.

A cohort.

A future distribution of adults.

A House with too many promising children in the same generation would not merely have more heirs. It would have more cousins available to marry outward without hollowing itself out. More candidates for orders. More people eligible for magistracies. More bodies to place in commercial councils, temple offices, military commissions and magical institutions. More chances that one of them would prove brilliant.

A generation did not have to be enormous to bend politics.

It only had to be larger than expected in the right places.

Thessa watched him arrive there.

"Don't decide which family," she said.

"I wasn't."

"You were about to."

"I was about to make a list."

"That is how you decide while pretending you haven't."

He gave her an offended look.

She took his bread.

"You have habits."

"So do you."

"Mine are attractive."

"Infuriatingly."

She leaned across the table and kissed him.

It was brief, warm and completely unhelpful to concentration.

When she sat back, Ilyan looked again at the line bearing both their names.

"Do you think they have projected our children?"

Thessa nearly inhaled her tea.

He had never seen a Warden officer lose a tactical engagement so completely.

"Ilyan."

"You said the document is normal."

"The document is normal."

"That was not my question."

Her ears had gone pink.

He loved that he knew what that meant now.

"If they have," she said with dignity, "they have not sent me that page."

"Cowards."

"I will throw you out."

"Without trousers?"

"It would improve the lesson."

They were still laughing when another knock came at the door.

This one was not polite.

Three hard taps, pause, two more.

Thessa's face changed immediately.

"Vaun."

She opened the door.

The messenger wore the plain grey of the Hall.

He handed Ilyan a sealed note and left.

The note contained six words.

**Come when convenient. Bring no records.**

Beneath them, in Judge Vaun's hand:

**This is not urgent. Eat first.**

Thessa read over his shoulder.

"I like her."

"Everyone likes her until she starts asking questions."

"You like her while she's asking questions."

"That is because something is wrong with me."

They finished breakfast.

---

Judge Lethra Vaun had acquired a second table.

Ilyan stopped in the doorway of her workroom.

"Should I be concerned?"

"About the table?"

"You only had one yesterday."

"The other one became inadequate."

"That sounds like concern."

Vaun pointed at a chair.

Examiner Sael sat near the window with a stack of thin folders tied in five different colors. None bore names on their covers. A clerk Ilyan did not know was copying references onto a clean sheet.

No one appeared alarmed.

That helped.

Vaun waited until Ilyan sat.

"Your non-identifying requests from last night have begun returning."

"Already?"

"You asked for indexes, not secrets. Indexes are easier."

Sael lifted one of the folders.

"And less satisfying."

"Usually a sign they're useful," Vaun said.

Ilyan glanced at the colored ties.

"What did we get?"

"Not the reserve holder."

He nodded.

Vaun watched him for disappointment.

He had learned enough not to provide it theatrically.

"We have the Bloomtide 264 chartered-order emergency cross-index. It confirms that external continuity reserve authority could be vested under four classes relevant to Aurelith at the time. Crown transport continuity, chartered protective order, regional civil continuity, or jointly commissioned receiving authority."

"That matches Sir Othren."

"Broadly."

Sael slid a page across the second table.

Four headings. No names.

"Three classes can be excluded for the west-tower interval," Sael said.

Ilyan looked up.

"How?"

"Not because they were innocent. Because they were busy elsewhere. Crown transport continuity was activated for the south hospital evacuation. Regional civil continuity was carrying flood-displaced records from the lower archive. The relevant chartered protective order had its Aurelith reserve authority suspended for two days after an unrelated oath dispute."

"Which leaves jointly commissioned receiving authority."

"As the only class not presently excluded," Vaun said. "Not yet as the proven holder."

Ilyan read the page again.

"What commissioned it?"

"That is the next question."

"And the expenses?"

Sael's expression became almost cheerful.

"Boring."

"Excellent."

"Three emergency wayhouse charges. Two horse relays. One room charged under sealed civic necessity. Lamp oil. Feed. A replacement cloak."

"A cloak?"

"Apparently secrecy gets wet."

Vaun gave Sael a look.

"Sorry."

Ilyan leaned over the expense abstract.

The charges did not identify a traveler. They did not identify a destination. They did not say that the black selector had been exercised. They established only that during the relevant period, somebody operating under a sealed jointly commissioned continuity authority had moved through ordinary logistical systems.

"Can we compare timing?"

"Within a day," Vaun said. "Not within an hour. The old accounts were posted by service date, not movement time."

"Still useful."

"Yes."

She pushed another sheet toward him.

"And this is why I asked you here without records."

It was not an emergency register.

It was an Academy planning index.

Ilyan stared at the heading.

**CONCORD ACADEMY — GRADUATING COHORT PROJECTION SERIES, 250–270 S.M.**

He felt the morning return to him: Thessa's tea, the Eris-Vey columns, his own name beside hers.

"Where did this come from?"

"Public institutional archives," Vaun said. "Mostly."

"Why were you looking for it?"

"We weren't."

The clerk at the second table turned a page.

Sael answered.

"The expenditure search touched an old budget classification for academy-transition escorts. That index sits beside annual cohort projections because transport, induction and placement estimates were funded together."

Ilyan did not touch the sheet.

"What did they project?"

"Graduation numbers. Likely order placements. Broad House affiliation where disclosed. Scholarship obligations. Expected Crown or civic service. Nothing sinister."

"Marriage?"

"Not in the Academy series."

Vaun's mouth tightened slightly.

"Other people kept those numbers."

Ilyan looked at her.

"Who?"

"Everyone with estates."

That answer was almost funny because it was so obvious.

Households projected heirs. Orders projected recruits. The Crown projected officers. Councils projected eligible magistrates. Banks projected estates. Temples projected vows. The Academy projected graduates.

Nobody needed a secret conspiracy to count the future.

They needed paper.

"The series itself proves nothing," Vaun said.

"I know."

"Say what it gives us."

Ilyan studied the index.

"A bounded record category. We can compare ordinary pre-Silver-Night cohort projections against whatever later planning intersected the Dmitrigical review, emergency credentials or House succession work."

"Better."

"And if somebody requested unusually granular data about one graduating cohort—"

"Then we ask why," Vaun said. "We do not call it murder."

"Yes."

Sael untied another folder.

"There is one more thing."

Ilyan waited.

"The Academy projection series has circulation records. Most years are dull. Education office. Crown placement. Three orders. Two scholarship trusts. Sometimes House offices when they were sponsoring students."

"And Silver Night's year?"

"We have not opened the circulation ledger yet."

Ilyan looked at Vaun.

She looked back.

"Why not?"

"Because it is sealed with student-protection material from the old regime of records."

He sat very still.

There it was again.

The shield.

Eighteen years after the students died, the paper around them still remembered that adults were not supposed to reach casually into their futures.

"Can it be opened?"

"Lawfully, yes. Narrowly."

"Do we have grounds?"

"We have enough to ask for circulation categories and dates without student names."

Ilyan exhaled.

"Do that."

Vaun's eyebrows rose.

He corrected himself.

"Please do that."

"I was going to."

Sael hid a smile.

Ilyan leaned back.

On the second table, the colored folders looked absurdly harmless.

A House family review had counted children who might exist. An Academy had counted students who might graduate. A Crown office had counted future officers. None of those acts was criminal. Most were necessary.

The horror was not that someone, somewhere, had counted the Last Seven and their dead classmates.

Of course they had been counted.

The question was whether anyone had crossed the line between preparing for the adults those students would become and deciding that some of those adults must never exist.

"I need to tell the others something," Ilyan said.

Vaun considered.

"What?"

"Not the sealed circulation ledger. The category. That we're looking at historical cohort planning."

"Why?"

"Because Damaris's family will have succession projections. Caelen's will have Crown placement estimates. Mireth may know whether the Green Covenant kept intake forecasts. Rhyl's family may have nothing formal, which is itself useful. Lyrion's diplomatic people probably tracked future marriage availability whether they admit it or not. Thessa has just been sent a current House review that demonstrates what ordinary arithmetic looks like."

Vaun tapped one finger against the table.

"You want comparison material."

"I want to know what normal looked like before we decide anything was abnormal."

Sael nodded once.

Vaun said, "Ask for categories, not private family contents. Nobody owes you their genealogies."

"I know."

"And do not turn a refusal into suspicion."

"I know."

She looked at him.

"Good. Then perhaps I can stop saying it."

Ilyan smiled.

"I would appreciate that."

"So would I."

---

They met that evening at a tavern near the Academy because none of them wanted another official room.

The place had low beams, bad acoustics and excellent fried onions. It was crowded enough that nobody could listen without making an effort and ordinary enough that seven young adults at one table attracted less attention than seven institutional affiliates in a Hall chamber.

Lyrion arrived last and blamed diplomacy.

"Diplomacy is not a person," Damaris said.

"You have not met my supervisor."

Rhyl pushed a mug toward him.

"Drink before you become educational."

Thessa sat beside Ilyan with one boot hooked around the rung of his chair. Damaris and Rhyl had taken the opposite side of the table and were sharing a plate without admitting it. Mireth had stolen Caelen's scarf because the tavern door leaked cold air. Caelen appeared resigned to never seeing it again.

Ilyan waited until food arrived.

He had learned something from Vaun.

Hungry people made worse committees.

"I have a question," he said.

Six faces turned toward him.

"Not a secret one."

"Those are always the dangerous ones," Lyrion said.

"What does your family count?"

There was a pause.

Damaris laughed first.

"Everything."

"Useful."

"No, genuinely. Grain, debt, births, ships, marriages, warehouse capacity, bridge tolls, cousins with gambling problems. You need to narrow it."

"Future adults."

That changed the table.

Not dramatically.

Mireth stopped tearing bread. Caelen looked at Ilyan rather than his plate. Thessa already knew where the question came from.

Ilyan explained only what he could: the existence of ordinary Academy cohort projections, the need for normal comparison categories, the possibility that House and institutional planning had counted future office eligibility and succession strength before Silver Night.

He did not mention the sealed circulation ledger.

He did not mention the reserve expenses.

Damaris wiped salt from her thumb.

"Caladren branches maintain generational exposure tables."

Rhyl stared at her.

"That sounds terrible."

"It means how many people in each age band can inherit obligations, hold contracts, sit councils or become financially dependent."

"It still sounds terrible."

"It is a spreadsheet, Rhyl."

"Exactly."

She kicked him beneath the table.

He grinned.

Caelen said, "Crown household offices maintain eligibility projections for commissions and ceremonial seats. Not by individual student unless there is already a reason. Mostly by House, age and expected qualification."

Mireth nodded slowly.

"The Covenant forecasts novices and licensed practitioners. We also track family clusters when inherited magical tendencies matter for training capacity."

"Could you get old categories?" Ilyan asked.

"Probably. Not names without cause."

"I don't want names."

"Good."

Lyrion had become suspiciously interested in his drink.

Thessa noticed too.

"Silver Bough?"

He sighed.

"We count marriage availability."

Rhyl's eyebrows rose.

"Of course you do."

"Not as a list of who should marry whom. Usually."

"Usually?"

"Rhyl."

"I am learning."

Lyrion ignored him.

"Diplomatic households maintain long-range kinship maps. Adult heirs, probable heirs, uncontracted adults, existing alliances, secondary households, legitimization possibilities. If two Houses are likely to have six unattached adults in the same generation, that matters. If one has one and another has eight, that also matters."

Ilyan felt the shape of it becoming clearer.

Not a conspiracy.

A civilization planning around long lives.

Rhyl shrugged when everyone looked at him.

"Orthell counts who can ride."

Damaris leaned into his shoulder.

"You count more than that."

"Fine. Who can ride, who knows which forest, who has children old enough to be sent somewhere, who owes patrol service, who is too old to sleep on the ground without complaining."

"You complain," Damaris said.

"I am preserving tradition."

The laughter eased something in the room.

Then Thessa pulled the Eris-Vey household review from her satchel.

She did not hand it around.

She placed it face down beneath her palm.

"My family sent me a current review this morning. I am in it. So is Ilyan."

Lyrion smiled.

"Congratulations."

"Don't."

"I haven't even said anything."

"Your face has."

Damaris looked delighted.

"Do they project children?"

Ilyan said, "Thank you. Finally, someone serious."

Thessa covered her face with one hand.

Mireth laughed so hard she nearly dropped Caelen's scarf into the sauce.

For a few minutes the future was allowed to be embarrassing rather than murderous.

That mattered too.

When the laughter faded, Ilyan looked around the table.

"This is what I needed."

Caelen understood first.

"Normal baselines."

"Yes. If we find that someone before Silver Night was counting Academy students, that is not enough. Everyone counted future adults in some form. We need to know whether the counting changed. Became unusually specific. Intersected with offices or licenses or emergency machinery it had no ordinary reason to touch."

Mireth's expression had gone thoughtful.

"Or whether someone asked for information they were not normally entitled to receive."

"Exactly."

Damaris said, "I can ask what historical projection categories River Ledger houses commonly bought or maintained. I won't give you private Caladren tables."

"I don't want them."

"Good."

Caelen nodded.

"I can do the same for Crown placement categories."

Lyrion lifted a finger.

"I can tell you what the Silver Bough considered ordinary demographic intelligence eighteen years ago. I cannot promise the old files are unsealed."

"Categories are enough."

Rhyl looked at Damaris.

"I can ask my grandmother what everyone was worried about."

Damaris stared at him.

"That may be the most dangerous archive any of us have proposed opening."

"She likes me."

"That is why I'm afraid."

Under the table, Thessa found Ilyan's hand.

Her thumb moved once across his knuckles.

He thought about the line in her family review. Current intimate association. No recommendation. Monitor only for material change.

It should have felt invasive.

Part of it did.

Another part was simply absurdly intimate in a new direction. Somewhere in an Eris-Vey office, a competent clerk had acknowledged that he mattered to Thessa's future enough to be entered in a column.

Not as permission.

Not as destiny.

As a fact with possible consequences.

He squeezed her hand.

"You are smiling," she murmured.

"No."

"You are."

"I am thinking about administrative categories."

"That is not better."

Across the table, Mireth was telling Caelen that if his family had projected their hypothetical children she wanted to know whether the Crown had at least assigned them attractive uniforms.

Caelen, to his credit, answered that he would accept no child of his being dressed by committee.

Lyrion raised his mug.

"To the next generation. May they survive our paperwork."

The toast landed differently than he intended.

For half a breath, nobody moved.

Then Ilyan lifted his cup.

The others followed.

"To them," he said.

He thought of the classmates who had never reached this table. Never been entered into lovers' household reviews. Never been offered bad postings, good careers, inconvenient marriages, unsuitable affairs, children, no children, secondary households, old age, arguments over inheritance, or the chance to become entirely different people from what anyone had projected at nineteen.

That was what had been taken.

Not merely seven lives among many dead.

Futures.

Plural, untidy, statistically inconvenient futures.

And somewhere in the surviving paper of 264 S.M., adults had been counting them.

The fact of counting was ordinary.

The next question was not.

Who had asked to see the count?
