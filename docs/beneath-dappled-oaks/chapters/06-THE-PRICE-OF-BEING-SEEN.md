# Chapter Six — The Price of Being Seen

Lady Maeryn Caladren-Voss owned a dining room designed to make debt feel attractive.

The windows were tall enough to flatter the river, the silver was old without being ostentatious, and the pale blue porcelain had been painted with tiny barges carrying grain, wool and wine toward increasingly prosperous horizons. Even the flowers appeared solvent. White climbing roses stood in a low bowl at the center of the table with no visible bruising, insect damage or evidence that weather had ever happened to them.

Damaris had eaten here before.

That was part of the problem.

She knew which chair Maeryn gave to people she intended to reassure, which chair she used for people she intended to observe, and which chair faced the western windows so that an afternoon guest spent the meal squinting into reflected river light while Maeryn remained comfortably in shade.

Rhyl had been given the reassuring chair.

Damaris had been given the observing one.

She looked at her aunt's cousin across the table.

Maeryn smiled.

Damaris smiled back.

Rhyl unfolded his napkin.

"Should I be worried?"

"Always," Damaris said.

Maeryn's smile widened.

"She was such a pleasant child."

"I was not."

"You were when asleep."

Rhyl made the mistake of laughing.

Damaris turned to him.

"You have chosen a side remarkably early."

"I was told there would be lunch."

"There is," Maeryn said. "I am not a barbarian."

The first course arrived: chilled pea soup with mint, soft white cheese and a scattering of edible blue flowers. Bloomtide food. Pretty enough for courtship season and practical enough for a banker who disliked wasting an afternoon on anything that required sawing through a roast.

Maeryn waited until the servants withdrew.

Then she looked at Rhyl.

"What do you intend to do with my cousin?"

Damaris closed her eyes.

Rhyl swallowed his soup.

"That was quick."

"Interest compounds."

"Maeryn."

"Damaris."

Rhyl put down his spoon.

Damaris opened her eyes again because she wanted to see whether he would try to become clever.

He did not.

"I intend to keep seeing her," he said.

Maeryn waited.

Rhyl waited too.

The silence became almost pleasant.

"That is all?" Maeryn asked.

"No."

"Good."

"It is all you get before she does."

Damaris looked at him.

His ears had gone slightly red.

That made it worse.

Or better.

She had not decided.

Maeryn leaned back.

"Excellent."

"You approve?" Damaris asked.

"Of the answer. I have not yet audited the man."

"He is sitting here."

"Convenient."

Rhyl looked between them.

"Is this how your family flirts?"

"No," Damaris said.

Maeryn said, "Sometimes."

Damaris stared at her.

Maeryn took another spoonful of soup.

Older relatives remained a social catastrophe.

The second course brought river trout with sorrel, young potatoes and a sharp green sauce. Maeryn allowed them three minutes of ordinary conversation before returning to the purpose of the invitation.

"You were seen at Seven Lamps."

Damaris looked down at her plate.

"Yes."

"Not merely together. Together deliberately."

Rhyl's hand rested beside his wineglass.

Damaris could have denied nothing and still made the answer evasive.

She did not.

"Yes."

Maeryn nodded once.

"Then people will price it."

Rhyl frowned.

"Price what?"

"Access. Probability. Future behavior. Whether Damaris can be induced to favor a road, contract, posting or household because it matters to you. Whether you can be induced to favor a river interest because it matters to her. Whether either family has begun negotiations. Whether one of you is being used to reach the other. Whether the attachment is durable enough to matter and fragile enough to exploit."

She said it without cruelty.

That was why Damaris listened.

Maeryn did not think love was false because other people could calculate around it. She thought calculation was what people did around anything valuable.

Rhyl considered this.

"And what have you priced it at?"

Maeryn's eyebrows rose.

Damaris felt a ridiculous surge of pride.

"Unseasoned," Maeryn said. "Publicly legible. Probably genuine. Too new for either of you to know its limits. More dangerous to Damaris than to you in the immediate term because Caladren business touches more committees than Red Hart routes do. Potentially more dangerous to you later because wilderness postings make private access and correspondence easier to manipulate."

Rhyl nodded slowly.

"Fair."

"Do not encourage her," Damaris said.

"I am trying to survive lunch."

"Poorly."

Maeryn ignored them.

"I did not invite you to tell you to stop."

Damaris had expected that.

Rhyl had not.

"You didn't?"

"If I intended to stop it, I would not begin by inviting you to a pleasant meal and explaining the threat model. I would speak to Damaris privately, then to the relevant family elders, then make your schedules inconvenient until one of you became tired."

Rhyl stared at her.

Maeryn cut a potato in half.

"You asked."

"I did not."

"Your face did."

Damaris bit the inside of her cheek.

Rhyl glanced at her.

"Do not laugh."

That ended any possibility of restraint.

She laughed.

He looked offended for approximately two seconds before his mouth betrayed him.

Maeryn let them recover.

"There is another reason I wanted you here together. Damaris, your use of Meris's River Ledger cipher has now produced enough audit noise that three people outside her immediate office have asked why a Caladren-Ilyra credential is touching old West Tower records."

Damaris's amusement vanished.

"Who?"

"A treasury compliance clerk, a transport-contract examiner and Jalen."

"Jalen asks about everything."

"Yes. That is why we pay him."

Rhyl said, "Do any of them know what she found?"

"Not from the audit trail alone. They know where she looked, approximately when, and under whose authority."

Damaris thought of the certified abstract, the council packet, the old transfer records, the accumulating chain of perfectly ordinary paperwork that had become dangerous only because someone had bothered to compare it.

"Can the searches be hidden?"

Maeryn looked at her.

Damaris felt embarrassed before the answer came.

"Yes," Maeryn said. "Which is why you will not do that."

Rhyl's mouth twitched.

Damaris pointed at him.

"Nothing from you."

"I said nothing."

"Your face did."

Maeryn looked pleased to have been quoted.

Damaris sighed.

"Why not?"

"Because the audit is also protection. If someone later claims you fabricated the request, exceeded the cipher, altered the abstract or obtained records through an unofficial channel, the trail shows otherwise. Privacy and deniability are not the same thing. You currently have very little of the first and a useful amount of the second."

That was unpleasantly sensible.

"So what do I do?"

"Use your own authority where you have it. Use Vaun's where the investigation requires it. Use Meris's only when Meris actually intends to own the request. Stop borrowing family standing merely because it is faster."

Damaris nodded.

It was not a reprimand.

It still landed like one.

Maeryn looked at Rhyl.

"And you."

"I was afraid of that."

"Do not become her private courier merely because you can move through places clerks cannot."

Rhyl's expression changed.

Not anger.

Attention.

"I haven't."

"Good. Keep not doing it. If the Red Hart begins carrying River Ledger evidence informally, both institutions will eventually discover the arrangement and each will assume the other knows more than it does."

Damaris thought of the west service gate.

Of Rhyl's routes.

Of how easy it would be to ask him to carry something just once.

Maeryn saw her thinking.

"Exactly."

Damaris hated competent relatives.

Dessert was honey custard with the first strawberries of Bloomtide.

Maeryn waited until Damaris had eaten half of hers before delivering the final injury.

"If this becomes serious, tell your mother before someone else does."

Damaris stopped with the spoon halfway to her mouth.

"It is not—"

Rhyl looked at her.

She looked at him.

Maeryn drank her tea.

Damaris lowered the spoon.

"I hate this family."

"No, you don't."

"Intermittently."

"That is healthier."

Rhyl reached under the table and found her hand.

He did it where Maeryn could see the movement.

Not performatively.

Not secretly either.

Damaris laced her fingers through his.

Maeryn noticed.

Of course she did.

She merely asked whether they wanted more tea.

That, somehow, was worse than commentary.

---

Outside, the river smelled of wet stone and spring weeds.

Damaris and Rhyl walked without a carriage because the rain had stopped and because Damaris needed the city around her rather than another upholstered compartment in which relatives could become strategic abstractions.

They crossed a narrow bridge crowded with flower sellers preparing for the evening market.

Rhyl bought her nothing.

She liked him for it.

"Well?" she asked.

"Your family is terrifying."

"Correct."

"I like her."

"Traitor."

He squeezed her hand.

They walked another twenty paces.

Then he said, "Was she right?"

Damaris knew which part he meant.

"About us being priced? Yes."

"No."

She looked at him.

He had stopped beside the bridge parapet.

People moved around them carrying baskets, ledgers, folded awnings and bundles of cut flowers.

"About telling your mother if this becomes serious."

Damaris looked at the river.

A barge was turning below them, its towline dark with water.

"Probably."

"And?"

"And I don't know what serious means yet."

"Neither do I."

That helped.

She turned back.

"I know I want you tomorrow."

His expression softened.

"Good."

"And the day after."

"Ambitious."

"Do not ruin it."

"Sorry."

She stepped closer until the front of his coat touched hers.

"I know I don't want my family deciding what this is before we do."

"Then they don't."

"You say that as if houses are weather."

"Weather is less organized."

"Debatable."

His thumb moved across her knuckles.

"Damaris."

"Mm?"

"I meant what I said at lunch."

Her stomach tightened pleasantly.

"Which part?"

"The part Maeryn didn't get."

She looked at his mouth.

That was becoming a problem of its own.

"Good," she said.

He kissed her beside the bridge while three flower sellers, one elderly ferryman and a child carrying onions were in perfectly adequate positions to notice.

The world did not end.

It merely acquired more witnesses.

When they separated, Damaris rested her forehead against his.

"That was politically irresponsible."

"I can do worse."

"I know."

"Tomorrow?"

She smiled.

"Ambitious."

---

Ilyan found Teren in the practice court behind the Valeric town house.

He had delayed until afternoon exactly as promised.

He had also spent the morning deciding what not to ask.

That proved more useful.

Teren was teaching a boy of perhaps fourteen how to stop overcommitting his shoulder during a downward cut. The boy was not family. Ilyan knew because Valeric children trained in the inner court and because Teren used a different voice with them.

"Again," Teren said.

The boy raised the wooden sword.

Teren corrected his elbow with two fingers.

"Your opponent is not under the floorboards. Stop trying to reach him there."

The boy tried again.

Better.

Teren saw Ilyan over the boy's shoulder.

Nothing changed in his face.

That hurt more than it should have.

"Five minutes," Teren told the boy. "Water. Then footwork."

The boy bowed and ran toward the side passage.

Teren set down the practice blade.

"You came."

"I said I would."

"You said tomorrow afternoon. I have known Valerics to interpret that as any time before winter."

Ilyan almost smiled.

Almost.

Teren noticed.

"What happened?"

Ilyan took the certified copy from inside his coat.

He did not hand it over.

"Do you remember renewing a restriction on Revision Seventeen after Silver Night?"

Teren went still.

There.

Not guilt.

Recognition.

Ilyan felt his pulse in his throat.

"Yes," Teren said.

The answer was too easy.

Ilyan had prepared for denial.

"Why?"

"Because I was instructed to."

"By whom?"

"The Joint Office for Order and Credential Integrity."

Ilyan looked at him.

Teren looked back.

"You remember the office."

"Of course I remember the office."

"It has been dissolved for years."

"That does not make me forget working with it."

Ilyan took a breath.

This was why he had delayed.

He wanted Teren to be evasive because anger was easier when it had a shape.

"What did they tell you?"

Teren's eyes dropped briefly to the paper in Ilyan's hand.

"That material connected to an active security review had been exposed by the West Tower disaster and that existing restrictions required renewal under surviving delegated authority."

"Did they say what material?"

"No."

"Did you ask?"

"Yes."

"And?"

"I was told I did not have compartment access."

Ilyan laughed once.

There was no humor in it.

"So you renewed it anyway."

"Yes."

"Why?"

Teren's expression tightened.

"Because that was my office."

"That is not an answer."

"It is the answer you dislike."

The boy returned carrying a cup of water.

Teren turned.

"Inside. Tell Master Edrin I am occupied."

The boy looked between them and disappeared without argument.

When the door closed, Teren said, "Ilyan, I was a delegated seal officer. I authenticated requests that arrived through recognized channels. I did not possess the authority to open every compartment whose restrictions I renewed."

"You could have refused."

"Yes."

That stopped him.

Teren folded his arms.

"I could have refused. The request would have gone to another seal officer. I had no evidence the request was unlawful. I had no evidence the underlying review was fraudulent. I had a dead academy tower, emergency orders across half the city and a Joint Office request carrying the proper countersigns. So I renewed it."

Ilyan hated that too.

Because it sounded possible.

Because it sounded ordinary.

Because terrible systems did not require every person inside them to be secretly delighted by the result.

"Did my father ask you to do it?"

"No."

"My uncle?"

"No."

"Did either of them discuss Revision Seventeen with you?"

"Not then."

Ilyan caught the last word.

"Later?"

Teren looked toward the empty doorway.

"Your uncle asked whether the restrictions remained intact."

The practice court seemed suddenly too bright.

"When?"

"Several weeks after the disaster."

"What did you tell him?"

"That the renewal had been executed."

"Did he say why he cared?"

"No."

"Did you ask?"

"Yes."

"And?"

"He told me it concerned protected review material and that curiosity was not a virtue merely because I possessed it."

Ilyan's hand tightened around the certified copy.

Teren watched him.

"You recognize that sentence."

"I recognize him."

Neither spoke for a moment.

A sparrow landed on the practice wall, reconsidered the family atmosphere and left.

Ilyan said, "Do you know Mera Holt?"

Teren frowned.

"No."

"Selwyn Arven?"

"The examiner? I knew the name. I do not remember meeting him."

"Tal's comparison die?"

Nothing.

Not even recognition.

"Whose?"

Ilyan watched him carefully.

Teren's irritation sharpened.

"Do not do that."

"Do what?"

"Look at me as though you have already decided what my face means."

Ilyan felt heat rise into his own.

"You renewed a restriction tied to the same office that removed evidence from an old counterfeit investigation."

Teren's anger vanished.

"What?"

That was different.

Ilyan had known Teren most of his life. He knew surprise could be performed. Teren had taught him how.

This did not prove anything.

It still felt real.

"Eighteen years ago," Ilyan said. "The Joint Office took custody of a returned comparison die from North Quay under a lawful warrant. The return field is blank. The same office later requested the restriction renewal you executed."

Teren sat down on the low stone bench.

For the first time since Ilyan arrived, he looked old.

Not ancient. Not weak.

Simply like a man discovering that an administrative act he had filed away as one bad week among many had acquired roots extending backward beyond his knowledge.

"I did not know that," he said.

Ilyan believed that Teren believed what he was saying.

That was not the same as knowing it was true.

Examiner Sael would have approved.

The thought irritated him enough to be useful.

"Do you still have anything from the Joint Office?"

Teren looked up.

"Records?"

"Letters. Seals. Routing slips. Anything."

"Official records went to successor custody when the office dissolved."

"Personal papers?"

Teren was quiet.

Ilyan waited.

"Possibly."

"Where?"

"Do not make that face."

"What face?"

"The one where you become twenty years older because someone said possibly."

Ilyan nearly told him not to joke.

Then he remembered Teren correcting his grip when he was eight, smuggling him sweet buns after funerals, teaching him that a wooden sword still hurt if one behaved stupidly with it.

Affection did not disappear because suspicion arrived.

That was the worst part.

"Where?" Ilyan asked again.

"My old service chest. Country house. If I kept personal routing notes, they would be there."

"Will you look?"

Teren's gaze stayed on him.

"Yes."

"With me?"

A longer pause.

"Yes."

Ilyan exhaled.

Teren leaned forward, forearms on his knees.

"Now you answer one."

Ilyan did not like that.

"Ask."

"How much does your father know about what you are doing?"

"I don't know."

"That is not the same as nothing."

"I know."

"And your uncle?"

"I don't know."

Teren swore quietly.

"What?"

"If the Joint Office request intersects something they were protecting, you may already have made yourself visible."

"I was visible when I refused the oath language."

"To the family. Not necessarily to everyone else."

Ilyan stared at him.

"Everyone else who?"

Teren shook his head.

"I don't know. That is the point."

For once, Ilyan believed him without effort.

Teren stood.

"Do not go to the country house alone."

"I wasn't planning to."

"Good."

"Do not tell my father."

Teren's face closed.

There it was.

Family.

Duty.

The old fracture.

"Ilyan—"

"Do you intend to tell him?"

Teren looked at him for a long time.

"No."

Ilyan's chest loosened.

Then Teren continued.

"Not today."

The relief disappeared.

"That is not what I asked."

"It is what I can promise."

Ilyan hated the answer.

He also understood it.

That was becoming an exhausting habit among the people he loved.

He tucked the certified copy back into his coat.

"Tomorrow morning. Your service chest."

Teren nodded.

"Bring someone you trust."

Ilyan thought of the Seven.

"I will."

As he turned toward the passage, Teren said his name.

Ilyan stopped.

"I did renew it," Teren said. "Do not make me innocent because you love me."

Ilyan looked back.

Teren's face was tired now.

"And do not make me guilty because it would be simpler."

Ilyan nodded once.

Then he left.

---

By evening the Seven had gathered in the back room of a tea house Lyrion liked because the proprietor understood that diplomats, lovers and investigators all paid extra for doors that shut properly.

Damaris arrived with Rhyl.

Nobody commented.

That was suspicious.

Mireth lasted twelve seconds.

"How was lunch?"

"Educational."

"Did Maeryn ask his intentions?"

Damaris stared at her.

Mireth brightened.

"She did."

Caelen covered his mouth with his cup.

Thessa said, "What did he say?"

"None of you are my friends."

"We survived school together," Lyrion said. "That is not the same thing."

Rhyl sat beside Damaris with the serenity of a man who had decided humiliation was weather.

"I said I intended to keep seeing her."

Mireth made a small delighted sound.

Damaris pointed at her.

"I will tell your mother everything."

"She already knows everything."

"Then I will invent something."

For a few minutes they were young enough to be ridiculous.

Then Ilyan put the certified renewal copy on the table.

The room changed.

Not completely.

Damaris's knee remained against Rhyl's. Caelen still had Mireth's ribbon tied beneath his Fellowship clasp. Thessa stole a sugared plum from Lyrion's saucer without asking.

Their lives did not stop being themselves because the investigation entered the room.

Ilyan told them what Teren had said.

He did not improve it.

He did not call Teren innocent.

He did not call him complicit.

When he reached the part about his uncle asking whether the restrictions remained intact several weeks after Silver Night, nobody interrupted.

Caelen finally said, "That matters."

"Yes."

"It still doesn't tell us why."

"I know."

Mireth asked, "Who is going with you tomorrow?"

Ilyan looked around the table.

Rhyl said, "Not Damaris."

Damaris turned on him.

"Excuse me?"

"You have a River Ledger appointment tomorrow morning. You told me on the bridge."

She had.

That was infuriating.

"I hate when you remember things."

"I can try less."

"Don't."

Thessa said, "I'll go."

Everyone looked at her.

She shrugged.

"Wardens spend half their lives opening old military chests and discovering someone thought mildew was an archival method. I know how not to destroy paper."

Lyrion touched two fingers to her wrist.

"And if Teren decides he dislikes the company?"

"Then I shall survive his disappointment."

The corner of Ilyan's mouth moved.

"Thank you."

Thessa nodded.

No speech.

No declaration of eternal friendship.

She had offered.

He had accepted.

That was enough.

Damaris looked around the table.

They were no longer students protected from the games of their elders. Maeryn had been right about that much: people would price them now. Their friendships, their work, their kisses, their routes through the city, their access to archives and households and old service chests.

But pricing a thing was not the same as owning it.

Rhyl's hand found hers beneath the table.

Across from them, Ilyan was asking Thessa what time she could leave.

Mireth was already arguing with Caelen about whether he could obtain successor-custody information without exceeding his Fellowship remit. Lyrion had gone quiet in the particular way that meant he was remembering who owed him a favor.

Tomorrow had begun arranging itself.

Damaris squeezed Rhyl's fingers.

This time, when he looked at her, she did not look away.

## The Service Chest

The Valeric country house was not in the country so much as at the point where the city became embarrassed by its own density and began pretending orchards counted as wilderness.

Thessa approved of it immediately.

There were stone walls, a practical stable, a rain cistern large enough to matter and no ornamental pond placed where a horse could fall into it. Whoever had laid out the property had expected people to live there rather than merely arrive beautifully.

Ilyan noticed her inspection as they came through the gate.

"You are judging the drains."

"They are good drains."

"You sound surprised."

"Your family has curtains worth more than a cavalry remount. I had concerns about priorities."

Teren, walking ahead of them, looked back.

"Those curtains belonged to my grandmother."

"Were they waterproof?"

"No."

"Then I stand by my assessment."

Teren gave Ilyan a look.

Ilyan shrugged.

"I brought someone I trust."

"So I see."

The house had been opened for the season but not inhabited properly. White covers still lay over several pieces of furniture in the entrance hall. A maid had lit the kitchen range and one downstairs fire before their arrival, then retreated to some safer part of the estate after being told that three adults intended to spend the morning opening old government papers.

The service chest lived upstairs in what had once been Teren's room.

Ilyan had slept there as a child.

He remembered being seven and furious after losing a practice bout to a cousin four years older than he was. Teren had made him sit on the edge of the bed and explain, in painful detail, why being smaller had not forced him to attack from the same angle six times.

He remembered sweet buns hidden in the desk.

He remembered rain against the western windows.

He remembered believing Teren knew the answer to everything worth asking.

The room was smaller now.

Or he was.

Teren crossed to a wardrobe, knelt and pulled out a long iron-bound chest with both hands.

Thessa crouched beside it.

"Mildew?"

"Probably."

"Mice?"

"Possibly."

"Explosive seal?"

Teren looked offended.

"I was a civil officer."

"That was not an answer."

Ilyan laughed before he could stop himself.

Teren glanced up.

For a moment the morning became something else: an uncle on the floor, a friend beside him, a house full of old summers.

Then Teren took a key from his pocket.

"No explosive seal."

"Good."

"One privacy thread. Broken when the chest opens."

Thessa held out a hand.

"Wait."

Teren stopped.

She examined the lid without touching the thread.

"Original?"

"Yes."

"Has anyone else had the key?"

"Not knowingly."

"Has the chest been moved?"

"Three times. From my city rooms to the Joint Office quarters, from there to the Valeric town house after dissolution, then here."

"Anyone inventory it?"

"The official material was inventoried before I retained the chest. Personal papers were not."

Thessa nodded.

"Open it."

Teren did.

The thread snapped with a tiny dry sound.

Nobody spoke.

There were no secrets visible from the top.

There were shirts.

Ilyan stared.

Thessa stared.

Teren said, "I did warn you this was personal storage."

"You kept government routing notes under shirts?" Ilyan asked.

"Nobody steals old shirts."

Thessa lifted one between two fingers.

"Someone should have."

Teren took it from her.

"I liked that shirt."

"You were wrong."

Beneath the clothes lay two pairs of boots, a leather sword belt gone stiff with age, a cracked shaving case, three bundles of letters tied by sender, a small wooden box, and six thin notebooks.

Teren sat back on his heels.

"Those."

Ilyan reached for the notebooks.

Thessa caught his wrist.

"Table first. Dry hands. One at a time."

He looked at her.

"You volunteered for this because you enjoy giving orders."

"That was among the reasons."

They moved to the writing table beneath the window.

Teren opened the shutters. Bloomtide light spilled across dust, faded wood and the pale edges of paper that had not been handled in years.

The notebooks were not diaries.

Ilyan was grateful.

Reading Teren's private thoughts would have felt different from reading the professional residue Teren had explicitly offered. Instead each volume contained dates, docket numbers, initials, brief reminders, expenses, names of people Teren intended to chase for missing countersigns and occasional insults directed at forms.

Thessa found the system first.

"Left margin is incoming. Right is what you did with it."

Teren nodded.

"Usually."

"Usually?"

"I was younger."

"You were employed."

"The two conditions are not mutually corrective."

Ilyan turned pages.

The years moved beneath his fingers.

Ordinary work filled almost everything.

Permit renewals. Witness-screen maintenance. Restricted testimony transfers. A dispute about whether a sealed botanical sample could legally be transported in the same carriage as a magistrate's lunch. Three pages of irritation about temporary credentials issued during a flood.

The Joint Office had not spent every day conspiring against the Realm.

It had spent most days being an office.

That mattered.

It also made the dangerous parts harder to see.

Teren leaned over his shoulder.

"There."

Ilyan stopped.

The date was six days after Silver Night.

The entry was brief.

**JOCI / protected review / R17 restriction renewal. Delegated seal. Countersigns verified. No compartment access.**

In the right margin Teren had written:

**Executed. Copy to successor packet if office closes before review terminates.**

Ilyan read it twice.

"Successor packet."

Teren nodded slowly.

"I had forgotten that."

"What was it?"

"A dissolution precaution. Joint offices were temporary by design. Anything still active when one closed had to be assigned somewhere permanent."

Thessa looked up from the next notebook.

"Where?"

"Depends on the material."

"Revision Seventeen?"

Teren rubbed his jaw.

"Truth-order material should have gone to Hall custody. Security review material could have gone to Crown Integrity, Hall of Veils, or a tribunal annex depending on who owned the originating authority."

Ilyan felt impatience rise.

"So the successor packet might tell us who inherited it."

"If it survives."

"Where would it be?"

"Not here."

Thessa said, "Then find the dissolution year."

Teren looked at her.

She tapped the notebooks.

"If you kept notes when the office closed, you may have written where the boxes went."

They moved forward three volumes.

The work became slower.

Teren had been reassigned during the dissolution and his notes grew crowded with arrows, crossed-out room numbers and abbreviated destinations that meant nothing to Ilyan.

Thessa made him explain each one.

She did not let him turn memory into certainty.

"You think HRA means Hall Restricted Annex?"

"Yes."

"Think or know?"

Teren frowned.

"Think."

"Then say think."

Ilyan looked at her.

Thessa did not notice.

Or pretended not to.

Examiner Sael would have liked her.

That was unfortunate.

Near midday they found a folded sheet tucked into the back cover of the final notebook.

It was not a secret memorandum.

It was a packing list.

Teren laughed once when he saw it.

"Of course."

"What?" Ilyan asked.

"We spent three weeks preparing the formal transfer register. I kept the sheet telling the porters which rooms received which crates."

Thessa flattened it carefully.

The paper listed twenty-three crates.

Most were dull even by their current standards.

Personnel files.

Budget records.

Expired warrant books.

Seal molds for destruction.

Closed administrative correspondence.

Then:

**Crates 14–16 — active protected-review continuations — Hall of Veils, Restricted Annex C. Transfer against master successor register.**

Below it:

**Crate 17 — retained comparative property / credential exemplars — Hall of Veils, Restricted Annex C pending disposition.**

Nobody moved.

Ilyan read the two lines again.

Thessa said, "Same destination."

"Yes."

Teren had gone pale.

Ilyan looked at him.

"Did you know?"

"No."

"You packed it."

"I helped close an office with twenty-three crates, four successor institutions and half the staff already reassigned. I knew comparative property went somewhere. I did not know your Revision Seventeen renewal and North Quay exemplars had ended in the same annex."

Ilyan wanted to accuse him of remembering too little.

He stopped himself.

Eighteen years of administrative debris did not become memorable merely because it had become important to Ilyan yesterday.

Thessa pointed to the bottom of the sheet.

"Master successor register."

Teren nodded.

"That is the document that matters. This only tells us where porters were told to put crates. The register should identify originating dockets and final receiving custody."

"Hall of Veils?" Ilyan asked.

"If Annex C still exists under that name."

"Sael can find it."

Teren's expression changed.

"Do not ask him to find it unofficially."

Ilyan bristled.

"I wasn't going to."

"Good. Because if the annex contains active or inherited restrictions, an informal search by a newly sworn examiner with a family connection to one of the records will be noticed for all the wrong reasons."

Thessa folded her arms.

"He's right."

"I know."

"You sound delighted."

"I am surrounded by people being right at me."

Teren's mouth almost smiled.

Ilyan looked back at the packing list.

The paper did not prove that Tal's die had remained in Crate Seventeen.

It did not prove that Revision Seventeen had been in Crates Fourteen through Sixteen.

It did not prove that anyone at the Hall had connected the two categories.

It gave them a place where the successor register ought to have gone.

That was enough for one morning.

Thessa was reading the rest of the sheet.

"Who signed the porter copy?"

Teren leaned closer.

At the bottom were three signatures.

One was Teren's.

One belonged to a property clerk he remembered only after several seconds.

The third was an archive receiving officer.

The name was still legible.

**Aric Saren.**

Ilyan's chair scraped the floor.

Teren looked at him.

"You know him?"

"Not personally."

Thessa's eyes narrowed.

"North Quay."

Ilyan nodded.

"He witnessed Tal's die leaving the disposal lot."

Silence settled over the table.

Teren reached for the sheet, then stopped before touching it.

"The same Saren?"

"We need to verify that."

Thessa gave Ilyan an approving glance.

He resented it less than he expected.

Teren sat down.

"If it is the same man, he moved from North Quay stores into archive receiving work."

"Or there were two Aric Sarens," Thessa said.

"Yes."

"Or the signature is someone signing on his authority."

"Possible."

"Or—"

Ilyan held up a hand.

"I understand the exercise."

Thessa smiled.

"Do you?"

"Unfortunately."

They copied the packing list in full.

Teren refused to surrender the original without process.

Ilyan did not argue.

That surprised both of them.

Before they closed the chest, Teren picked up the small wooden box.

He turned it in his hands.

"This has nothing to do with the Joint Office."

Ilyan waited.

Teren opened it.

Inside lay a child's wooden practice token, painted blue and worn smooth at the edges.

Ilyan recognized it.

He had won it from Teren when he was nine.

Not honestly.

Teren had let him.

At nine, Ilyan had known that and pretended not to.

At twenty, he had forgotten the token existed.

Teren looked at him.

"You were unbearable for a week."

"You cheated."

"In your favor."

"Still cheating."

Thessa looked between them.

"Should I leave?"

"No," both men said.

That made her laugh.

Teren put the token back.

He closed the box.

Affection remained.

So did the packing list on the table.

Neither cancelled the other.

---

They ate bread, soft cheese and cold chicken in the kitchen because none of them wanted to wait for a formal meal.

Teren made tea badly.

Thessa told him so.

"You are remarkably comfortable criticizing people in their own houses," he said.

"Western hospitality requires honesty."

"Does it?"

"No."

Ilyan nearly choked on his tea.

The laughter left him unexpectedly raw.

He set down the cup.

Teren noticed.

He did not ask whether Ilyan was all right.

For that, Ilyan was grateful.

Instead Teren said, "Your uncle will know the Hall has a successor register if he ever cared enough to ask where the office records went."

Ilyan looked at him.

"Do you think he did?"

"I don't know."

"Would my father?"

"I don't know."

Thessa tore bread in half.

"Then the useful question is who can see a lawful request for it."

Teren nodded.

"Hall records staff. Possibly the successor custodian. Depending on classification, whoever currently owns the inherited restriction."

"And family?"

"Not automatically."

Ilyan thought of his father's face.

His uncle's voice.

The oldest oak.

The Seven listening while he told them what his family had done.

"I want Vaun involved."

Thessa nodded.

Teren did not.

Ilyan looked at him.

"Problem?"

"No."

"You made a face."

"Apparently everyone has one now."

"Teren."

His uncle rubbed a hand over his mouth.

"A judge turns curiosity into a record. That is not a reason not to do it. It is a reason to understand what you are doing."

"I do."

"Do you?"

Ilyan's temper stirred.

Then he looked at the copied packing list beside his plate.

"Enough to know I don't want this hidden in my coat."

Teren studied him.

Then nodded.

"Good."

It was not forgiveness.

It was not permission.

It was simply one adult recognizing another adult's choice.

Ilyan found that he wanted it anyway.

---

Judge Vaun read the copy that afternoon.

Damaris arrived halfway through because her River Ledger appointment had ended early. Rhyl came ten minutes later with mud on one boot and no explanation anyone required.

Mireth and Caelen were still at their respective duties. Lyrion had sent word that he was chasing a different archive question and would join them after dusk.

Vaun read the packing list once.

Then again.

"Aric Saren," Damaris said.

"Possibly," Vaun replied.

"The name matches."

"The name matches."

Damaris sighed.

Ilyan felt a small, vicious pleasure that someone else was being corrected.

Thessa saw it.

She kicked his boot under the table.

Vaun continued.

"We verify Saren's employment history. We request the master successor register and the receiving inventory for Restricted Annex C. Separately."

Rhyl leaned over Damaris's shoulder to read the copied sheet.

"Why separately?"

"Because if one survives and the other does not, I want to know that before someone tells me they were always one record."

He nodded.

Damaris pointed to Crate Seventeen.

"If Tal's die was still retained comparative property when the Joint Office dissolved—"

Vaun looked at her.

Damaris stopped.

"If."

"Good."

"I dislike you."

"Frequently useful."

Ilyan sat back.

The room smelled of tea and wet wool.

Damaris's shoulder rested against Rhyl's arm as though it had begun doing so without requiring discussion. Thessa had stolen Ilyan's pencil and was making a clean list of the record requests. Teren had returned to the country house with the original packing sheet and the ugly shirt Thessa had insulted.

Tomorrow the Hall would receive a formal request.

Aric Saren would be located if he could be located.

And somewhere inside the Hall of Veils, perhaps under another name, perhaps under another custodian, perhaps already destroyed according to a perfectly valid schedule, there had once been a Restricted Annex C that received both active protected-review continuations and retained credential exemplars from the dead Joint Office.

Ilyan looked at the copied signature again.

Aric Saren.

One name appearing at two different custody boundaries years apart.

Not an answer.

A person to ask.

---

## The Man Who Signed Twice

Judge Vaun found Aric Saren by asking the pension office whether it had managed to keep paying him.

It had.

This pleased her more than it pleased Ilyan.

"You expected something dramatic," Thessa said.

They were standing beneath the Hall of Veils' eastern arcade while rain worked patiently at the courtyard stones. Clerks crossed between buildings under grey umbrellas. Somewhere above them a bell marked the middle of Veilday morning.

"I expected the Hall to have a record."

"It did."

"A pension address is not the record I meant."

"Still a record."

Ilyan looked at her.

Thessa had discovered that his irritation could be cultivated for recreation.

She was becoming dangerously efficient at it.

Vaun came through the bronze doors carrying a narrow folder.

"Same man."

Ilyan straightened.

"Certain?"

"Certain enough to ask him questions. North Quay personnel roll, transfer appointment, pension record and two witnessed signatures. Aric Saren left North Quay Stores nine years after the Tal inquiry and entered Joint Office property receiving. He remained through dissolution, then served six years in Hall annex custody before retiring."

Thessa held out her hand.

Vaun did not give her the folder.

"You may look when we are inside."

"Inside where?"

"A carriage."

"His house?"

"No."

Ilyan frowned.

Vaun started walking.

"He has asked to meet at the pension garden."

"Why?"

"Because his wife is asleep and he does not want three investigators, a Valeric and a Western Warden frightening her before luncheon."

Thessa glanced at Ilyan.

"Reasonable."

"I am not frightening."

"You arrived at your uncle's house carrying suspicion like a sword."

"That is not the same thing."

Vaun opened the carriage door.

"Get in."

That ended the discussion.

---

The pension garden occupied a strip of old civic land above the southern canal, where retired clerks, widows of public officers and anyone with sufficient age to ignore posted rules had spent decades turning municipal flowerbeds into private kingdoms.

Aric Saren was pruning a pear tree.

He was smaller than Ilyan expected.

That annoyed him immediately, because he had no defensible reason to have expected anything else.

Saren wore a brown coat with one elbow patched in green wool. His hair had gone almost entirely white. He had tied a red cord around the handle of his pruning knife so that he would not lose it in the grass.

He looked at Vaun's clasp, then at Thessa's Warden insignia, then at Ilyan.

His attention stayed on Ilyan longest.

"Valeric."

Not a question.

Ilyan nodded.

"Ilyan."

"Teren's nephew?"

"Yes."

Saren returned to the pear branch.

"He still alive?"

"Yes."

"Still keeping papers he was meant to throw away?"

Thessa made a small sound.

Ilyan ignored her.

"Apparently."

Saren cut the branch cleanly.

"Useful habit in old age. Criminal habit in youth. Depends who asks."

Vaun said, "May we sit?"

Saren pointed with the knife toward a bench.

"You may. My knees prefer standing after breakfast."

Vaun sat.

Thessa and Ilyan followed.

Saren remained beside the tree.

Vaun began with North Quay.

Not Tal's die.

Employment dates.

Supervisors.

His transfer.

Saren answered without hurry. When he did not remember, he said so. When Vaun gave him a date, he sometimes recovered a season or a supervisor but rarely a precise day.

Then she showed him the certified North Quay receipt copy.

Saren took longer with that.

He held it at arm's length, cursed his eyes and put on spectacles from his coat.

"Mine."

"The signature?"

"Yes."

"Do you remember the transaction?"

"No."

Ilyan felt disappointment before he could prevent it.

Saren looked at him.

"Young man, I signed hundreds of property releases. If I remembered every cancelled seal and broken credential plate, I would have no room left for my grandchildren."

Ilyan inclined his head.

"Fair."

Vaun produced the copy of Teren's porter sheet.

Saren read the bottom first.

His eyebrows rose.

"That is also mine."

"You remember this one?"

"The dissolution? Yes. Everyone remembers closing an office. Half the building becomes furniture nobody owns and the other half becomes boxes nobody wants."

Thessa leaned forward.

"Crate Seventeen."

Saren read the line.

"Comparative property. Yes."

"Do you remember what was in it?"

"No."

"Would you have known then?"

"Some of it."

That changed the air.

Vaun said, "Explain."

"Receiving inventory was checked against property slips. We did not open every wrapped exemplar if its seal was intact, but we checked numbered bundles and transfer counts."

"Would Tal's die have had a number?"

"If it remained in comparative custody, yes."

"Do you remember seeing it?"

Saren looked down at the porter sheet.

"No."

Vaun waited.

Saren looked toward the canal.

"I remember a problem with the crate."

Ilyan kept his hands still.

"What kind?"

"Weight."

Thessa's head turned.

Saren noticed.

"Not your eleven ounces. Don't make that face."

Ilyan almost smiled despite himself.

"What weight?"

"Crate Seventeen arrived heavier than the porter sheet expected. Not by much. A few pounds."

"Why?"

"Because somebody had put a document packet in with property."

Vaun's voice remained level.

"What packet?"

"I don't remember the title. I remember being annoyed because documents belonged in document custody and exemplars belonged in property custody."

"What did you do?"

"Refused the crate until the packet was removed or separately receipted."

Thessa said, "Was it removed?"

"Yes."

"Where did it go?"

Saren closed his eyes.

For several breaths only the canal moved.

"Annex C had a side register for exceptions. Blue cover."

Vaun's pencil stopped.

"A side register."

"Temporary irregularities. Mispacked material. Broken seals. Wrong-room deliveries. Things too small to amend the master register before intake closed."

"Does it survive?"

Saren opened his eyes.

"It should have been retained with the receiving inventory."

"Name?"

"Annex C Exception Book. Or Exception Register. We called it the blue book."

Ilyan said, "Who removed the packet?"

Saren looked at him.

"I don't remember."

"Would the blue book say?"

"If I was doing my job."

"Were you?"

Thessa turned toward Ilyan.

Saren laughed.

"Usually."

Ilyan felt heat rise into his face.

"Sorry."

"Don't be. At your age I asked worse questions with less reason."

Vaun tapped the porter copy.

"Do you remember Teren Valeric being present?"

"At the transfer? Yes."

Ilyan's stomach tightened.

"Handling Crate Seventeen?"

"No."

"Handling the packet?"

"No."

"Do you remember him knowing there was a problem?"

Saren considered.

"I remember swearing at three people. Teren may have been one of them. That does not mean he knew what was inside."

Vaun nodded.

"Good."

Saren looked mildly offended.

"I was an archive receiving officer. We were trained not to improve memory for visitors."

Thessa's mouth twitched.

"I like him."

"You like everyone who tells Ilyan he is being impatient," Vaun said.

"A growing population."

Ilyan looked at the pear tree and chose survival.

Vaun asked, "Did you ever encounter Mera Holt after leaving North Quay?"

Saren's amusement disappeared.

"Once."

Ilyan forgot the pear tree.

"When?"

Vaun glanced at him.

He shut his mouth.

Saren rubbed his thumb over the red cord on his pruning knife.

"Before I transferred. She asked whether I remembered signing comparison releases."

"Tal's specifically?"

"No."

"Why did she ask?"

"She said some returned lots were lighter than their reconciliations. I told her that if the releases were receipted, the property wasn't missing. It was transferred."

"Did she seem frightened?"

Saren looked at Vaun.

"She seemed angry."

That was different.

Useful because it was different.

"At whom?"

"The office. Hers. Theirs. Mine. Government generally. Holt had range."

Thessa looked down to hide a smile.

Saren continued.

"She said a receipt proved who had been allowed to take something. It did not prove where the thing went afterward."

Ilyan thought of the blank return field.

"Did you follow up?"

"No. I was a deputy stores clerk. Then I transferred. I thought she was arguing about reconciliation practice."

"Did you know she later visited Mara Venn?"

"No."

"Selwyn Arven?"

"Knew him. Didn't know he'd asked Holt about the weights."

Vaun closed her notebook.

"One last question. Did you ever see the Joint Office use comparative property for credential work connected to protected reviews?"

Saren's face became careful.

"Sometimes protected reviews needed exemplars."

"Revision Seventeen?"

"I don't know."

"Silver Night?"

"I don't know."

"Dmitrigical Review?"

"I remember the name. I don't remember a property request I can tie to it."

Vaun stood.

"Thank you."

Saren took off his spectacles.

"That's it?"

"For today."

He looked at Ilyan.

"Your uncle kept the porter sheet?"

"Yes."

"Tell him he still owes me two crowns."

Ilyan blinked.

"For what?"

"Lunch."

"Eighteen years ago?"

"Interest."

Thessa laughed all the way to the gate.

---

The Hall answered one of Vaun's requests before they returned.

Not the master successor register.

The receiving inventory.

Examiner Sael was waiting in the registration chamber with a grey case on the table and an expression suggesting the case had personally inconvenienced him.

Ilyan knew better than to ask whether he had found it quickly.

Sael opened the case.

"Restricted Annex C receiving inventory. Dissolution intake. The judge's order permits inspection of entries tied to the Joint Office transfer only."

Vaun showed him the porter copy and Saren verification.

Sael read both.

"Aric Saren."

"Same man," Vaun said.

Sael nodded once.

The inventory was narrower than Ilyan expected. Crate numbers occupied the left column. Receiving seals occupied the right. Between them sat bundle counts and property-slip ranges.

Crate Fourteen.

Fifteen.

Sixteen.

Protected review continuations, each referenced to docket ranges they could not yet inspect without the master successor register.

Then Seventeen.

**Retained comparative property / credential exemplars. Twenty-one sealed property bundles. One intake exception — see Annex C Exception Register, entry 17-4.**

Ilyan read the line once.

Then again.

Thessa stood beside him, close enough that her sleeve touched his.

Nobody declared victory.

The inventory did not identify Tal's die.

It did not identify the mispacked packet.

It did not connect Revision Seventeen to Crate Seventeen.

It did something smaller and better.

It proved Saren's remembered exception had existed on paper.

Vaun looked at Sael.

"The Exception Register."

"Already requested."

Ilyan looked at him.

Sael raised one eyebrow.

"The inventory told me to request it."

"Of course it did."

"You sound disappointed."

Thessa made an undignified noise beside him.

Ilyan refused to look at her.

Sael turned the page.

"The master successor register is held under inherited restriction. Your judge's request has been referred to the current custodian."

"Who?" Ilyan asked.

Sael looked at Vaun rather than him.

"Truth Beyond the Veil."

The room went quiet.

Ilyan felt the answer land in several places at once.

His order.

His oath.

His family's old language inside it.

The investigation he had entered because he wanted somewhere truth might remain truth under necessity.

Vaun said, "Which office within the order?"

"Office of Restricted Testimony and Successor Custody."

"Who heads it?"

Sael's expression did not change.

"Examiner Halwen Serrat."

Ilyan knew the name.

Not well.

Everyone newly sworn knew it.

Serrat had administered the closed portion of their induction examinations.

He had also been the examiner who told Ilyan, three months before graduation, that family reputation was neither evidence for nor evidence against a candidate.

At the time Ilyan had admired him for it.

He still did.

That did not make the room simpler.

"Does Serrat know the request concerns me?" Ilyan asked.

Sael answered carefully.

"The request identifies the records sought and Judge Vaun's authority. It does not need to explain your family history. Your name appears on the inspection team."

So yes, if Serrat read it.

Ilyan nodded.

Vaun closed the inventory.

"We wait for the exception register and Serrat's response."

Ilyan hated waiting.

He also understood why they had to.

That was becoming an irritating feature of adulthood.

---

Damaris was at Reedglass when Ilyan returned after dusk.

Rhyl sat on the carpet with his back against the sofa, repairing a torn glove with the concentration of a surgeon and the skill of a man who had clearly done this badly many times before.

Damaris had both feet tucked beneath her and was reading a River Ledger circular.

She looked up when Ilyan entered.

"Saren?"

"Same man."

Rhyl's needle stopped.

Ilyan told them the rest.

Not every word of the interview.

Enough.

The mispacked packet. The blue exception register. The receiving inventory. Serrat.

Damaris listened without interrupting.

When he finished, she folded the circular.

"How do you feel about Serrat holding it?"

Ilyan sat opposite her.

"I don't know."

"Useful answer."

"Everyone is becoming Sael."

Rhyl bit off a thread.

"Terrifying."

Damaris's mouth curved.

Then she became serious again.

"Do you trust Serrat?"

"Professionally? More than most people. Personally? I barely know him."

"Do you think he trusts you?"

Ilyan thought about that.

"I think he believes I passed the examinations."

Rhyl looked up.

"That's not the same thing."

"No."

Damaris nodded.

No one told him what to do with that.

From the hall came Meris's voice asking a servant whether anyone had remembered to feed the investigators.

Rhyl looked at Damaris.

Damaris looked at Rhyl.

Ilyan watched the look pass between them and understood absolutely none of its private detail except that it existed.

That, too, was becoming ordinary.

He leaned back.

"Saren says Teren owes him two crowns."

Damaris blinked.

"Evidence?"

"Lunch."

Rhyl laughed.

Meris entered carrying a tray herself.

"What did I miss?"

"Eighteen years of compound interest," Ilyan said.

For the first time that day, waiting did not feel entirely like failure.

## The Blue Book

The blue book arrived before Examiner Serrat did.

It was not blue anymore.

Eighteen years of dust, handling and the slow indignities of archival storage had faded the cloth cover to something between storm-grey and old lavender. One corner had been repaired with darker linen. A brass label frame held a handwritten card:

**ANNEX C — INTAKE EXCEPTIONS — DISSOLUTION TRANSFERS**

Ilyan stood on the other side of the inspection table and tried not to resent an object for looking ordinary.

Thessa noticed.

"You expected lightning?"

"No."

"A skull worked into the clasp?"

"No."

"Blood?"

"Thessa."

She folded her hands behind her back, entirely unrepentant.

Judge Vaun was speaking with the Hall registrar at the door. Examiner Sael had already checked the order twice, the register seal once, and Ilyan's patience more often than either of them strictly required.

Rain silvered the high windows. Bloomtide had spent three days threatening warmth and then changing its mind.

Sael put a narrow paper marker beside the book.

"Entry seventeen-four only."

Ilyan nodded.

"I heard you the first time."

"Then today is full of pleasant surprises."

Thessa looked away very quickly.

Ilyan gave Sael the expression he deserved.

Sael opened the register.

The entries were short.

Broken wax. Wrong crate number. A property bundle delivered to Annex B and walked back across the courtyard under two signatures. A locked box whose key had arrived in another cart. Somebody had packed a clerk's lunch tin inside a records crate and, judging by the annotation, had never recovered it.

Then entry 17-4.

Ilyan stopped breathing for a moment.

Not because the entry was dramatic.

Because it was specific.

**Crate 17 — comparative property intake. Weight excess on porter verification. One paper packet found between property bundles 17-11 and 17-12. Packet exterior: Credential Review, defect-inheritance comparisons, case CR-88. Three sheets under old review cord. Removed from property intake by Receiving Officer A. Saren. Re-receipted to Protected Review Continuation 16-2 pending successor disposition. Receiving clerk: N. Elsen.**

Below that, in another hand:

**Accepted to 16-2. Cord intact. No contents inspection at Annex C intake.**

A receiving mark followed.

No name Ilyan knew.

Thessa leaned closer without touching the page.

"Three sheets."

Vaun had returned to the table.

"Yes."

"Selwyn remembered three comparison impressions."

"He did."

"And his comparison sheet was missing when the old case came back."

"Yes."

Thessa looked at the entry again.

"Same sheet?"

Vaun's answer came immediately.

"We don't know."

Thessa nodded.

No argument.

Ilyan read the words **defect-inheritance comparisons** again.

Selwyn had used that phrase.

Not exactly. Close enough to make his skin tighten and not close enough to permit the conclusion he wanted.

"CR-88," he said.

Sael had already written it down.

"Credential Review case number."

"Can we retrieve the old index?"

"Requested."

Ilyan looked at him.

Sael looked back.

Thessa murmured, "He keeps doing that to you."

"I know."

Vaun pointed to the second line.

"More important first. Protected Review Continuation sixteen-two."

The three of them looked at it.

Crates Fourteen through Sixteen had contained active protected-review continuations when the Joint Office dissolved.

Crate Seventeen had contained comparative property.

The packet had crossed that boundary during intake.

Not secretly.

Not necessarily improperly.

Under a receipt.

Ilyan had begun to hate receipts in ways he had not known were available to him.

"If the master successor register maps sixteen-two," he said, "we know where the packet went."

"If the mapping survives," Vaun said.

"If Serrat permits it."

Sael's eyes moved toward him.

Ilyan heard his own phrasing after it was too late.

He had made Serrat the obstacle before Serrat had done anything.

He exhaled.

"If the restriction permits inspection under the judge's order."

Sael said nothing.

That was worse than correction.

Thessa's shoulder touched Ilyan's.

Not comfort exactly.

A reminder that she was there.

He let it remain.

The registrar entered carrying a smaller folder.

"Credential Review index."

Sael took it.

The index was not restricted.

CR-88 appeared halfway down a page of old investigations.

**Comparative study — cancelled sponsor dies / inherited defect replication. Lead examiner: M. Venn. Supporting examiner: S. Arven. Status: superseded by joint review.**

Nobody spoke.

Ilyan felt the room narrow around the table.

Mara.

Selwyn.

The old counterfeit investigation.

The packet in Crate Seventeen was not merely something with similar language.

It belonged to their case.

Vaun rested one finger beside the index entry.

"Now we know which case the packet came from."

Thessa said, "Still not what was on the three sheets."

Vaun looked at her.

"Correct."

Thessa smiled faintly.

"I'm learning."

"Dangerous habit."

Ilyan almost laughed.

Then the far door opened.

Examiner Halwen Serrat entered alone.

---

Ilyan remembered Serrat as a man who made silence feel graded.

He had not changed much in three months.

Tall, narrow-shouldered, dark hair going silver at the temples, robes cut without ornament except for the black-and-pearl clasp of Restricted Testimony. His face was neither severe nor kind. It was attentive in a way that made careless speech feel expensive.

During Ilyan's induction examination Serrat had asked him whether a witness from a disgraced House should be presumed compromised.

Ilyan had answered no.

Serrat had asked whether a witness from an honored House should be presumed reliable.

Ilyan had answered no again.

Serrat had nodded and moved to the next question.

At the time, Ilyan had thought that exchange reassuring.

Now Serrat held a register that might contain a path through his family's crime.

Reassurance had become more complicated.

Serrat greeted Vaun first.

"Judge."

"Examiner."

Then Sael.

Then Thessa.

Then Ilyan.

"Valeric."

"Examiner."

No recognition beyond what was professionally required.

Ilyan was absurdly grateful for it.

Serrat placed a sealed folio on the table.

"I have reviewed the order and the inherited restriction."

Vaun waited.

"The restriction does not bar judicial inspection. It bars unrestricted duplication and disclosure of protected successor identities. I can permit the docket mapping relevant to the transfer you identified. Names outside the requested chain remain masked unless the judge establishes necessity."

No obstruction.

No favor either.

Procedure.

Ilyan felt something in his chest loosen and immediately distrusted the relief.

Serrat noticed the blue register.

"You found the exception."

Vaun turned it toward him.

He read entry 17-4 once.

"That narrows the request."

"It does."

Serrat broke the folio seal.

Inside lay a certified successor extract rather than the master register itself.

Ilyan understood why before anyone explained it.

The extract showed only the requested Joint Office continuations and their successor destinations.

Crate Fourteen had been divided among two judicial archives.

Crate Fifteen had gone partly to Crown emergency records and partly to Hall restricted testimony.

Crate Sixteen remained almost entirely under Truth Beyond the Veil successor custody.

The docket list ran down the page.

16-1.

16-2.

16-3.

Ilyan found the second line.

**16-2 — Credential Integrity Support / Emergency Substitution Review. Successor docket: RSV-17-S. Supporting materials retained under restricted testimony custody.**

His mouth went dry.

"RSV-17-S."

Serrat nodded.

"Revision Seventeen support docket."

Thessa went utterly still beside him.

Vaun did not.

"What does 'Credential Integrity Support' mean in this docket?"

"The successor register does not define it."

"Does the support docket survive?"

"Yes."

"Can we inspect it?"

Serrat looked at the order.

"Not under the present scope."

Ilyan's relief vanished so quickly it almost embarrassed him.

Vaun merely asked, "Why?"

"Because the present order concerns successor mapping and transferred property. RSV-17-S contains restricted testimony and protected identities not yet shown relevant to your property chain."

"The exception register establishes that CR-88 material was transferred into 16-2."

"It establishes that a sealed packet bearing that exterior description was accepted to 16-2."

Vaun's eyes sharpened slightly.

Serrat continued.

"That is likely sufficient for a narrower supplemental order concerning the packet's receipt and disposition. It is not sufficient for me to open every testimony record in the docket."

Ilyan hated the answer.

He also knew it was the answer he would have admired if the records belonged to someone else.

That realization sat badly.

Thessa glanced at him.

She knew him well enough not to ask whether he was all right in front of three examiners and a judge.

Instead she said, "Does the successor extract show whether the packet remained in the docket?"

Serrat looked at her.

"No."

"Would the docket have an internal property or document receipt?"

"Yes."

"Then that's the next request."

Vaun said, "Exactly."

Thessa leaned back.

Ilyan looked at her.

She raised one eyebrow.

He had no idea when she had become good at this.

That was not true.

He knew exactly when.

The last week had been teaching all of them things they had never expected to need.

Serrat closed the successor extract.

Ilyan said, "Examiner."

Serrat waited.

"Did you know my family was connected to Revision Seventeen when you examined me?"

Vaun did not interrupt.

Neither did Sael.

Serrat's expression remained unreadable.

"I knew your surname."

"That isn't what I asked."

"No."

A pause.

"I knew Revision Seventeen had Valeric administrative contacts. I did not know what you knew about your father or uncle. I did not know whether those contacts were culpable. I still do not know that from the records before me."

Ilyan held his gaze.

"Why tell me family reputation was neither evidence for nor against me?"

"Because it isn't."

Nothing else.

No revelation that Serrat had secretly been protecting him.

No confession that the examination had been designed around his family.

Just the same answer he had given with his conduct months ago.

Ilyan looked down at the successor extract.

Professional respect remained.

Personal trust did not magically appear beside it.

But suspicion had changed shape.

That mattered.

Serrat said, "If Judge Vaun obtains the supplemental order, I will execute it."

Vaun was already writing.

"You will have it."

"Then I expect we will speak again."

He resealed the extract into its folio.

At the door he stopped.

"Valeric."

Ilyan looked up.

"Yes?"

"Do not confuse discovering that your institution possesses an ugly record with discovering that your institution created the ugliness."

Ilyan felt irritation flare.

Serrat's face did not change.

"And do not confuse custody with innocence either."

Then he left.

Thessa waited until the door closed.

"I understand why you find him irritating."

Sael said, "You find everyone irritating."

"Not everyone."

She looked at Ilyan.

"Only the educational ones."

This time he did laugh.

Damaris was not there.

Rhyl was.

He had been sent by Meris with a basket of food and had apparently decided that delivering it required eating some of it himself.

"Where is she?" Thessa asked.

"River Ledger. Maeryn found three ways to make yesterday's luncheon continue after it ended."

"That sounds ominous."

"One of them is a committee invitation."

"Terrifying."

Rhyl handed her a pastry.

Thessa accepted it without shame.

Ilyan put the certified CR-88 index copy on the table.

Rhyl stopped chewing.

"You found it."

"We found where the packet was sent."

He explained.

Rhyl listened with the particular stillness he used in the woods when he had heard something beyond ordinary hearing.

When Ilyan reached RSV-17-S, Rhyl sat down.

"So the old counterfeit comparison material went into Revision Seventeen support custody."

"A packet labeled as CR-88 defect-inheritance comparisons went into the protected review continuation that became the Revision Seventeen support docket."

Rhyl looked at him.

"Sael has infected you too."

"Apparently."

"Good."

Ilyan frowned.

Rhyl took another bite.

"Annoying, but good."

Thessa sat on the edge of the table.

"We still don't know whether the packet was the missing three-impression sheet."

"But CR-88 was Mara and Selwyn's case," Rhyl said.

"Yes."

"And the packet was moved into Revision Seventeen support."

"Yes."

"Before or after Revision Seventeen was written?"

Nobody answered.

Vaun looked up from Mara's statement.

That was the first question of the day that changed the room.

Ilyan turned toward her.

"The dissolution transfer was years later."

"Obviously," Rhyl said. "I mean when the Joint Office first put CR-88 material with the protected review. The Annex transfer only tells us they were together by dissolution."

Thessa lowered the pastry.

"So the packet could have joined 16-2 years after Revision Seventeen."

"Or before," Ilyan said.

Vaun nodded.

"Now we have the right question."

Not who used the old counterfeit research to create Revision Seventeen.

Not whether the missing comparison sheet had caused Silver Night.

When did CR-88 material enter the protected-review chain that eventually became RSV-17-S?

The answer could destroy the apparent connection.

Or make it much worse.

Vaun amended the supplemental request.

She asked for the packet's first receipt into protected-review custody, every internal transfer attached to that packet identifier, and its final disposition.

Nothing broader.

Nothing theatrical.

Ilyan watched her sand the page.

"How long?"

"If Serrat moves quickly? Tomorrow."

"And if he doesn't?"

"Tomorrow afternoon, because I will go stand in his office."

Thessa smiled.

"I like you."

Vaun did not look up.

"A regrettable lapse in judgment."

Rhyl offered the judge a pastry.

She took one.

That somehow made the whole thing more alarming.

Damaris returned after sunset carrying a committee packet thick enough to injure someone.

Rhyl met her in the entrance hall.

Ilyan saw them from the library doorway.

Damaris said something he could not hear.

Rhyl took the packet from her.

She caught the front of his coat and kissed him before he had finished setting it on the side table.

Not a ceremonial kiss.

Not one designed for witnesses.

The sort that made Rhyl forget, briefly, that he was holding several pounds of River Ledger bureaucracy against his hip.

When she let him go, he looked pleasantly disoriented.

Damaris noticed Ilyan.

"How long have you been standing there?"

"Long enough to learn that committee service is apparently exciting."

"It isn't. That's why this was necessary."

Rhyl looked at the packet.

"Should I be jealous of municipal finance?"

"Constantly."

She took his hand and came into the library.

Thessa had gone back to the Warden house. Vaun had taken the supplemental request to the Hall. Meris was upstairs with Aereth.

For once the room belonged mostly to friends.

Ilyan told Damaris what they had found.

Her expression changed at CR-88.

Then again at RSV-17-S.

She sat beside Rhyl.

"Does Mara know?"

"Not yet. Vaun wants to tell her after the supplemental order is filed."

"Good."

Ilyan looked at her committee packet.

"Maeryn?"

Damaris groaned.

"Maeryn has decided that if I insist on being publicly visible, I may as well be useful in public. River transport reconciliation committee. Observer seat."

Rhyl said, "She made it sound like an honor."

"It is an honor. That's the problem."

Ilyan smiled.

Damaris pointed at him.

"You have your own institutional nightmare. Do not enjoy mine."

"Serrat cooperated."

That surprised her.

"Entirely?"

"Within the order. He refused unrestricted docket access and explained why."

"Do you believe him?"

Ilyan considered.

A week ago he might have answered the wrong question.

"I believe the restriction exists. I believe he applied it consistently with what I've seen from him before. I don't know what he knows about the underlying records."

Damaris nodded.

"Better."

"Everyone keeps saying that when I become more annoying."

Rhyl leaned back.

"It's adulthood."

"I was told there would be freedom."

"Who told you that?"

"No one reliable."

Damaris laughed.

For several minutes they did nothing useful.

Rhyl opened the committee packet and made horrified noises at a table of barge-loss adjustments. Damaris tried to take it back. He held it over his head. She climbed half across him to reach it, which defeated whatever practical advantage his longer arms had provided and created several new problems neither seemed eager to solve.

Ilyan looked away before friendship required commentary.

The rain had stopped.

Beyond the window, Reedglass's orchard shone black and silver beneath the cloud-broken moon.

Somewhere in the Hall of Veils, a supplemental order was moving toward Examiner Serrat.

Somewhere behind inherited restrictions, a receipt might still say when a packet from an old counterfeit investigation entered the protected review that became Revision Seventeen's support file.

Tomorrow they might learn that the connection was late, administrative and meaningless.

Ilyan wanted that possibility to remain real.

He was no longer sure he expected it.

## The First Receipt

Examiner Serrat sent for Ilyan before breakfast.

The message arrived at Reedglass folded into a narrow black envelope with no seal beyond the Hall's ordinary receiving stamp. Ilyan found it beside his tea while Rhyl was attempting to convince Damaris that the River transport committee could not possibly require a packet thick enough to stun livestock.

"It has bridge schedules," Damaris said.

"It has ambitions."

"It has appendices."

"That is what I said."

Damaris kicked him lightly beneath the table.

Rhyl smiled into his cup.

Ilyan opened the note.

Thessa watched his face from across the table.

"Good?"

"Serrat has the supplemental order."

"That isn't what I asked."

Ilyan read the last line again.

**The requested packet history survives. Attend Restricted Testimony at second bell if you wish to witness inspection.**

"I don't know yet."

Thessa reached for another piece of toast.

"Better."

Damaris had already stopped pretending to read her committee agenda.

"The packet itself?"

"He says the history survives. Not the packet."

She nodded once.

No one made the sentence carry more than it did.

That had become a habit among them. An irritating one. A necessary one.

Rhyl leaned back in his chair.

"I'm taking her to the Ledger."

Damaris looked at him.

"You are walking in the same direction as me."

"With exceptional professionalism."

"You kissed me against the canal wall yesterday."

Thessa looked delighted.

Rhyl considered this.

"Professionally."

Damaris's mouth twitched despite herself.

Ilyan looked down before he smiled. There was something indecently comforting in the fact that the world still contained ridiculous people before second bell.

Then he folded Serrat's note and put it inside his coat.

---

Restricted Testimony occupied the oldest eastern range of the Hall of Veils, where the windows were too narrow for fashionable architecture and the doors had been built when iron was cheaper than trust.

Thessa came with him.

She had not asked whether she should.

Ilyan had not asked her to stop.

Judge Vaun was already in the inspection room when they arrived. Examiner Sael stood beside her with a cup of something that smelled strong enough to remove varnish. Serrat had laid three folders on the table.

No blue book today.

No dramatic crate.

Three folders, two pencils, a sand tray and a clerk who looked as though he had been born disappointed by handwriting.

Serrat indicated the chairs.

"The supplemental order authorizes the packet-specific receipt chain, transfer annotations and disposition record. It does not authorize inspection of unrelated testimony in RSV-17-S."

Vaun sat.

"Understood."

Ilyan sat beside Thessa.

Serrat opened the first folder.

"CR-88 entered Joint Office protected-review custody on Bloomtide twenty-third, two hundred sixty-four Sunder Moon."

Ilyan stared at him.

He knew the date before he finished converting it.

"Before Revision Seventeen."

"Yes."

Sael set down his cup.

"How long before?"

The clerk slid a certified chronology across the table.

Revision Seventeen had been filed seventeen days before Silver Night in the current year.

CR-88 had entered protected review eighteen years earlier.

The apparent answer should have been disappointing.

Instead Ilyan felt his pulse slow.

Eighteen years meant the packet had not been slipped into the review chain immediately before Silver Night to manufacture a convenient credential. Whatever had happened was older.

Older did not mean harmless.

It meant the question had changed.

Vaun read the first receipt.

"Reason for transfer?"

Serrat turned the page.

**Comparative methods retained for recurrent credential-defect reference. Joint review requested after sponsor-die defect reproduced across nonmatching impressions.**

Thessa leaned forward.

"Recurrent reference."

"Yes," Vaun said.

Ilyan read the line again.

The old case had not vanished into a conspiracy cupboard.

Someone had considered it useful enough to keep.

"Who requested joint review?"

Serrat looked to the clerk.

The clerk produced another slip.

**Office of Credential Integrity, technical referral. Reviewing liaison: Joint Order Liaison Desk.**

No personal name.

No Dmitrigical Review.

No Valeric.

Ilyan was surprised by the relief and disliked himself for it.

Thessa noticed that too. She said nothing.

Serrat opened the second folder.

"The packet remained under comparative-method reference for sixteen years. It was consulted nine times. Eight consultations concern credential forgery, sponsor-seal replication or inherited die damage. The ninth is the transfer relevant to your order."

There it was.

The clerk placed a single certified slip in front of Vaun.

The date was twenty-nine days before Silver Night.

Twelve days before Revision Seventeen was filed.

Ilyan's mouth went dry.

**Temporary transfer: CR-88 defect-inheritance comparison packet. From Credential Integrity reference custody to Emergency Substitution Review working file. Purpose: assess reproducibility of inherited sponsor-seal defects under substituted witness-credential procedure. Return required at review closure.**

Below it:

**Requested through Joint Order Liaison — Dmitrigical Review.**

Nobody spoke for several breaths.

Thessa's hand closed around the edge of her chair.

Ilyan looked at Vaun.

She was reading the line a second time.

"Twelve days," he said.

Sael answered him.

"Before Revision Seventeen was filed."

"Yes."

The room seemed suddenly too small.

The old counterfeit case had not merely been associated with Revision Seventeen years later when the Joint Office dissolved.

Its comparison packet had been deliberately borrowed into the emergency substitution review before Revision Seventeen existed.

That still did not tell them why.

It did not tell them what the three sheets contained.

It did not prove the procedure had been designed to facilitate Silver Night.

But the chronology no longer allowed the comfortable explanation that the two files had merely been shelved together afterward.

Vaun looked at Serrat.

"Return?"

Serrat opened the third folder.

"Not at review closure."

Ilyan felt Thessa turn slightly beside him.

The disposition record was shorter.

**Review closed: Revision Seventeen adopted. Supporting comparative packet retained under RSV-17-S pending credential-integrity implementation audit.**

A second notation followed three months later.

**Original CR-88 comparison packet returned to Credential Integrity reference custody. Certified working copy retained in RSV-17-S.**

Ilyan stopped.

"Copy."

"Yes," Serrat said.

"So the packet in Crate Seventeen at dissolution—"

"Was the original returned to Credential Integrity custody."

Thessa finished the thought.

"And Annex C put it back into sixteen-two because the exception clerk saw it belonged to the protected review that had retained a copy."

Serrat nodded.

"That is what the surviving transfer annotations indicate."

Vaun tapped the disposition line.

"Does the certified working copy survive in RSV-17-S?"

"Yes."

Ilyan looked at him.

Serrat did not make him ask twice.

"The present order establishes relevance to the packet chain. I will permit inspection of that copy's three sheets under the same restriction: no unrelated testimony, no protected identities beyond what appears on the sheets themselves."

Vaun's expression did not change.

"Today?"

"The copy is being brought from lower custody."

Thessa exhaled through her nose.

"You could have led with that."

Serrat regarded her.

"Then you would have listened less carefully to the chronology."

Thessa looked at Ilyan.

"I continue to understand why you dislike him."

Sael drank from his cup.

"Educational people."

"Exactly."

Ilyan almost laughed, but his eyes had returned to the date.

Twenty-nine days before Silver Night.

The old counterfeit research entered the emergency substitution review.

Seventeen days before Silver Night, Revision Seventeen was filed.

Twelve days before Silver Night, it took effect.

Then the west-tower witness-material chain began.

He could feel his mind trying to draw the whole shape at once.

His father.

His uncle.

Dmitrigical Review.

The altered oath.

The witness screens.

The Academy dead.

Vaun's pencil touched the table once.

Ilyan looked up.

"Don't," she said.

One word.

Enough.

He sat back.

"I know."

"Do you?"

He looked again at the transfer slip.

"I know the packet was requested into the review before Revision Seventeen. I know the request passed through the Dmitrigical liaison. I don't know who made the request. I don't know whether the method was used to build a fraudulent credential. I don't know whether this had anything to do with the Academy delivery."

Vaun nodded.

"Better."

Thessa's knee touched his beneath the table.

He let it stay there.

---

The working copy arrived under two seals.

Serrat inspected both before the clerk opened the outer case.

Three sheets lay beneath old review cord, each protected by a translucent archival leaf.

Ilyan had expected diagrams.

There were some.

Mostly there were impressions.

Sponsor seals pressed into black wax. Enlarged tracings of tiny defects along the crescent edge. Measurements. Notes in two hands.

The first sheet was Mara Venn's.

The second was Selwyn Arven's.

The third bore no examiner's name on its face, only a comparison grid and a notation in the lower corner.

**Replication trial: inherited flaw reproduced from secondary impression without access to parent die.**

Thessa went very still.

Ilyan read it again.

Without access to parent die.

Vaun bent closer.

"Method?"

Serrat turned the archival leaf just enough for the lower section to be visible.

The notes described a process, but not a simple one: layered impression transfer, compensating pressure, a mineral suspension used to exaggerate shallow defects, then a second-stage cut made from the transferred pattern rather than the original die.

Ilyan understood perhaps half of it.

Enough to understand the danger.

A forged sponsor seal did not need the sponsor's original die if the forger possessed a sufficiently good impression and knew how inherited defects propagated.

The dead freight assessor's duplicated seal on Eren Vale's credential returned to him with nauseating clarity.

Thessa whispered, "Oh."

Vaun did not correct her.

The third sheet carried a later annotation in different ink.

**Applicable to emergency substituted witness credentials where parent authorization cannot be physically presented. Defect continuity may satisfy visual lineage checks if secondary reference is accepted.**

Ilyan's hands went cold.

That was not a confession.

It was worse in a quieter way.

It was a technical answer to a bureaucratic problem.

How to make a substitute credential appear descended from a legitimate authorization when the original die was unavailable.

Serrat's face had changed.

Only slightly.

The attentive neutrality remained, but the distance was gone from his eyes.

He had not known what was on the sheets either.

Ilyan realized that before Serrat said anything.

"This annotation is not original to CR-88."

Vaun looked at him.

"How do you know?"

"Different ink. Different hand. And it references emergency substituted witness credentials, which did not exist under that terminology when CR-88 was opened."

"Can you date it?"

"Not from sight."

Sael leaned over the table without touching it.

"Initials?"

There were two at the edge of the annotation.

**T.V.**

Ilyan stared.

Not Teren.

Not any name he knew.

Vaun wrote them down.

"We identify the hand before we identify the person."

Ilyan nodded.

He was grateful she had said it before his family name could manufacture another suspect for him.

The second sheet held Selwyn's three comparison impressions.

There was no question this time.

The missing comparison sheet had survived.

Not in the old case file.

Here.

Copied into the support docket of Revision Seventeen.

Selwyn's handwritten note ran along the bottom margin:

**All three retain identical crescent break despite nonmatching pressure artifacts. Replication is procedural, not coincidental.**

Thessa looked at Ilyan.

"So this is it."

"This is Selwyn's sheet," Vaun said. "Yes."

A conclusion at last.

Small enough to survive.

Serrat looked toward the clerk.

"Seal the room log. No further access to RSV-17-S without my countersignature or Judge Vaun's order until provenance on the later annotation is established."

The clerk nodded and left.

Ilyan watched Serrat.

"You didn't know."

Serrat met his eyes.

"No."

Ilyan believed him.

That was not the same as trusting him.

It was one fact.

He could live with one fact.

For the moment.

Damaris found them outside the Hall at midday.

She had escaped the transport committee carrying fewer papers than she had entered with, which Rhyl declared a political triumph.

He was beside her, close enough that their sleeves brushed whenever they walked.

Damaris saw Ilyan's face and stopped teasing him before she had begun.

"What happened?"

They crossed to the quieter side of the court.

Ilyan told them.

Not every technical line.

Enough.

CR-88 had entered protected review eighteen years ago. Its packet had been borrowed into the emergency substitution review twenty-nine days before Silver Night. The request had passed through the Dmitrigical liaison. Revision Seventeen followed twelve days later. A copy remained in the support docket. Selwyn's missing comparison sheet was there. A later hand had annotated the method for emergency substituted witness credentials.

Damaris's face lost color.

Rhyl asked the useful question.

"Does it tell us how Eren Vale's credential was made?"

"No."

"Could it?"

"Yes."

"Different question."

"Yes."

Damaris looked toward the Hall doors.

"And the initials?"

"T.V. We don't know whose."

Rhyl's mouth tightened.

"That will produce an unpleasant number of guesses."

"Which is why we're not guessing."

Damaris looked at Ilyan for a long moment.

"How are you?"

He almost said fine.

She would not have believed him.

Thessa would have kicked him.

"Angry."

"At whom?"

"I haven't earned that yet."

Damaris nodded.

Then she stepped forward and hugged him.

It was not elegant. Her committee packet caught between them and dug into his ribs.

Ilyan laughed once despite himself.

"Ow."

"Sorry."

She moved the packet and hugged him again.

Rhyl looked away toward the rain-dark court, giving them privacy without going anywhere.

Thessa stayed at Ilyan's other shoulder.

The Seven were becoming very good at this strange new adult art: standing beside one another while refusing to pretend they could solve one another's lives.

When Damaris let him go, she said, "I have something less terrible."

"Please."

"The committee discovered that two bridge authorities have been billing the same dredging barge for three months."

Rhyl brightened.

"Crime?"

"Incompetence, probably."

"Tragic."

"Maeryn says I get to find out."

Ilyan smiled.

There it was again.

The world refusing to become only one thing.

Vaun emerged from the Hall behind them.

"Valeric."

Ilyan turned.

She held a fresh request slip.

"The annotation hand is next. Then we compare the CR-88 method against the fraudulent credential without consuming or altering it. Separately, we trace Tal's die."

Separately.

Still.

Ilyan nodded.

"And the Academy parcel?"

"Waits until the credential evidence earns it."

He had expected frustration.

Instead he felt relief.

One chain at a time.

Not because the chains were unconnected.

Because if they were connected, he wanted the connection to survive being hated.

Damaris slipped her hand into Rhyl's.

Thessa bumped Ilyan's shoulder with hers.

Rain began again over the Hall court, light enough that nobody moved for shelter.

## The Hand in the Margin

The handwriting room had six windows, no curtains and a table scarred by people who had been told not to press too hard.

Ilyan noticed the table first.

Thessa noticed him noticing it.

"You are becoming very exciting."

"I noticed furniture."

"Exactly. Another month in the Hall and you'll have opinions about storage cord."

"I already have opinions about storage cord."

She stopped beside him.

"That's worse."

Examiner Serrat, three paces ahead, did not turn around.

"Blue cord sheds fibers into wax impressions."

Thessa stared at his back.

"There are two of you now."

Judge Vaun entered behind them carrying the certified copy of the annotation under a stiff protective leaf.

"Sit down."

They did.

The Hall's script examiner was an elderly woman named Pellis Nareth whose left hand trembled until she put it on paper. Then it became perfectly still.

She did not ask why the annotation mattered.

Serrat gave her the question instead.

"Can the hand be associated with an archived office writer without attributing the substance of the note to anyone not demonstrated by the writing?"

Pellis looked over her spectacles.

"Yes."

"Can you do it today?"

"Possibly."

Thessa leaned toward Ilyan.

"I like her."

Pellis heard her.

"Most people do until I tell them their grandmother did not write the romantic letter they have built their personality around."

Thessa sat back.

"I like her more."

Pellis ignored this and examined the copied annotation.

The initials sat at the lower edge of the note.

**T.V.**

Ilyan had spent the previous night refusing to make names from them.

That had not prevented names from arriving anyway.

Teren Valeric had been the obvious one. So obvious that Ilyan distrusted it immediately. Teren's surviving service hand was square, heavily separated, the product of a man trained to make warehouse instructions legible to people carrying crates in rain. The annotation was narrower and ran forward at the top, impatient without becoming untidy.

Still, initials were cheap things. Families reused them. Offices accumulated them. Half the Realm seemed to contain a T or a V when one badly needed it not to.

Pellis set three transparent measuring strips over the copy.

"This isn't enough by itself."

Vaun nodded.

"What is?"

"Comparison samples. Preferably routine work. Not signatures. People perform signatures. I want notes they wrote while annoyed."

Serrat had anticipated that.

A clerk brought in two archive boxes.

The first held surviving technical-review sheets from Emergency Substitution Review. The second held Joint Office liaison notes from the same month.

Pellis worked through them without ceremony.

Ilyan watched hands emerge from the dead.

Some leaned backward. Some crowded the margins. One writer made every lower loop so broad that it threatened neighboring words. Another crossed every final stroke twice. The people were gone or retired or somewhere eating breakfast without knowing that four young adults and a judge were studying the way they once wrote *insufficient sample*.

Thessa slid a cup of water toward Ilyan.

He drank it.

She did not ask whether he was all right.

That helped.

After forty minutes Pellis stopped.

"This one."

She placed a routine defect-review note beside the annotation.

The note had been written twenty-eight days before Silver Night.

One day after CR-88 entered the Emergency Substitution Review working file.

The author line read:

**Tavian Veyr — Technical Examiner, Credential Integrity Liaison.**

Ilyan looked at the initials again.

T.V.

Thessa exhaled.

"Not Teren."

"No," Pellis said. "Unless Teren Valeric spent several years disguising his hand in routine technical work before anyone had reason to care, which is a theory you may entertain somewhere else."

Vaun's mouth moved slightly.

Not quite a smile.

"Strength?"

Pellis pointed with the blunt end of her pencil.

"Same formation of the capital A. Same open lower loop on the g. Same habit of beginning a second line beneath the final third of the first rather than returning to the margin. Same pressure break before a terminal t. There are twelve useful correspondences and no meaningful contradiction in the available samples."

"Conclusion?"

"The annotation is strongly consistent with Tavian Veyr's ordinary technical hand."

Not *was written by*.

Strongly consistent.

Ilyan had learned to love irritating language.

It survived contact with tomorrow.

"Is Veyr alive?" he asked.

Serrat opened a personnel extract.

Tavian Veyr had been seconded from Credential Integrity to the Emergency Substitution Review for eleven days.

Ilyan read the sentence twice.

That was almost exactly what the annotation addressed.

No secret office.

No midnight appointment.

A technical examiner had been assigned a technical problem and had written a technical answer.

The danger lay elsewhere.

Not flare.

Settle.

"You understand that resemblance is not identity?"

Ilyan nodded.

"And you?"

"I have been related to you for twenty-two years. I understand that you can make any sentence irritating."

"Excellent."

He began.

The CR-88 method had three observable consequences if used competently.

First, the inherited defect would be too stable across impressions made under different pressure because the flaw had been copied deliberately rather than generated naturally by the parent die.

Second, the secondary-stage cutting process tended to broaden the shallow side of the defect by a minute but measurable amount.

Third, the mineral suspension used to exaggerate the source impression could leave trace particulate in recessed cuts if the replica die was not thoroughly cleaned.

The first could be examined from existing images.

The second could be measured optically.

The third required light and magnification, not sampling.

No scraping.

No solvent.

No destruction.

Damaris stood beside Ilyan while Jalen and the Hall examiner worked.

Thessa stood on his other side.

He was beginning to suspect his friends had developed a formation.

The first result came quickly.

The crescent defect on Eren Vale's sponsor impression was too consistent with the surviving secondary reference impression.

Not identical.

Consistent beyond what ordinary pressure variation predicted.

The second took longer.

Jalen measured twice.

Then a third time.

"Broadening on the shallow edge," he said.

The Hall examiner checked his scale.

"Within CR-88 trial range."

Nobody spoke.

The third result required a lamp angled almost flat across the wax.

At first Ilyan saw nothing.

Then the examiner changed lenses.

Tiny pale grains caught the light in the recessed crescent.

Jalen's expression changed.

Damaris knew him well enough to notice.

"Possibly nothing."

"Jalen."

"The particles are where CR-88 predicts residue could survive. That does not tell us what they are."

"Can you tell?"

"Not without sampling."

Vaun answered before anyone else could.

"Then we don't."

Jalen nodded.

The credential remained whole.

Ilyan looked through the glass.

Eren Vale's false name sat above a sponsor lineage that had survived inspection because it looked old enough to be trusted.

The dead assessor had never sponsored him.

The original die had not been required.

The credential carried two observable features predicted by CR-88 and a third possible feature they would not damage the evidence to confirm.

Jalen folded his arms.

"Strongly consistent with the CR-88 replication method or a materially similar derivative. Not unique attribution. Another competent examiner could have independently developed comparable technique."

Serrat said, "But this method was in the Emergency Substitution Review before Revision Seventeen."

"Yes."

Damaris answered.

"Yes."

It was simply what happened when everyone had reached the same uncomfortable place and nobody needed it explained.

Vaun broke it.

"Now I can ask for the roster."

Ilyan looked at her.

She was already writing.

Ordinary.

The separate chain.

## The Roster and the Die

The senior countersignature arrived before breakfast.

Ilyan learned this because Examiner Serrat appeared at the Hall refectory while he was halfway through a bowl of oat porridge and set a folded notice beside his spoon.

Serrat did not sit.

"Nine o'clock. Restricted Testimony, room four."

Ilyan looked at the notice.

"The roster?"

"The portion covered by Judge Vaun's showing."

"Which portion?"

"You'll find out at nine."

Serrat walked away.

Thessa, across the table, watched him go.

"He enjoys that."

"No."

"You answered too quickly."

"He enjoys rules."

"Same thing with worse parties."

Ilyan folded the notice and put it inside his coat.

He had slept badly.

Not dramatically badly. There had been no prophetic nightmare, no dead classmates standing at the foot of his bed, no mysterious tapping at the window. He had simply woken every hour and discovered that his mind had continued working without him.

Tavian Veyr had been asked a technical question.

Veyr had answered it.

The answer could reproduce an inherited sponsor defect from a secondary impression.

Eren Vale's fraudulent credential carried the expected marks of that method or something very much like it.

Somebody had asked the question.

The sealed roster knew who had occupied the relevant desks.

That was all.

It was enough to ruin porridge.

Thessa reached across the table and stole a slice of pear from his plate.

"You have your own breakfast."

"Mine lacks pear."

"Because you ate it."

"A temporary condition."

He looked at her.

She ate the pear.

There were things about friendship the Academy had never properly covered.

At a quarter to nine, Judge Vaun arrived carrying nothing but her gloves. Examiner Sael followed with a cup that smelled strongly enough to suggest a personal grievance against tea.

Thessa stood.

Vaun looked at her.

"You aren't named on the order."

Thessa nodded once.

No complaint. No attempt to convert friendship into access.

"I'll be downstairs."

Ilyan had expected relief at not having her in the room.

Instead he wanted, with embarrassing intensity, to ask her to stay.

He did not.

She touched two fingers briefly to his wrist.

"Eat something afterward."

"I just ate."

"You moved porridge around a bowl."

"That counts."

"It doesn't."

Then she left.

Vaun waited until the door shut.

"Ready?"

No.

"Yes."

She gave him the look judges reserved for answers that were legally sufficient and otherwise useless.

"Good."

Restricted Testimony room four contained a table, four chairs and a window too high to look through while seated.

Serrat was already there.

So was a woman Ilyan had never met, perhaps seventy, with iron-grey hair braided tightly against her head and the narrow black collar of a senior successor-custody examiner. She introduced herself as Examiner Ressa Talven and did not offer a hand.

On the table lay a single sheet beneath glass.

Not the roster itself.

An extract.

Talven looked first at Vaun.

"Your order establishes necessity for identities attached to the request, technical review, custody liaison and approval path for the CR-88 transfer into Emergency Substitution Review. It does not establish necessity for unrelated witnesses, protected testimony sources or later disciplinary annotations."

"Agreed," Vaun said.

Talven looked at Ilyan.

"If you recognize a family name, that does not enlarge the order."

He felt heat rise under his collar.

"Understood."

She slid the extract across the table.

There were four lines.

**Requesting desk:** Dmitrigical Review — Emergency Procedure Desk.

**Request entered by:** Clerk-Examiner Oren Savel.

**Credential Integrity technical examiner:** Tavian Veyr.

**Joint Order custody liaison:** Teren Valeric.

Ilyan read the last line twice.

Nothing inside him broke.

He had been waiting for something to break.

Instead there was only the ugly steadiness of a fact arriving where suspicion had already made room for it.

Teren.

Not as author.

Not as technical examiner.

Custody liaison.

The role he had already admitted holding.

Ilyan kept reading.

A fifth line sat below the four identities.

**Review authority:** Dmitrigical Tribunal Contingency Secretariat — delegated approval under emergency witness-continuity mandate.

No personal name.

Vaun leaned forward.

"Why is the approving individual absent?"

Talven tapped the lower line.

"The surviving roster records the office authority. The individual countersignature was stored in the Contingency Secretariat's own authorization book. That book did not transfer to this Hall."

"Does the roster identify who requested CR-88 specifically?"

"Savel entered the request."

"That wasn't my question."

Talven's eyes moved to Vaun's.

"No. The request field says only: *technical precedent concerning inherited credential defects under absent-parent conditions*."

Ilyan looked at Teren's name again.

"What did the custody liaison do?"

Serrat answered.

"Accepted the transfer into the Joint Office working compartment, maintained access custody and certified return or successor disposition."

"Would he have read it?"

"Not necessarily."

"Could he have?"

"If his access role required opening the compartment for an authorized examiner, yes. That does not mean he was authorized to inspect the technical material himself."

The distinction irritated Ilyan because it mattered.

He had wanted, for one exhausted second, for Teren's name to mean one thing.

It meant several smaller things instead.

Teren had been closer to CR-88 than he had admitted at the service chest.

Or perhaps closer than he understood Ilyan was asking.

Those were not the same accusation.

Vaun said, "Is Savel alive?"

Talven checked a note.

"Yes. Retired."

"Where?"

"That is outside the current extract, but ordinary personnel retirement records are not restricted testimony."

"Good."

Ilyan heard himself ask, "Did Teren renew the restriction on this roster?"

Serrat looked at him.

"No."

Ilyan's head came up.

"No?"

"The renewal you traced applied to Revision Seventeen's support docket and successor materials. This roster inherited that restriction because the CR-88 packet was attached to the support docket. Teren executed the renewal as the surviving Joint Office administrative contact. He did not create a separate restriction for this roster."

That changed something.

Not innocence.

Shape.

Teren's later signature had looked like a hand deliberately keeping a specific name buried. It might instead have been the administrative consequence of a broader compartment he had been responsible for maintaining.

Ilyan hated that too.

He had become remarkably good at hating accurate distinctions.

Vaun asked, "Can we establish whether Teren opened the CR-88 packet during the original review?"

Talven nodded toward Serrat.

"Access sheets."

Serrat produced another extract.

Tavian Veyr had opened the technical packet three times.

Oren Savel had signed it across desks twice without opening the inner technical leaves.

Teren Valeric had opened the working compartment on five dates, each corresponding to authorized review sessions.

The sheet did not say he read CR-88.

It did establish that he physically controlled the compartment while Veyr and others worked.

One name appeared beside Veyr on two of those sessions.

**Examiner Pel Ardis — witness-procedure implementation.**

Vaun pointed.

"New?"

Serrat nodded.

"To us."

Talven said, "Ardis died fourteen years ago. His surviving work may still be in Dmitrigical procedural archives."

Ilyan sat back.

Teren had not vanished from the problem.

He had become harder to simplify.

That felt, perversely, more like the uncle Ilyan knew.

The man who had taught him to keep warehouse chalk dry by carrying it inside the cuff. The man who had once spent an entire afternoon helping a twelve-year-old Ilyan rebuild a broken practice seal because Ilyan had been too proud to admit he had stepped on it. The man who had renewed a restriction after Silver Night. The man who had asked whether the restrictions remained intact. The man who had embraced him at Seven Lamps.

People did not become easier merely because one obtained better records.

Vaun turned the extract toward herself.

"We speak to Savel. We find Ardis's procedural work. We speak to Teren again after we know enough to ask him something narrower than whether he remembers a packet from eighteen years ago."

Ilyan nodded.

Talven collected the extract.

"You may retain the certified necessity copy. Not this one."

Vaun handed over the appropriate sleeve.

Nobody argued.

That, Ilyan thought, was probably how civilizations survived archivists.

Thessa was waiting on the lower steps with two honey rolls.

She handed him one.

"Well?"

Ilyan looked at the roll.

"Teren was the custody liaison."

She did not gasp.

She did not ask whether that meant he had done it.

"And?"

"A retired clerk-examiner named Oren Savel entered the request. Tavian Veyr did the technical work. A dead implementation examiner named Pel Ardis sat in on two sessions. Approval came from the Dmitrigical Tribunal Contingency Secretariat, but the personal countersignature is in a book the Hall never received."

Thessa bit into her roll.

"So your uncle is in it."

"He's in the room."

She chewed.

Then nodded.

"Different sentence."

"Yes."

They walked toward the Credential Integrity offices.

Rain had stopped sometime during the roster review. Bloomtide sun came through the cloud breaks in hard white shafts that made every wet roof shine. Street vendors had pulled covers off their stalls. Someone nearby was frying onion cakes. A pair of apprentices were arguing over whether a crate of river pears had been counted before or after one of them ate two.

The city continued being offensively alive.

Ilyan ate the honey roll.

Thessa noticed and said nothing.

Credential Integrity occupied three floors of a narrow building whose stone facade had been cleaned recently enough that the carved seals above the entrance looked surprised by daylight.

Judge Vaun met them there with Serrat.

The North Quay bundle was waiting.

Not the objects.

The ledger.

The surviving bundle register was bound in cracked brown hide and smelled faintly of dust and old oil. Its entries were less elegant than Hall records. Warehouse people had written them for warehouse people: numbers, weights, seals, destinations, initials.

Serrat found the line.

**NQ-C17-08.**

Received into Crate 17 with four comparison objects.

A damaged sponsor plate.

Two voided credential matrices.

One brass comparison die marked **TAL / LOT 44-C**.

Ilyan stopped breathing for a moment.

Vaun's finger remained beside the line.

There it was.

Not inferred from weight.

Not remembered by a witness twenty years later.

Written down.

Tal's die had been in the bundle.

The next notation recorded its transfer back to Credential Integrity during the implementation audit.

The receiving bench was **CI-4**.

The purpose field read:

**Inherited-defect comparison / substituted-parent verification.**

Thessa said, very softly, "That's the same problem."

"Yes," Ilyan said.

Vaun looked at Serrat.

"Who signed it out?"

Serrat turned the page.

The first signature belonged to Tavian Veyr.

The second belonged to Pel Ardis.

The third was not a name.

It was a bench return mark indicating all four objects had been resealed together six days later.

Then came another line.

**One object retained under implementation exception. Three returned to successor property custody.**

The object was identified only by sub-number.

08-D.

Ilyan looked back to the intake line.

The Tal die was the fourth listed object.

D.

Nobody needed to say it.

Vaun did anyway, because this one deserved precision.

"The Tal die was retained."

Serrat nodded.

"Under implementation exception."

"Where did it go?"

The answer occupied the next page.

Not far.

**Credential Integrity secure methods cabinet — demonstrator retention.**

Ilyan stared.

"It stayed here?"

The current custodian, a compact woman named Master Enna Ro, looked personally offended by the possibility.

"Not in this building. The old methods cabinet moved twice before the office relocated."

"Does it still exist?"

"The cabinet? Yes. The contents? We inventory annually."

Vaun's expression changed.

"When was the last inventory?"

"Three months ago."

"Does it list 08-D?"

Ro turned to a clerk.

The clerk left at a run.

Nobody spoke while they waited.

Ilyan became aware of Thessa's shoulder against his.

Not holding him up.

Simply there.

The clerk returned carrying a thin current register.

Ro opened it.

Her finger moved down the page.

Stopped.

Moved back up.

"No."

Vaun said, "No entry?"

"There is an entry."

Ro turned the book around.

**Legacy demonstrator 08-D — brass sponsor comparison die.**

Status:

**Removed from active demonstrator cabinet under evidence-preservation directive, 266 S.M.**

Sixteen years ago.

Two years after Silver Night.

Destination:

**Dmitrigical Tribunal sealed comparative vault.**

Authority:

**post-incident credential integrity review.**

Ilyan felt the room narrow.

Not because the line proved what he wanted.

Because it refused to.

The die had survived Silver Night.

It had remained in Credential Integrity for two years afterward.

Then the Dmitrigical Tribunal had taken it under a post-incident review.

"Personal signatory?" Vaun asked.

Ro shook her head.

"Vault transfer should have one. Not in this register."

Serrat said, "Tribunal comparative vaults use separate receiving books."

Vaun closed her eyes for one second.

"Of course they do."

It was the closest Ilyan had heard her come to swearing.

Thessa almost smiled.

Ilyan did not.

He was thinking about the dates.

The Tal die had helped test the same inherited-defect problem that CR-88 addressed.

It had been retained as a demonstrator.

It had survived the disaster.

Then, two years later, it had been removed to a Dmitrigical sealed vault under a review explicitly concerned with credential integrity after the incident.

That could mean concealment.

It could also mean investigators had found exactly the same problem they were finding now and preserved the die because it mattered.

If so, someone had investigated after Silver Night.

Someone might have known.

Someone might have written it down.

Vaun saw the direction of his thoughts.

"Don't choose yet."

Ilyan looked at her.

"I know."

"Good."

Master Ro folded her arms.

"If you're going to my old demonstrator cabinet next, don't."

Thessa raised an eyebrow.

Ro pointed at the register.

"The thing left sixteen years ago. People see an old cabinet and start believing wood remembers paperwork."

Vaun nodded.

"We go to the receiving book."

"Thank you."

Ro sounded genuinely relieved.

By midday Damaris had learned that the two bridge authorities were neither thieves nor idiots.

They were, in her opinion, something worse.

They had used two incompatible accounting conventions for emergency dredging crews and then spent four months billing one another as though the difference were evidence of bad faith.

Maeryn sat at the head of the committee table with the expression of a woman watching competent adults construct a siege engine from clerical pride.

Damaris had three ledgers open in front of her.

"They are both correct," she said.

One of the senior observers looked offended.

"They cannot both be correct."

"They can if one authority records a barge crew from departure and the other records from river-entry."

"Then the hours differ."

"Yes."

"Which means one is wrong."

Damaris looked at Maeryn.

Maeryn looked back.

Neither helped the man.

Damaris turned the first ledger around.

"This one is billing custody of the crew. This one is billing use of the channel. They are measuring different things and using the same column heading."

Silence.

Then Maeryn said, "There. Now we can stop accusing two bridge offices of stealing the same six hours and decide which convention the reconciliation order actually requires."

The senior observer stared at the ledgers as if betrayed by arithmetic.

Damaris sat back.

She enjoyed the work more than she wanted to admit.

That was dangerous in a completely different way.

Maeryn waited until the others had begun arguing about the reconciliation order, then slid a folded note toward her.

Damaris opened it beneath the table.

Rhyl's handwriting.

**Hall. Lunch. They found the die. Not lost. Bring your appetite, not your cipher.**

Damaris smiled before she could stop herself.

Maeryn noticed.

Of course she did.

"Useful?" her aunt asked quietly.

"Very."

"Professional?"

"Debatable."

Maeryn's mouth curved.

"Then you may answer it after we decide whether the eastern bridge authority is entitled to six imaginary hours of outrage."

Damaris folded the note and tucked it into her sleeve.

She did not leave early.

That mattered to her.

Not because Maeryn required obedience.

Because this was her work now too.

She could love Rhyl, help Ilyan, distrust parts of her family's political appetite and still finish the damned bridge reconciliation.

Adulthood, she was discovering, contained an unreasonable number of simultaneous truths.

Damaris kissed him before she sat down.

He caught her at the waist and kept her close for one additional heartbeat after the kiss ended.

"You smell like ink."

"You smell like wet dog."

"Cruel."

Thessa looked as though she had appointed herself guardian of whether he ate.

Not every procedural line.

The things that mattered.

Teren had been the custody liaison. Oren Savel had entered the technical request. Tavian Veyr had performed the technical work. Pel Ardis had joined two implementation sessions. The approving individual remained in a Dmitrigical authorization book they did not yet have.

It had gone to Credential Integrity bench four for inherited-defect comparison.

Veyr and Ardis had signed it out.

It had been retained as a demonstrator.

Two years after Silver Night, it had been transferred to a Dmitrigical Tribunal sealed comparative vault under a post-incident credential-integrity review.

"So somebody investigated the credential problem after Silver Night."

Vaun, at the end of the table, said, "Somebody conducted a review."

Damaris nodded.

"Right. We don't know whether it was an honest investigation, containment, cleanup, or something boring with an alarming title."

"Correct."

"Do we know the vault still exists?"

Serrat answered.

"The vault does. Whether the die remains there is the next question."

"Requested."

"Teren."

"Yes."

"You don't think I'm avoiding him?"

Damaris leaned back against Rhyl's shoulder.

"I think you want to ask your uncle whether he helped build the thing that killed your classmates. If you can acquire one more fact before doing that, acquiring the fact seems sensible."

Thessa pointed her spoon at Damaris.

"See?"

"What?"

"I told him not to have tomorrow's disaster today."

Damaris looked at Ilyan.

"That's annoyingly good."

"I know," Thessa said.

Ilyan stared at both of them.

Rhyl began laughing.

For several minutes they ate.

The investigation did not vanish.

Neither did lunch.

Damaris stole a piece of bread from Rhyl's plate. He retaliated by taking one of the roasted carrots from hers. She put her hand on his knee beneath the table and left it there. Across from them, Thessa made Ilyan finish the rest of his stew through the simple technique of continuing to talk until he ate automatically.

Vaun eventually stood.

"Savel this afternoon if personnel records confirm the address. Tribunal receiving book as soon as the preservation order clears. Nobody visits Teren alone."

Ilyan's jaw shifted.

"I wasn't planning to."

"Good."

She left.

Serrat followed.

Damaris watched them go.

"She likes you."

Ilyan looked horrified.

"Vaun?"

"Professionally."

"That is not what that sentence sounded like."

Rhyl said, "You should see your face."

"All of you are terrible."

Thessa took his remaining bread.

"And yet you keep coming to lunch."

Oren Savel lived above a bookbinder in a narrow street south of the old tribunal quarter.

He was eighty-three, broad through the shoulders despite age, and opened the door holding a glue brush.

"If this is about the roof assessment, I paid it."

Vaun showed her seal.

"It isn't."

Savel looked at the seal, then at Ilyan, then at Serrat.

His expression changed only when Serrat said, "Emergency Substitution Review."

Savel put the brush down.

"Come in."

The apartment smelled of paste, paper and onions.

A stack of repaired children's books occupied one chair. Savel moved them to the floor and apologized to nobody.

Vaun did not begin with Silver Night.

She showed him the certified roster extract.

"Do you remember entering this request?"

Savel read it.

His eyes lingered on Tavian Veyr's name.

"Yes."

Ilyan felt every muscle in his back tighten.

Vaun asked, "Why?"

"Because Ardis wanted a technical answer and the Secretariat wanted it yesterday."

"What answer?"

"Whether a substituted witness credential could inherit a verifiable defect without the parent authorization object being physically present."

"Who told you to request CR-88?"

Savel frowned.

"Veyr did."

That was not what Ilyan had expected.

Savel continued.

"Ardis brought the procedural problem. I sent it to Credential Integrity. Veyr said the old Venn-Arven counterfeit case had already demonstrated the relevant defect behavior. So I entered the archive request."

Vaun's voice remained even.

"Who brought Ardis the procedural problem?"

Savel looked at the ceiling.

Not evasion.

Memory.

"The Secretariat."

"A person."

"I don't remember."

"Would you have known then?"

"Yes."

"Was it Teren Valeric?"

Savel looked at Ilyan before answering.

That glance hurt more than the answer.

"No. Teren was custody. He complained about everyone leaving working packets open on his tables."

Ilyan nearly laughed.

It was exactly the sort of thing Teren would complain about.

Vaun asked, "Did he participate in the technical or procedural discussion?"

"Not that I remember. He unlocked the compartment, checked seals, logged people in and told Ardis to stop putting tea near restricted paper."

Serrat's mouth twitched.

"Did he know why the work mattered?"

Savel shook his head slowly.

"He knew it was emergency witness procedure. We all did. Whether he knew where it would be used? Ask him."

Ilyan said, "Where was it going to be used?"

Savel looked at him.

"I didn't know."

"But you knew it was urgent."

"Everything from Contingency was urgent. That's why they called it Contingency instead of Eventually."

Vaun allowed the old man half a smile.

Then she asked, "Did Silver Night change your understanding of the work?"

Savel's smile disappeared.

"Yes."

The room became very quiet.

"How?"

He looked down at his hands.

There was glue along one thumb.

"Three weeks after the funerals, Ardis asked me for the request copy. He wanted to know exactly when the technical work had started."

"Why?"

"He said the substituted credentials used in the west-tower review had behaved too well."

Ilyan heard his own heartbeat.

Vaun did not move.

"Those words?"

"Close enough that I'll swear to them."

"What did you do?"

"Gave him the copy. He compared dates. Then he told me to preserve my desk notes."

"Did you?"

Savel looked toward the back room.

"For fourteen years."

"And then?"

"Ardis died. The Joint Office was already gone. Nobody had asked for the notes in years. My wife wanted the cupboard."

Ilyan's stomach dropped.

Savel raised a hand.

"I didn't burn them."

Nobody breathed.

"I gave them to Ardis's daughter."

Vaun said, "Why?"

"Because they were mostly his questions, and because by then I had learned that throwing away paper was the fastest way to make someone important suddenly want it."

Thessa, standing near the window, whispered, "I like him."

Savel heard her.

"You shouldn't. I was unbearable at forty."

"Most people are."

"Fair."

Vaun asked for the daughter's name.

Savel gave it.

**Lena Ardis.**

A school archivist in the northern ward.

Then he added one more thing.

"If you're looking for the authorization book, don't start with the vault."

Vaun's eyes sharpened.

"Why?"

"Contingency kept duplicate countersignature slips with the chair's monthly expense packet. Nobody trusted anybody after the Delphimi reforms, so every secret procedure eventually generated an invoice."

Damaris would have loved him.

Ilyan almost said it aloud.

Instead he asked, "Where do the expense packets go?"

Savel shrugged.

"Back then? River Ledger audit archive after seven years."

Vaun looked at Ilyan.

Ilyan looked toward the door as though Damaris might materialize through it by professional instinct.

She did not.

But for the first time all day, he smiled.

"I know someone."

Thessa groaned.

"Don't say it like that. She'll charge you."

"She'll charge the Realm."

"Worse."

Savel picked up his glue brush again.

"If you find the name, don't assume the person understood what came after."

Ilyan's smile faded.

"Why not?"

Savel looked at him for a long moment.

"Because I entered the request."

There it was.

Not absolution.

Not confession.

A warning from an old man who had spent decades living with his own line in the ledger.

Ilyan nodded.

"I won't."

He meant it.

Outside, Bloomtide sunlight had reached the street again. The wet stones shone between the shadows of the buildings.

Vaun was already dictating three requests to Serrat: preservation contact with Lena Ardis, the Dmitrigical comparative-vault receiving book, and a River Ledger search for Contingency Secretariat monthly expense packets from the month before Silver Night.

Three doors.

None of them answers yet.

Ilyan stood on the step while Thessa waited beside him.

Teren's name remained in the chain.

So did his father's crime.

So did his uncle's terrible language about necessity.

Better evidence had not made any of that disappear.

It had merely made the shape of responsibility more difficult to lie about.

Thessa nudged him with her shoulder.

"Food tonight?"

"Again?"

"It happens daily."

"I've heard rumors."

"Lyrion found a place near the canal. Mireth and Caelen are coming. Damaris says she might escape the bridge people. Rhyl says he'll extract her if necessary."

"Professionally?"

"Furiously."

Ilyan laughed.

It surprised him.

Then they went to find the others.

## The Notes He Left Her

Lena Ardis worked in a school archive that smelled of chalk, old wool and rain-damp children.

Ilyan had expected something grander.

Not because school archivists required marble, but because Pel Ardis had been dead for fourteen years and had somehow grown larger in Ilyan's mind during the walk north. Dead people did that when they left behind unanswered questions. They became corridors instead of people.

The northern ward school was resolutely uninterested in cooperating with that transformation.

A boy of perhaps nine ran past them carrying a wooden model of the moon with one half painted silver and the other half still wet. Two girls were arguing beside a rack of cloaks about whose turn it was to feed something called Mistress Pebble. Somewhere beyond the archive door, a teacher was attempting to persuade twenty children that multiplication was not a personal insult.

Judge Vaun stopped beneath a painted sign that read ARCHIVE — STAFF ONLY.

Thessa looked at Ilyan.

"Better than Restricted Testimony."

"The lettering?"

"The threats."

Vaun knocked.

A woman called, "If you're bringing another box of winter compositions, put them outside."

Vaun opened the door.

Lena Ardis was kneeling on the floor between two low shelves with a bundle of tied exercise books in her arms. She looked to be in her middle fifties, dark-haired except for a broad silver streak above her left temple, with ink on three fingers and spectacles pushed up into her hair.

She looked at Vaun's judicial collar.

Then Serrat's black one.

Then Ilyan.

The exercise books went very still in her arms.

"This isn't about the children."

"No," Vaun said.

Lena stood.

"My father?"

Nobody had said his name.

Vaun closed the door behind them.

"Oren Savel told us he gave you some of Examiner Ardis's old papers."

Lena's face changed in a way Ilyan recognized immediately.

Not surprise.

Calculation.

"He gave me a box."

"Do you still have it?"

"Some of it."

Vaun waited.

Lena set the exercise books on a desk.

"You're going to need a better question."

Thessa glanced at Ilyan.

He almost smiled.

Vaun did not.

"Did the box contain Savel's desk notes concerning Emergency Substitution Review?"

"Yes."

"Do you still have those?"

"Yes."

"Have you altered, recopied or separated them?"

"Separated, yes. Altered, no."

"Why separated?"

"Because my father was a procedural examiner for forty-one years and believed throwing away paper was a character defect. The box contained tax notices, two recipes, a list of boots he meant to have repaired, notes from three unrelated hearings, letters from my mother and enough obsolete forms to build a small house. I sorted it."

Serrat said, "By what method?"

Lena looked at him over the top of her spectacles.

"Archivally."

Thessa coughed into her hand.

Ilyan looked at the floor.

Vaun's mouth moved by perhaps half a degree.

"Can you show us the substitution-review material?"

Lena did not answer immediately.

Instead she crossed the room to a narrow cabinet and unlocked it with a key from a chain beneath her collar.

Inside were six document boxes.

She touched the third.

Then stopped.

"Before I give you anything, I want to know whether my father is being accused of something."

There it was.

Not obstruction.

A daughter.

Vaun answered her as one.

"No accusation has been made against him. We have evidence that he participated in a technical review before Silver Night and later became concerned about how substituted credentials performed during a west-tower review. We are trying to establish what he learned and what he did with it."

Lena's fingers remained on the box.

"Silver Night."

"Yes."

Her eyes moved to Ilyan.

He had become accustomed to that moment. Recognition did not always arrive at his face. Sometimes it arrived at his surname before anyone spoke it.

"Valeric?"

"Ilyan Valeric."

She studied him.

"My father disliked your uncle."

Ilyan's stomach tightened.

"Teren?"

"He disliked his filing habits."

Thessa made a small sound that might have been a laugh dying of exhaustion.

Lena continued, "He liked him personally. Said he was one of the few custody men who would tell an examiner to get his tea off restricted paper and mean it."

That matched Savel almost word for word.

Ilyan hated the relief that moved through him.

He let it exist anyway.

"Did he ever say Teren knew what the work was for?"

Vaun looked at him.

Not a rebuke.

A reminder.

Lena shook her head.

"Not to me."

Ilyan stopped there.

She lifted the box.

"There's a reading table in the next room. No ink. Pencil only. Gloves if Serrat insists, though my father would rise from his grave to complain about people losing their grip on paper."

"Clean hands are sufficient," Serrat said.

"He would have liked you."

"Unlikely."

"Then he definitely would have liked you."

---

The papers were not a revelation.

That was the first important thing about them.

They were work.

Pel Ardis had written in a compact slant, heavily abbreviated and apparently convinced that vowels were a luxury supplied to other departments. Savel's hand was broader and more legible. Several pages were nothing more than dates, room numbers, request codes and complaints about missing attachments.

Ilyan felt his shoulders loosen as the first half hour passed.

Not because the papers were harmless.

Because they were ordinary enough to be believable.

Nobody had written *we are preparing to falsify credentials for a massacre* in a margin.

Ardis had written things like:

**Need defect continuity when parent object absent. Veyr says CR-88 proves secondary carry possible. Ask whether lawful reproduction can be distinguished from counterfeit reproduction after sealing.**

And:

**Contingency wants field-verifiable result, no destructive opening. Problem: examiner at receiving end cannot know production chain. Only object.**

And, underlined twice:

**If validation tests only the inherited flaw, then a copied flaw becomes validation.**

Ilyan read that sentence three times.

Thessa leaned over his shoulder without touching the page.

"That's the problem."

"Yes."

Serrat was reading a different sheet.

Vaun stood beside him.

Lena sat at the far end of the table with her hands wrapped around a cup of tea she had forgotten to drink.

Ilyan turned the page.

A date.

Nine days before Revision Seventeen was filed.

Ardis had written:

**Pushed back on broad language. Emergency substitution should require secondary witness check where parent authorization absent. Secretariat says delay defeats emergency purpose. Asked Veyr whether physical defect inheritance alone sufficient. He said technically sufficient to establish method consistency, not provenance. Important distinction.**

Ilyan looked across the table.

"Serrat."

The examiner came over.

Ilyan pointed.

Serrat read it once.

Then again.

"Veyr warned them."

Vaun said, "Ardis recorded that Veyr warned them."

"Right."

Ilyan rubbed his thumb against the side of his forefinger.

Method consistency was not provenance.

The same distinction Jalen had made when examining Eren Vale's credential.

Not because Jalen knew this note existed.

Because competent people looking at the same technical problem could reach the same limit.

Vaun asked Lena, "Did your father ever discuss this language with you?"

"Not before Silver Night."

"After?"

Lena looked at the page.

"He said he'd helped build a door that could not tell who walked through it."

Nobody spoke.

She frowned.

"Those were his words. I was young enough to think he meant an actual door."

Ilyan thought of the west service gate.

For one irrational second he wanted the metaphor to mean that gate too.

It did not.

A credential system was a door in the sense Ardis meant. Nothing more was earned.

"When did he say that?" Vaun asked.

"After the funerals. Maybe a month. He was sleeping badly."

"Did he name Silver Night?"

"Yes."

"Did he say his work caused it?"

"No."

Lena's voice sharpened.

"He said he didn't know. That was what frightened him."

Ilyan understood that better than he wanted to.

He turned another page.

Savel's desk notes began where Ardis's became less orderly.

There were copies of request times. A list of people present at two sessions. Tavian Veyr. Pel Ardis. Teren Valeric. Oren Savel.

Teren's name had no annotation beside it except **custody / keys**.

On the next sheet, Ardis had drawn a line between three dates.

Technical request.

Revision filing.

West-tower credential issuance.

The last date sat four days before Silver Night.

Beside it:

**Need actual issuance register. Were these ours?**

Ilyan's pulse climbed.

"What west-tower credential issuance?"

Serrat was already reading.

"We don't know."

Vaun leaned closer.

The next note answered only part of it.

**Post-event: substituted credentials recovered from west-tower access review show defect consistency beyond expected variation. Need originals compared to bench examples. Asked Contingency for issuance register. No response.**

Then, two lines lower:

**Savel says expense duplicates may preserve chair authorization. River audit after retention period.**

Damaris.

Again.

Ilyan looked toward the window as if professional instinct might finally summon her.

This time there was a knock at the archive door.

Lena rose.

A school clerk entered carrying a folded judicial message.

"For Judge Vaun. River Ledger runner."

Thessa looked at Ilyan.

"Don't."

"I didn't say anything."

"Your face did."

Vaun opened the message.

Read it.

Then handed it to Ilyan.

Damaris's handwriting filled the lower half beneath an archivist's formal notation.

**Found the monthly packet. Contingency Secretariat, Bloomtide 264. Duplicate countersignature survives. Personal hand, not office stamp. Jalen is comparing against known administrative specimens. Do not come running; the archive closes nothing merely because you are impatient. — D.**

Below that, squeezed into the margin:

**Rhyl says this is me being kind. I dispute the characterization.**

Ilyan laughed once.

It escaped him before he could stop it.

Lena looked offended on behalf of the dead.

"Sorry."

"Good news?"

"Potentially useful bureaucracy."

She considered that.

"My father would have approved."

---

Damaris had found the packet because Maeryn refused to let her search for it.

"You are an observer," Maeryn said, without looking up from the bridge reconciliation draft. "Not an archive warrant with hair."

Damaris stood beside her aunt's desk holding Vaun's preservation request.

"I have authorization."

"The archive has authorization. You have an interest. Those are different things."

"You enjoy this."

"Immensely."

Damaris sat.

The River Ledger audit archive occupied two underground levels beneath the eastern counting hall, where temperature and humidity were kept stable enough that the clerks complained more reliably than the paper. Vaun's request had gone to the proper custodian. Damaris had been permitted to identify the likely accounting series because she knew what Savel had described and because her current committee work gave her legitimate familiarity with the archive's transport-expense classifications.

Then she had been told to wait.

So she waited.

Badly.

Rhyl arrived twenty minutes later carrying two cups of spiced milk and a paper packet of seed cakes.

Maeryn looked at him.

"You aren't an archive warrant either."

"I brought food."

"That improves your standing considerably."

He handed Damaris a cup.

She took it and caught his wrist before he could pull away.

"You're late."

"You didn't invite me."

"A technicality."

He bent and kissed her forehead.

Maeryn continued reading as though young adults had not been kissing in offices since the invention of offices.

Damaris tugged Rhyl down into the chair beside hers.

"They found Ardis's daughter."

"Alive?"

"The daughter, yes. Ardis remains inconveniently dead."

"Rude of him."

"Extremely."

Rhyl opened the seed cakes.

Damaris took one.

"Ilyan is going to climb through the archive wall if this takes another hour."

"No, he won't."

"Metaphorically."

"Thessa will sit on him."

Damaris pictured it.

"Probably."

Maeryn turned a page.

"If you two intend to become unbearable, do it quietly."

Damaris smiled into her cup.

A clerk arrived with a grey packet sealed in fresh preservation tape.

The smile vanished.

The packet contained routine misery: catering receipts, carriage reimbursements, copying fees, emergency room rentals, messenger charges, seal wax, two disputed lodging claims and an expense for six replacement lockplates that nobody had bothered to explain.

Then the countersignature slip.

The approving office was the Dmitrigical Tribunal Contingency Secretariat.

The procedure code matched Emergency Substitution Review.

The amount was small enough to be insulting.

Three technical sessions. Two archive transfers. One credential bench fee.

At the bottom, beside the authorization line, sat a personal countersignature.

Not a full signature.

A compact administrative mark used for recurring approvals.

Damaris did not know it.

Maeryn did.

Her aunt's face did not change much.

That was how Damaris knew it mattered.

"Who?"

Maeryn looked at her.

"I am not answering from memory."

Damaris felt irritation flare.

Then she recognized the discipline underneath it.

"You recognize it."

"I think I recognize it."

"From where?"

"Old grain-contingency releases. Possibly."

"Valeric?"

Maeryn's gaze sharpened.

"Do not fish with surnames."

Rhyl, beside Damaris, said nothing.

His knee remained against hers.

Maeryn called for Jalen.

The forensic accountant arrived with his coat half buttoned and a plum in one hand.

"If this is another bridge-hour problem, I resign from the family."

"Compare this mark," Maeryn said.

He stopped chewing.

Damaris watched him work.

Not dramatically.

He requested three old administrative specimen books. Then two more. He compared stroke order, pressure, abbreviation habit and the tiny backward hook beneath the terminal line. He rejected the first apparent match. He rejected the second.

After forty minutes he placed two sheets side by side.

"Same hand," he said.

Damaris looked down.

The known specimen came from a grain-contingency release nineteen years earlier.

The name printed beneath it was not Teren Valeric.

It was **Corren Valeric**.

Ilyan's father.

For a moment Damaris heard nothing except the archive ventilation moving air through the room.

Rhyl's hand closed around hers under the table.

Maeryn did not touch her.

She did something more useful.

"Confidence?"

Jalen looked again.

"High. Not absolute. I want the original specimen book and a second examiner before anyone says the word proven."

Damaris swallowed.

"Send that. Exactly that."

She wrote the message to Vaun.

Then stopped before the final line.

Ilyan would read his father's name in a room full of other people if she sent it now.

He deserved the fact.

He also deserved not to receive it as a clever note beneath a bureaucratic joke.

Damaris tore the sheet in half.

Rhyl watched her.

"What?"

"The packet survives. The mark survives. Jalen is comparing it. That's enough for the runner."

Maeryn looked at her for a long moment.

Then nodded once.

Damaris sent the shorter message.

The name could wait until Vaun had the second comparison.

Not because she intended to protect Ilyan from truth.

Because truth did not become more truthful when delivered carelessly.

---

The second comparison arrived near dusk.

By then the Seven had begun gathering at the canal eating house Lyrion had found, though nobody had yet managed to call the evening ordinary.

Mireth and Caelen occupied one end of the table and were pretending not to be touching beneath it. They were failing so thoroughly that the pretense had become ceremonial. Lyrion had acquired a bottle of pale summer wine. Thessa sat beside Ilyan. Rhyl and Damaris arrived last.

Damaris took off her cloak.

Ilyan saw her face.

The room changed.

Not for everyone.

For him.

He stood.

"What happened?"

Vaun entered behind them.

That answered enough.

The judge carried a sealed River Ledger copy sleeve.

Nobody reached for the wine.

Vaun sat.

"The Contingency expense packet survived."

Ilyan remained standing.

"The authorization?"

"A duplicate personal countersignature survived with it. River Ledger performed a handwriting comparison against known administrative specimens. A second examiner repeated it."

He knew before she said the name.

Damaris could see that.

Maybe because his father had already occupied too much of the shape around the evidence. Maybe because hope was not the same thing as expectation.

Vaun said, "Both examiners assess the mark as highly consistent with Corren Valeric's administrative countersignature."

Ilyan sat down.

Nobody spoke.

Thessa's hand moved across the table.

Stopped before touching him.

Ilyan looked at it.

Then put his hand over hers.

Damaris watched his face.

No collapse.

No revelation of a father newly transformed into a monster.

Ilyan already knew his father had helped engineer Silver Night.

This was different.

Smaller.

Worse in its own way.

A line connecting a known crime to a particular procedural choice before the crime occurred.

"What exactly did he authorize?" Ilyan asked.

Vaun slid the certified copy toward him.

"The expense packet covers the Emergency Substitution Review sessions, archive transfers and credential bench work. The countersignature authorizes expenditure and procedure under Contingency authority. It does not tell us whether Corren originated the request, dictated Revision Seventeen, selected the west-tower credentials, or knew how the resulting method would be used."

Ilyan read the page.

"But he signed for the work."

"Yes."

His thumb rested beside the copied mark.

Damaris sat opposite him.

"I almost sent you the name by runner."

He looked up.

"Why didn't you?"

"Because Jalen had one comparison and wanted another. And because I didn't want you learning this from a joke in my handwriting."

For several seconds he said nothing.

Then: "Thank you."

Rhyl's shoulder touched hers.

Lyrion poured wine.

Not as celebration.

Because there were seven cups and something had to happen next.

Mireth asked, "What did Ardis leave?"

Ilyan told them.

The notes. Veyr's warning that method consistency was not provenance. Ardis pushing for a secondary witness check. The Secretariat rejecting delay. The post-Silver-Night concern that west-tower substituted credentials had behaved too well. The request for an issuance register that had apparently gone unanswered.

Caelen's expression hardened at that.

"West tower."

"Yes."

"Our west tower?"

"We don't know yet."

Caelen nodded.

He did not make the next leap for him.

Thessa said, "Ardis wrote 'west-tower access review.' We need the issuance register before we decide which west tower or which access event."

"Yes," Ilyan said.

Damaris looked at Vaun.

"And the die?"

"Tribunal receiving book tomorrow morning."

"Teren?"

Ilyan looked down at his father's countersignature.

"After the receiving book."

Nobody challenged him.

He was no longer delaying because he feared the conversation.

He was sequencing evidence.

That mattered.

Outside, canal lamps came on one by one in the Bloomtide dusk. Their reflections stretched in the black water and broke whenever a boat passed.

The food arrived.

For a while nobody touched it.

Then Thessa nudged Ilyan's plate toward him.

"Eat."

He looked at her.

"You are becoming repetitive."

"You are becoming reliably feedable."

A laugh moved around the table, small and tired.

Ilyan picked up his fork.

His father's mark remained beneath Vaun's seal.

Pel Ardis's notes remained in a school archive among children's compositions and obsolete forms.

Tal's die remained, perhaps, behind a tribunal vault door.

And somewhere in an issuance register that nobody at the table had yet seen was the answer to a narrower question than the one they had begun with:

Who had been given substituted credentials before Silver Night, and where had those credentials opened a door?

## The Door That Opened

The issuance register had survived because nobody had thought it important enough to destroy.

That was Examiner Sael's opinion, delivered while Ilyan stood beside him in a basement room under the old Credential Integrity offices and tried not to resent the existence of dust.

"You don't know that," Ilyan said.

"Correct."

"Then why say it?"

Sael looked at the shelf label again. "Because you were looking at the room as though survival were evidence of innocence."

Ilyan opened his mouth.

Closed it.

Thessa, behind him, made a thoughtful noise.

"I hate when he does that."

"You met him yesterday."

"I learn quickly."

Sael ignored both of them.

The room had once been a working archive and had since been demoted to the place where working archives sent things they were not yet permitted to burn. Narrow shelves ran from stone floor to a ceiling low enough that Thessa had already struck her knuckles against a hanging lamp while removing her coat. The air smelled of paste, old leather and the faint mineral damp of a building that had spent two centuries pretending groundwater was someone else's administrative problem.

Judge Vaun stood at the central table with the transfer authorization open before her. Serrat had remained at the Hall of Veils to supervise the restricted-copy chain. Caelen was on Royal Martial duty. Mireth had Green Covenant examinations until midday. Lyrion had been required to attend what he described in his note as a diplomatic breakfast conducted by people who considered toast a negotiating position. Damaris was at the River Ledger.

The Seven had not become an investigative unit merely because they were friends.

They still had jobs.

Ilyan found that reassuring.

Most days.

The archive clerk arrived carrying a grey ledger against her chest.

"Emergency credentials," she said. "Years two-sixty through two-sixty-six."

Vaun checked the seal before touching it.

"Original?"

"Original issuance book."

"Any known removals?"

"One page copied under tribunal order in two-sixty-six. No leaves removed according to the binding inspection."

"Who ordered the copy?"

The clerk glanced at the transfer sheet.

"Dmitrigical post-incident review."

Thessa looked at Ilyan.

He looked at the ledger.

Neither said anything.

Vaun signed the receipt.

The clerk laid the book down.

It was ugly.

Ilyan had expected that too by now.

Important records were almost never beautiful. They were too busy being used.

The leather corners had been repaired twice. The spine bore three different numbering systems. Somebody had spilled wax across the lower edge years ago and scraped most of it away with a knife.

Sael opened to Bloomtide, 264 S.M.

The entries were arranged by issuance batch rather than recipient.

Ilyan read slowly.

## What Teren Wrote

The order cleared at eleven minutes past noon.

Ilyan knew because he had watched the clock for the previous forty-three minutes and had begun to dislike it personally.

Examiner Sael noticed.

He said nothing.

That was worse.

Judge Vaun came out of Serrat's office carrying two sheets folded beneath her hand. Serrat followed her as far as the outer desk, where he stopped and looked at Ilyan with the expression of a man who had already decided which arguments he would not entertain.

"The tribunal presiding examiner signed," Vaun said. "The Hall restriction is satisfied for inspection under continuous custody. Nothing leaves SV-12. No rubbing, no impression work, no reagent testing, no destructive sampling."

Ilyan stood.

"And the designator key?"

"Included."

"Teren's disposition?"

"Included."

Serrat added, "If the compartment contains material outside the order, you stop."

"I know."

"Good."

Ilyan took his coat from the chair.

Thessa, who had been sitting on the window ledge with one boot braced against the wall, dropped lightly to the floor.

"That was almost affectionate."

"No."

"I stand corrected."

They left before she could improve the exchange.

Bloomtide had spent the morning undecided between rain and sunlight and had settled, by noon, on doing both. The paving stones outside the Hall shone black beneath a pale sky. Water dripped from the carved faces over the archive doors. Market awnings along the next street sagged under the last shower while vendors reopened trays of sugared nuts, ribbons, cheap devotional charms and early flowers.

Ilyan noticed all of it because his mind had become very good at noticing irrelevant things when the relevant thing was unbearable.

Vaun walked ahead.

Sael remained at the Hall to preserve the restricted-copy chain.

"You don't have to come," Ilyan said.

Thessa looked at him.

"I know."

That ended the matter.

Master Enna Ro had prepared the vault room before they arrived.

Prepared, in this case, meant she had placed three clean cloths on a table, brought in a second lamp, and stationed two tribunal witnesses by the door. She had also arranged six sharpened pencils in a line so straight that Thessa stared at them for several seconds and then deliberately did not touch any.

Ro approved of this restraint by not acknowledging it.

The compartment-opening order passed from Vaun to Ro, from Ro to the senior witness, and back again. Seals were checked against the register. The old compartment key came from one cabinet; the second came from another. Ro used neither until both witnesses had signed the opening line.

Ilyan had spent enough of the last week learning how records survived to understand that this was not ceremony for ceremony's sake.

People forgot.

People died.

People lied.

Paper did all three less often when several irritated clerks had signed it.

SV-12 occupied the lower half of a steel-fronted cabinet set into the stone wall. Its wax seal had darkened almost brown with age. The cord beneath it was dusty but intact.

Ro showed the seal to Vaun.

"No visible breach."

Vaun examined it.

"Agreed."

The witnesses agreed separately.

Only then did Ro cut the cord.

The sound was tiny.

Ilyan hated it anyway.

The lock resisted the first key. Ro frowned, withdrew it, turned it over, and tried again.

Thessa glanced at Ilyan.

He nearly laughed.

It came out as air through his nose.

The second lock opened cleanly.

Ro pulled the compartment door outward.

Inside were two document boxes, a narrow fitted case, and a flat packet wrapped in grey archive cloth.

Nothing glowed.

Nothing whispered.

No dead conspirator had possessed the courtesy to leave a confession on top.

Ro checked the receiving list before removing anything.

"Box one. ES-17-W return set."

She placed it on the first cloth.

"Box two. W-5 file and designator key."

Second cloth.

The fitted case was Tal's die.

Ro did not open it yet.

The grey packet carried Teren's name.

Ilyan looked away from it.

"Credentials first," Vaun said.

He was grateful to her for making the choice unnecessary.

Box one contained five narrow credential folders marked W-1, W-2, W-3, W-4 and W-6. Each held the returned paper credential between protective sheets, along with the post-incident physical examination slip.

They looked ordinary.

That was becoming the most frightening property an object could possess.

Veyr's technical work had done exactly what the emergency review had asked it to do. The sponsor lineage marks were clean. The inherited defect appeared where it should. The five documents differed in handwriting, bearer description and destination authority, but the sponsor impressions shared a consistency that had bothered Pel Ardis only after the fact.

Vaun read the variation report while Ro opened box two.

"Here."

The designator key was a single folded sheet sealed inside a smaller envelope.

Ro checked the seal number.

Then she opened it.

Ilyan stood very still.

W-1 belonged to a tribunal records porter.

W-2 to a contracted locksmith.

W-3 to an Academy receiving clerk temporarily transferred from the south stores.

W-4 to a ward inspector.

W-6 to a Contingency Secretariat witness examiner.

W-5 belonged to **Mara Venn**.

Thessa's breath caught.

Ilyan read the line again.

Mara Venn.

West tower third-floor restricted receiving.

WT-3.

The credential had been issued to her four days before Silver Night and had never been returned.

The notation in the issuance book had presumed it lost with receiving staff because Mara Venn had been listed among the dead.

Except Mara Venn had not died on Silver Night.

They knew that now.

They had spoken to people who remembered her afterward.

Afraid later.

Angry earlier.

Following returned lots that came up light.

Ilyan put a hand flat on the table.

"She survived."

Vaun did not tell him to slow down.

She was already reading the W-5 file.

"Yes."

"Then the credential wasn't necessarily lost in the tower."

"No."

"She could have kept it."

"Could have."

"Or somebody could have taken it from her."

"Could have."

Thessa leaned over the table without touching the file.

"Does the file say why she needed WT-3?"

Ro turned the next leaf.

The request purpose was painfully dull.

**Receiving continuity support during restricted witness intake.**

The attached duty assignment put Mara on temporary third-floor receiving because two regular clerks had been reassigned to graduation security logistics.

Not a secret mission.

Not a conspirator's privilege.

A staffing problem.

"So she was supposed to be there," Thessa said.

"For two shifts," Vaun said.

"Including Silver Night?"

Vaun checked the dates.

"Including Silver Night."

Ilyan thought of Mara's later questions about comparison releases.

A receipt proved who had permission to take something. It did not prove where it went afterward.

He looked at the missing credential's empty return field.

"Teren knew W-5 was hers."

Vaun looked toward the grey packet.

"We are about to find out what Teren wrote."

The report was twenty-seven pages long.

Teren's handwriting occupied only the margins and signature lines. The body had been dictated to a tribunal clerk in the dry formal prose of an examiner who expected strangers to argue with every sentence.

Ilyan recognized his uncle anyway.

Not in the phrasing.

In the order.

Teren had always sorted a problem by removing the things that could be answered immediately. When Ilyan was twelve and had broken a practice ward in the west garden, Teren had made him reconstruct the afternoon from the moment he woke until the ward failed. Breakfast first. Then weather. Then who had entered the garden. Then which chalk Ilyan had used.

At twelve, Ilyan had considered this maddening.

At twenty-three, he read the same habit applied to six emergency credentials and wanted to be sick.

The report began with the five returned documents.

All five were physically valid under the rules then in force.

All five were issued through an authorized emergency procedure.

All five had passed sponsor-lineage inspection.

Then Teren reached the variation problem.

The inherited sponsor defect was not merely consistent across the five credentials. Its secondary characteristics were too consistent for five independently prepared emergency documents.

Teren cited Veyr's method note.

He cited CR-88.

He cited Ardis's post-incident concern.

Then he wrote the sentence Ilyan had been waiting eighteen years to read without knowing it existed.

**The documents are individually valid by enacted procedure and collectively inconsistent with independent preparation.**

Thessa whispered, "Fuck."

Ro did not look up.

Vaun continued reading.

Teren had ordered the designator key opened.

He had identified Mara as W-5.

He had checked the Academy casualty roll.

Then he had found the contradiction.

Mara Venn was recorded dead in the first west-tower casualty compilation.

Three days later, a corrected receiving-office payroll return showed her alive and absent on medical leave.

Teren attached both records.

Ilyan's fingers went cold.

"He knew she survived."

"Yes," Vaun said.

The report continued.

Teren requested Mara for interview.

The request was denied.

Not because she was dead.

Because she had been transferred into **protected witness reassignment under Contingency authority**.

The destination was withheld from him.

Teren objected.

He objected twice.

The second objection was sharper than the first.

**A review of an outstanding credential cannot be completed while the assigned bearer is unavailable by order of the same authority whose procedure is under review.**

Ilyan closed his eyes.

His uncle had seen it.

Not everything.

Enough.

"Keep reading," Vaun said.

He opened them.

Teren had then requested the original personal countersignature for the Emergency Substitution Review.

Denied: outside review scope.

He requested the request-entry clerk's desk notes.

Unavailable.

He requested the west-tower access ledger for the night of Silver Night.

Partially destroyed in the fire.

He requested the surviving upper-corridor guard sheet.

That request was granted.

The guard sheet recorded five ES-17-W designators entering the west tower that evening.

W-1.

W-2.

W-3.

W-5.

W-6.

No W-4.

W-5 entered at the third-floor receiving checkpoint forty-one minutes before the first alarm.

There was no recorded exit.

Ilyan stared at the line until the ink blurred.

Mara had been there.

Or her credential had.

He corrected himself before Vaun had to.

"W-5 was used to enter."

Vaun glanced at him.

"Yes."

"We don't know by whom."

"Correct."

Thessa's hand found his wrist under the edge of the table.

Her fingers were warm.

He did not move away.

Teren's next section concerned Tal's die.

The die had been introduced into the post-incident review because its defect geometry matched the class of sponsor-lineage irregularity Teren was examining. Teren found no evidence that the die itself had been used to manufacture the ES-17-W credentials. Instead, he treated it as a comparison object for determining whether the repeated defect could be produced without the parent authorization die.

His conclusion was careful.

**It can.**

Then, beneath it:

**Therefore possession of the parent die cannot be inferred from a credential bearing its inherited defect.**

That was CR-88 stripped of every polite administrative layer.

A lineage check could be made to recognize ancestry that had never physically touched the document.

Teren had understood.

The final section was titled **Disposition and Unresolved Matters**.

Ilyan read it once.

Then again.

Teren recommended immediate suspension of Revision Seventeen pending independent review.

He recommended locating Mara Venn and interviewing her outside Contingency supervision.

He recommended identifying the person who used W-5 at the west-tower checkpoint through surviving witness testimony.

He recommended preserving Tal's die, the returned credentials, W-5's file, CR-88 and all related request records together.

And he recommended referral to Truth Beyond the Veil because the same administrative authority appeared in both the authorization chain and the subsequent restriction of witnesses and records.

None of those recommendations had happened.

Instead, attached behind the report was a one-page disposition order.

**Review held incomplete. No adverse finding entered. Materials sealed pending security necessity determination.**

Signed by the Contingency Secretariat.

Personal countersignature redacted on the vault copy.

Below that was Teren's handwritten objection.

Only four words.

**I do not concur.**

Ilyan sat down.

He had not meant to.

The chair simply happened beneath him.

For several seconds nobody spoke.

The report did not absolve Teren.

It did something more inconvenient.

It proved that eighteen years ago Teren had identified the central procedural failure, demanded the witness, challenged the authority restricting her, recommended suspension, and objected when the review was buried.

And then, years later, he had renewed the restriction.

Both things were true.

Ilyan pressed his thumb into his palm.

"Why renew it?"

Vaun closed the report.

"Ask him."

"He told me he renewed the restriction. He didn't tell me he'd written this."

"Ask him that too."

Thessa still held his wrist.

"After we finish."

Ilyan looked at her.

She nodded toward the fitted case.

Tal's die.

He almost laughed again.

"Right."

The world remained offensively committed to having more than one problem at a time.

Not happy.

Relieved.

Small.

Real.

Ilyan stepped closer.

"Yes."

The answer landed badly because Ilyan had wanted something easier to hate.

There it was.

"That I had misunderstood what the emergency credentials were protecting. That the west tower had contained material and witnesses whose exposure would have broken the Concord after Silver Night."

"Did you believe him?"

"No."

"Then what?"

Teren looked directly at him.

"Then he told me Mara was still alive and that if I kept forcing the review into open jurisdiction, people would find her."

Rain ticked against the sill.

Ilyan's mouth had gone dry.

"Did he prove it?"

"He gave me a phrase."

"What phrase?"

Teren hesitated.

"She had used it during my first attempted interview request. A private authentication line. It wasn't in the file I had."

Vaun spoke for the first time.

"What line?"

Teren looked at her.

"Ask for the blue pears before frost."

Thessa frowned.

Ilyan did not know it.

Vaun wrote it down.

"And after that?"

"I stopped trying to force Mara's location into the ordinary record. I kept objecting to closure. I kept the compartment sealed. When the successor restriction came up, I renewed it."

Ilyan's voice was quieter now.

"And you never told me any of this."

"You were five."

"I'm not five now."

"No."

Teren looked older than he had in the service-chest room.

"Now you are the one person I was most afraid would go looking for her if I told you she might still be alive."

"You don't get to decide that for me."

"No."

The immediate agreement took some of the force out of Ilyan's anger and left nowhere comfortable for it to go.

Teren continued.

"I made a choice. I thought preserving the restriction protected a witness. I also knew it protected your father from questions he deserved to answer. I told myself the first fact outweighed the second. Some years I believed that more easily than others."

Ilyan stared at him.

There was the thing he had wanted.

Not innocence.

Not confession.

A choice.

A compromised, frightened, consequential choice made by someone who had understood enough to know it was compromised.

"Did you ever speak to Mara?"

"No."

"Did you ever learn where she went?"

"No."

"Did my father?"

Teren's eyes dropped.

"I don't know."

Ilyan believed that he did not know.

He did not yet know whether he believed anything else.

Vaun closed her notebook.

"I will need a formal statement."

"I assumed so."

"Today."

Teren nodded.

Then he looked at Ilyan.

"You should be angry."

Ilyan almost laughed.

"Thank you for the permission."

Teren winced again.

Good.

Ilyan turned toward the door.

"Ilyan."

He stopped.

"There was one other thing," Teren said.

Ilyan looked back.

"When your father gave me Mara's authentication phrase, he said I should stop searching for the woman who carried W-5 and start asking why W-5 had been issued at all."

"Meaning?"

"I don't know. At the time I thought he was deflecting me. I still might."

Teren's gaze shifted to Vaun.

"But the original request named six credentials before the Academy staffing list named six temporary recipients."

Vaun went still.

"How much earlier?"

"Two days."

Ilyan understood a heartbeat later.

The procedure had not merely answered a staffing shortage.

Someone had requested six substitution credentials before the final six bearers had been selected.

That did not prove murder.

It did mean the number came first.

Vaun opened her notebook again.

"That belongs in the statement."

"Yes."

Ilyan left before his uncle could say anything else.

---

Thessa caught up with him halfway down the garden walk.

Rain had started again, soft enough that neither bothered with a hood.

"Where are you going?" she asked.

"I don't know."

"Good."

He stopped.

"Good?"

"Better than pretending you do."

Water gathered on the dark leaves above them and fell in heavy drops.

Ilyan looked back toward Teren's house.

"He protected her."

"Maybe."

"He protected my father."

"Also maybe."

"Both."

"Probably both."

Ilyan wiped rain from his face.

"I hate that."

"I know."

She stood close enough that their sleeves touched.

Not an embrace.

Not distance either.

"You don't have to decide what he is today," she said.

Ilyan looked at her.

"Sael has infected you."

"Don't say that."

He laughed then.

A real one, brief and miserable.

Thessa smiled.

"There. Better."

"No."

"Slightly less terrible."

"I'll allow it."

They started walking again.

Behind them, Vaun remained in the house taking Teren's formal statement.

Ahead waited a phrase about blue pears, a witness reassignment chain, an original request for six credentials made before six bearers had been chosen, and a father who had apparently known exactly which truth would make his brother stop digging.

Ilyan had wanted the vault to tell him whether Teren had been lying.

It had answered a harder question instead.

Teren had told him less than the truth because he believed silence protected someone.

Whether Mara Venn had ever asked for that protection was another matter entirely.

---

## Six Before Names

By the time Ilyan and Thessa reached the Hall again, the rain had become serious.

It came down in silver cords beyond the arcade, flattened the flower sellers' awnings, and sent everyone with any claim to dignity crowding beneath stone eaves with everyone who had none. Thessa's hair had escaped whatever arrangement it had begun the day in. Ilyan's coat was wet through at the shoulders. Neither had remembered to eat.

Examiner Sael took one look at them and pushed a plate across his desk.

There were two cold meat pies on it.

Ilyan stared.

"Is this an examination?"

"Yes."

"Of what?"

"Whether either of you possesses the judgment required to eat before becoming useless."

Thessa took a pie.

"I like this order."

Sael looked at her. "You are not joining it."

"I like it from a safe distance."

Ilyan sat.

The pie was cold, peppery, and better than anything had a right to be after the afternoon he had just had.

Judge Vaun returned twenty minutes later with rain on her sleeves and Teren's formal statement in a sealed folder beneath her arm.

She did not summarize the interview. She put the folder into Serrat's custody, signed the transfer line, and asked Sael for the old request register.

Sael had already sent for it.

That was why Ilyan trusted him more than he enjoyed being examined by him.

The register arrived with a junior archivist and a wheeled stand because it was large enough to injure someone if dropped. Its leather had once been green. Time had negotiated that down to an uncertain brown.

The archivist opened it to the week before the Emergency Substitution Review.

Teren had remembered correctly.

The request for six credentials had been entered two days before the Academy's final temporary staffing list named the six people who would receive them.

Ilyan read the line once.

Then he read the line above it and the line below it, because he had learned what happened when a single interesting sentence was allowed to become the whole world.

The entries around it were ordinary emergency work. Replacement archive passes after a flooded records room. Three temporary mine-inspection seals after a survey party lost its document chest in a river crossing. A set of hospital transport authorities after a fever ward had been isolated.

Then:

**Emergency Substitution Review. Six bearer-neutral witness-access credentials. West-tower restricted receiving continuity. Bearer designations to follow upon Academy staffing certification.**

The request had entered through Joint Order Liaison — Dmitrigical Review.

The administrative countersignature was the same one Jalen and the second examiner had already assessed as highly consistent with Corren Valeric.

The technical referral had gone to Credential Integrity.

And in the margin, in a clerk's smaller hand, was a notation that had not appeared on the later summary extract.

**Quantity confirmed before bearer assignment. Do not reduce upon staffing substitution.**

Thessa leaned closer.

"That sounds less accidental."

Vaun looked at her.

"It sounds intentional."

"Yes."

"Those aren't the same thing."

Thessa nodded.

Ilyan appreciated that nobody in the room felt obliged to explain why.

The archivist turned the page.

A loose slip had been stitched into the binding by one corner. It was not the request itself. It was a routing query from the clerk who had entered it.

**Bearer count not yet certified. Confirm whether six is provisional maximum or required issue quantity.**

The answer had come back the same afternoon.

**Required issue quantity. Bearer names may vary. Quantity does not.**

No personal name appeared beneath the answer.

Only the office mark of the Contingency Secretariat.

Ilyan sat back.

For a moment he could hear only the rain.

"My father knew," he said.

Vaun's gaze moved to him.

"He countersigned the expenditure."

"He told Teren to ask why six had been requested before the names existed."

"Yes."

"So he knew the sequence mattered."

"Yes."

That was enough. She did not rescue him from the next thought and did not put it into his mouth.

Ilyan looked again at the clerk's question.

Six was not the number produced by the final staffing list.

The final staffing list had been made to satisfy a request that already required six.

Why remained unanswered.

Sael rested one hand on the edge of the stand.

"What would make a quantity fixed while bearers remained interchangeable?"

Thessa said, "Six jobs."

"Perhaps."

"Six doors."

"Perhaps."

Ilyan looked at the west-tower notation.

"Six things that needed credentials whether the same six people showed up or not."

Sael nodded once.

"Better. Still broad."

Vaun pointed to the phrase *witness-access credentials*.

"Find the operational attachment."

The archivist grimaced.

"If it survived."

"If it survived."

The search took an hour and involved three cabinets, two obsolete index systems, and one elderly cross-reference book whose compiler had apparently believed alphabetical order was a decadent modern affectation.

Thessa discovered this last fact first.

"I would like permission to fight the dead clerk."

"Denied," Vaun said.

"On jurisdictional grounds?"

"Practical ones."

The archivist made a choking sound that might have been a laugh.

Ilyan found the attachment because someone eighteen years ago had filed a duplicate under **West Tower — Receiving Continuity** instead of **Emergency Substitution — Witness Access**.

It was four pages.

The first two were dull enough to make him briefly hopeful.

They described a contingency staffing problem created by graduation-week security. Regular Academy receiving personnel had been pulled into ceremony logistics, baggage inspection, guest routing and restricted dormitory closure. The west tower still needed to receive sealed witness materials and admit authorized temporary staff.

The third page listed six functional positions.

Not people.

Positions.

**One restricted receiving clerk.**

**One records porter.**

**One lock specialist.**

**One ward inspector.**

**One witness examiner.**

**One mobile continuity bearer.**

Thessa tapped the last line.

"What's that?"

The archivist did not know.

Sael did.

"A person authorized to carry continuity authority between stations when a normal chain is interrupted. Rare. Usually disaster work."

"What could they carry?"

"Authority. Sometimes sealed material. Sometimes a witness instruction. Depends on the instrument."

Ilyan looked back at the designator list from SV-12 in his memory.

W-1 records porter.

W-2 locksmith.

W-3 receiving clerk.

W-4 ward inspector.

W-6 witness examiner.

W-5 Mara Venn.

Mara had not merely filled a vacant receiving shift.

The temporary duty sheet had put her at third-floor receiving, yes.

But the original functional plan contained one position not accounted for by the ordinary job labels.

Mobile continuity bearer.

"Which designator was that?" he asked.

Vaun had already reached the same question.

"The attachment doesn't assign designators."

"The staffing certification might."

It did not.

The final Academy staffing list named six people and their temporary work assignments, but the credential office had translated those assignments into W-1 through W-6 separately. The surviving certification copy contained no functional-position column.

Ilyan felt irritation rising and welcomed it. Irritation was easier to use than grief.

"So we still don't know whether W-5 was the mobile bearer."

"Correct," Vaun said.

Thessa folded her arms.

"But one of the six was."

"Yes."

The fourth page was an approval sheet.

Most of it duplicated what they already knew.

Then Ilyan saw the distribution line.

The completed credential set was to be released in two groups.

Five credentials to Academy staffing control.

One credential to **Contingency witness continuity custody** for hand delivery to its bearer.

Nobody spoke.

The rain struck the windows hard enough to rattle them.

Ilyan read it again.

Five together.

One separately.

"That one should have its own receipt," Vaun said.

The archivist was already writing down the routing number.

---

The separate receipt survived.

It had been filed in a narrow book of Contingency hand-carries because bureaucracies sometimes preserved terrible things simply by being too stubborn to throw away paper.

The credential was W-5.

The recipient signature was Mara Venn's.

Ilyan did not let himself feel relief at that. They already knew signatures could be copied, inherited defects reproduced, credentials made to look legitimate.

Vaun sent for the old signature exemplars from Mara's payroll and receiving records.

They waited.

Thessa found tea.

The archivist found bread and a small crock of soft cheese.

Somewhere between the second cup and the arrival of the exemplars, Ilyan realized the day had become evening.

Bloomtide light lingered late, but the Hall lamps had been lit.

The hand-carry receipt was examined by two document clerks separately.

Both found Mara's signature consistent with the ordinary exemplars available to them. Neither would certify authorship beyond that.

Vaun accepted the limit.

More useful was the witness line beneath the signature.

**Authentication phrase received and matched.**

The phrase itself was not written there.

A reference number was.

Ilyan stopped breathing for half a second.

"Can we follow that?"

The archivist checked the reference.

"Protected witness authentication ledger. Different custody."

Serrat, from the doorway, said, "Mine."

Nobody had heard him arrive.

Thessa looked at him.

"Do you practice that?"

"No."

"Pity. It would be impressive if deliberate."

Serrat ignored her and read Vaun's inspection authority.

The existing order covered the authentication reference but not any location or reassignment destination attached to it.

That mattered.

Vaun requested only the challenge phrase and its date of registration.

Serrat approved that much.

The protected ledger did not come into the room. An examiner brought a certified extract instead.

The challenge phrase registered to Mara Venn was:

**Ask for the blue pears before frost.**

Ilyan closed his eyes.

His father had known it.

The phrase had been registered three days before Silver Night, at the same time Mara entered protected witness handling for the emergency assignment. Access to the phrase was limited to the bearer, the receiving examiner, and Contingency witness-continuity authority.

Teren's original post-incident review file had not contained it.

That corroborated one important part of his story.

Corren had given Teren information Teren could not have obtained from his own review compartment.

It did not explain how Corren obtained it.

The countersignature already placed him inside the administrative chain. The authentication phrase placed his knowledge closer to witness handling.

Closer was not the same as complete.

Ilyan had become very tired of sentences like that.

He had also become grateful for them.

They were the difference between an investigation and a family legend.

"Who was the receiving examiner?" he asked.

Serrat checked the extract.

"Pel Ardis."

Thessa exhaled.

"Of course."

"That isn't evidence of anything except that Ardis did his assigned work," Vaun said.

"I know. I'm allowed to be annoyed by the shape of it."

Vaun's mouth almost moved.

Almost.

The more interesting line came beneath Ardis's name.

**Continuity authority acknowledgment: C.V.**

Not a signature.

Initials.

No surname.

Ilyan stared at them.

He did not say his father's name.

He did not need to.

Vaun requested the authority roster for that date.

Serrat did not have it in the same room.

"Tomorrow," he said.

Ilyan looked at him.

"Can it be preserved tonight?"

"Already is."

That was the answer he wanted.

---

Damaris was still at the River Ledger when the Hall finally released them.

Not investigating Silver Night.

Working.

Her committee had spent the afternoon discovering that the duplicated dredging-barge invoice was not, in fact, a sophisticated fraud. Two district offices had each paid a copy because one clerk had changed the work-order number after a flood inspection and another had not.

The money was recoverable.

The embarrassment was not.

Rhyl had arrived with supper and no documents.

He had apparently taken his refusal to become an unofficial courier as a personal discipline.

Damaris rewarded this by stealing half his roasted onions.

When Ilyan and Thessa entered the committee anteroom, Rhyl was leaning against the window with his sleeves rolled up and Damaris was sitting sideways in a chair, one shoe off beneath the table, reading a corrected account sheet.

She looked up.

Whatever she saw in Ilyan's face made her put the sheet down.

"Bad?"

"Complicated."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the one I have."

She studied him for another second and accepted it.

Rhyl handed him a wrapped piece of flatbread.

Ilyan took it.

"Does everyone think I don't eat?"

"Today?" Rhyl asked.

"Apparently."

Thessa sat on the edge of Damaris's desk.

"He failed an examination on the subject."

"I passed."

"Under supervision."

Damaris's mouth curved.

It was the first thing all evening that made Ilyan feel twenty-three instead of ancient.

He told them only what Vaun had cleared for the Seven.

Six had been fixed before six names existed.

The original plan described six functions, including one mobile continuity bearer.

Five credentials went through Academy staffing control.

W-5 went separately to Mara.

Mara authenticated receipt with the blue-pears phrase.

The phrase Teren said Corren later used was real and had not been in Teren's review file.

He did not tell them the protected-ledger access structure beyond what Vaun had authorized.

Damaris listened with her fingers resting against Rhyl's wrist.

"So your father knew something he shouldn't have known from Teren's papers."

"Yes."

"But might have known through his own office."

"Yes."

Rhyl looked at Ilyan.

"And Mara actually received W-5?"

"Her signature is consistent. The authentication was accepted."

"Which still doesn't prove she used it at the tower."

"No."

Nobody sighed at the answer.

That, more than anything, told Ilyan how much the last week had changed them.

They had learned to live with a fact ending before they wanted it to.

Damaris put her shoe back on.

"What happens tomorrow?"

"The authority roster. Then the request-entry chronology. Vaun wants to know who had the power to fix six before the staffing list existed."

"And Mara?"

Ilyan looked toward the rain-black windows.

"We verify the reassignment chain without exposing where it ends."

Damaris nodded.

She did not ask whether he wanted to find Mara.

He was grateful for that too.

Rhyl unwrapped another piece of flatbread and handed it to Thessa.

"Lyrion says we're all becoming impossible to invite anywhere."

"Lyrion says that as though it will stop him," Thessa said.

"It will not."

Damaris leaned back against Rhyl's arm.

"Mireth and Caelen?"

"Dinner with her family," Rhyl said. "Apparently Caelen has been informed that touching Mireth under the table is not subtle simply because the tablecloth is long."

Thessa brightened.

"By whom?"

"Her mother."

Ilyan laughed before he could stop himself.

The sound hurt less this time.

For a few minutes they ate and complained about work.

Damaris described the two district clerks who had each been absolutely certain the other office was responsible for the duplicate payment. Rhyl described a Red Hart quartermaster who had requisitioned six waterproof saddle covers and received six waterproof covers for chairs. Thessa described a Warden lieutenant who believed maps became more accurate if he stabbed them with a finger hard enough.

Nobody mentioned Silver Night.

Then Ilyan looked at his friends and remembered the line in the old attachment.

Six functions.

Bearers to follow.

He had spent the afternoon thinking about six as a number chosen before six people.

Now, absurdly, he was sitting with three of the six people who remained from a graduating class that had almost ceased to exist.

He did not say that either.

Some thoughts did not improve by being given to a room.

---

Vaun's runner found him before he left the Ledger.

The note was short.

The original request-entry clerk's routing packet had been located in an off-site judicial store and placed under preservation seal for morning review.

One surviving cover sheet carried a handwritten instruction not present in the central register.

Vaun did not reproduce the whole line in an unsecured note.

She reproduced four words.

**Selection follows credential capacity.**

Ilyan read them twice.

Thessa read them over his shoulder because she had never respected the fictional privacy of a note held openly at chest height.

"That's worse," she said.

"Maybe."

"You hate that word now."

"Deeply."

He folded the note.

The rain had stopped.

Across the canal, lamps trembled in the black water. Bloomtide flowers sagged under the weight of the storm and would probably stand again by morning.

Tomorrow they would read the routing packet.

Tomorrow they might learn whether *selection follows credential capacity* meant nothing more sinister than an administrator filling six predetermined jobs.

Or whether someone had built six usable identities first and chosen the people afterward because the people were the disposable part.

For tonight, the difference remained exactly where it belonged.

Unproven.

---

## Selection Follows Capacity

The routing packet was smaller than Ilyan had expected.

That annoyed him.

After a week of registers that required carts, ledgers that had outlived their clerks, sealed compartments, duplicate receipts and one archive cross-reference system apparently devised by a person who hated the future, he had begun to expect important wrongdoing to require substantial furniture.

The packet sat in the middle of Judge Vaun's table beneath a preservation weight no larger than his palm.

Outside, Bloomtide had recovered from the previous night's rain. Water still shone in the seams of the courtyard stones. The climbing roses against the Hall wall had dropped half their petals into the drains and appeared otherwise unconcerned.

Thessa arrived carrying breakfast.

She put a wrapped parcel beside Ilyan's elbow.

He looked at it.

"I've eaten."

"Good. That's mine."

He looked at her.

She smiled and took the chair beside him.

Examiner Sael came in behind her, saw the food, saw Ilyan, and evidently decided no intervention was required.

Vaun waited until Serrat had signed the morning custody transfer before lifting the preservation weight.

The cover sheet lay on top.

Ilyan already knew the four words in the margin.

**Selection follows credential capacity.**

Seeing them in the clerk's hand made them no clearer.

The rest of the packet consisted of an intake sheet, two routing slips, a Contingency memorandum and a short response from Academy staffing. Nothing had been removed according to the old page count. The string holes matched. So did the numbering.

Vaun slid the memorandum toward Sael.

"Read it in order."

Nobody objected.

The first page was administrative sludge: graduation-week congestion, temporary closure of two ordinary receiving points, restricted witness material expected through the west tower, continuity requirements if a scheduled bearer became unavailable.

The second page explained the six.

Not six people.

Six **capacities**.

Each emergency credential was to be cut with a bounded authority package before a bearer was selected. One admitted the holder through records transfer points. One permitted lock access. One covered receiving. One permitted ward inspection. One allowed witness examination. The sixth carried continuity authority between the other five functions if the normal chain broke.

The bearer's name, likeness and ordinary identifying particulars were to be added afterward.

Thessa stopped chewing.

"So the credential came first."

Sael kept reading.

"The authority package came first."

"That's what I meant."

"It isn't quite."

She considered that and nodded.

Ilyan looked at the memorandum again.

A credential blank was not a person. A capacity was not an identity. The order had not asked the Academy to find six convenient victims for six false names.

It had asked Credential Integrity to prepare six kinds of authority and the Academy to nominate suitable bearers afterward.

That was a real distinction.

It was also not comforting.

The third paragraph contained the sentence Vaun's runner had abbreviated the night before.

**Selection follows credential capacity so that staffing substitution does not require reauthorization of the emergency instrument. Academy staffing should nominate qualified temporary bearers against the attached capacity schedule.**

Thessa leaned back.

"That could be completely legitimate."

"Yes," Ilyan said.

He hated how relieved he sounded.

Vaun did not look at him.

"Keep going."

The attached capacity schedule had survived elsewhere. They had read it yesterday.

What had not survived with that copy was the paragraph beneath it.

**Bearer substitutions may be made by Academy staffing for capacities one through five. Capacity six remains under Contingency witness-continuity assignment and will be delivered separately.**

Nobody needed to ask which credential had gone separately.

W-5.

Mara's.

Ilyan felt the relief leave again.

Thessa folded the paper wrapper around the remains of her breakfast into smaller and smaller squares.

"So five were jobs the Academy could fill. The sixth wasn't."

Sael nodded.

"That is what this says."

"And they still called all six Academy staffing."

"In the summary register."

"Convenient."

Vaun glanced at her.

Thessa lifted one shoulder. "I didn't say criminal."

That earned her the smallest possible concession from Vaun: no correction.

Ilyan read the paragraph again.

Mara had appeared on the final staffing list with a temporary receiving assignment. Yesterday that had looked like a possible translation problem between the six operational functions and six ordinary jobs.

Now it looked deliberate.

The Academy could substitute bearers for five capacities.

It could not substitute the sixth.

Yet Mara had been placed on the Academy list anyway.

"Why put her on the staffing certification?" he asked.

Sael turned to the Academy response.

The answer was there.

**Academy requires all temporary persons present in restricted service areas to appear on the graduation-week staffing certification irrespective of appointing authority. Contingency bearer may therefore be listed under nearest operational duty for access accounting. Appointment remains external.**

Nearest operational duty.

Receiving.

WT-3.

Mara's ordinary-looking assignment had not been her appointing authority at all.

It had been the label the Academy needed in order to account for her presence.

Ilyan sat very still.

The story they had been assembling changed shape without changing any of its pieces.

Mara had not been selected by an Academy clerk to cover an empty receiving shift and then happened to receive W-5.

Contingency had selected her for its own continuity function. The Academy had then fitted that external appointment into its local staffing paperwork.

"Who selected her?" he asked.

Vaun tapped the routing slip.

"That's the next page."

It was not a name.

Of course it was not a name.

The selection authority was listed as **Contingency witness-continuity authority**, with a roster reference and a countersignature requirement.

The roster reference was the one Serrat had preserved overnight.

He opened a thin certified extract.

There were four names authorized to act in that capacity during the week before Silver Night.

Pel Ardis, receiving examiner.

Tavian Veyr, technical credential examiner, limited to technical acceptance.

Oren Savel, request-entry clerk, administrative only.

And **Corren Valeric**, liaison authority for Contingency continuity approvals.

Ilyan read his father's name.

No one spoke for him.

That was becoming one of the things he valued most about the people in this room.

The roster did not say Corren selected Mara.

It said he had the authority to approve a continuity selection.

Ardis also had authority to receive and authenticate the bearer. Savel could enter the request but not choose the bearer. Veyr could reject a technically invalid credential but not appoint the person carrying it.

Two people, then, could have put Mara into the continuity chain at the relevant stage: the Contingency liaison authority and the receiving examiner acting within a valid appointment.

Corren and Ardis.

Ardis was dead.

Corren was not.

Ilyan's hands had gone cold.

Thessa's knee touched his under the table.

She did not look at him.

He left it there.

Vaun turned to the last routing slip.

"There should be an appointment instrument."

Serrat said, "There is a reference."

"Where?"

"Protected witness handling."

"Still yours."

"Still mine."

Thessa looked between them.

"You two make ownership of paperwork sound indecent."

Sael coughed into his hand.

Serrat ignored her with the stamina of a man who had probably ignored much worse for decades.

The appointment instrument was restricted because it named the bearer and described the continuity duty. Vaun's existing order covered Mara's authentication chain but not the substance of the protected assignment.

She drafted a supplemental request.

Not for Mara's later location.

Not for protected witness destinations.

Only for the appointment instrument as it existed before Silver Night, with any later relocation material redacted.

Ilyan watched her narrow the request and understood the kindness of it.

Not kindness to him.

Kindness to Mara.

If she was alive, an eighteen-year-old investigation did not acquire the right to expose her simply because Ilyan desperately wanted answers.

He signed as an interested order member where Sael indicated and then went to work.

Because the Hall had work for him that did not involve his father.

---

For three hours Ilyan indexed oath appeals from people who had made promises under circumstances ranging from tragic to stupid.

One involved a mill partnership, a dead aunt and a goat that had apparently been included in an inheritance settlement through either malice or clerical fatigue.

Another involved a betrothal oath whose parties agreed they wanted to dissolve it and disagreed only about which family should be publicly blamed.

The third was a mining fellowship whose surviving members had discovered that the exact wording of their thirty-year compact required them to maintain a communal supper every Forgeday even though two now lived four days' travel apart.

Ilyan liked that one.

Not because the problem was serious. It was not, particularly.

Because the old men had kept doing it for thirty years before asking whether they still had to.

At midday Sael found him smiling at the file.

"Dangerous."

"What is?"

"Enjoying the work."

"I was told this order was dedicated to truth. Nobody mentioned goats."

"The goats are where truth becomes difficult."

Ilyan looked at him.

Sael's face remained perfectly straight.

"Was that a joke?"

"No."

"Liar."

Sael took the completed index slips and walked away.

Ilyan returned to the mill appeal.

The ordinary work helped.

It did not make Corren's name disappear from the roster. It made the name one fact among other facts instead of the only thing his mind could hold.

By the time Thessa returned from Warden duty in the late afternoon, he had eaten twice without supervision and had almost stopped feeling proud of it.

She arrived in field trousers and a dark Warden coat, dust at the hem where the morning rain had dried into the roads.

"You smell like a stable," he said.

"You smell like old paper."

"Mine is dignified."

"Yours is mildew with institutional authority."

She dropped into the chair opposite him.

He noticed the loose strand of hair stuck to the side of her throat and then noticed himself noticing it.

That had been happening more often.

He did not immediately convert the observation into a crisis.

Thessa caught him looking anyway.

One eyebrow rose.

Ilyan looked back at the file in front of him.

"Don't."

"I didn't say anything."

"You were about to."

"Probably."

Her boot touched his beneath the desk.

Neither moved it.

Serrat appeared in the doorway ten minutes later.

Thessa sighed.

"You absolutely practice that."

"The judge's order was granted."

Ilyan stood.

The appointment instrument had been copied by hand into the protected ledger and countersigned on a separate leaf.

Serrat provided certified extracts of both.

The first line was ordinary enough.

**Bearer: Mara Venn.**

The second was not.

**Capacity: mobile continuity bearer, west-tower witness receiving.**

There it was.

W-5.

Not inferred from the missing job label.

Not reconstructed from who received which credential.

Mara had been the sixth capacity.

The instrument authorized her to carry sealed witness instructions between the west-tower receiving station, the upper review rooms and an emergency continuity point identified only by a protected designator.

That destination remained redacted under Vaun's request.

The duty began at midday on the day before Silver Night and expired at noon after graduation unless revoked earlier.

The appointment line read:

**Nominated: P. Ardis.**

**Approved: C. Valeric.**

Ilyan did not move.

Thessa's boot was no longer touching his. Her hand found the back of his chair instead.

Not him.

The chair.

Close enough to be there. Far enough to let him decide what he needed.

He was grateful for that too.

Corren had approved Mara.

That was now evidence.

It still did not prove why.

The countersignature leaf made the problem stranger.

Ardis had added a handwritten condition beneath the appointment:

**Bearer informed that continuity authority survives local staffing substitution and may be exercised without Academy countersignature if receiving chain is compromised.**

Mara had signed beneath it.

Then, in another hand:

**Approved. Preserve independent bearer authority. — C.V.**

Ilyan read the words until they blurred.

Independent bearer authority.

His father had approved giving Mara the power to act without Academy permission if the receiving chain was compromised.

A man who had helped engineer Silver Night had deliberately preserved an independent witness bearer inside the west tower.

That did not fit neatly into the version of Corren Ilyan carried in his head.

It did not make Corren innocent.

It made him harder to understand.

"Could he have needed her there?" Thessa asked quietly.

Vaun answered before Ilyan could.

"Yes."

"Could he have been protecting her?"

"Yes."

"Could he have been making sure somebody had authority to move something during the attack?"

"Yes."

Thessa looked annoyed.

"I know why you do that. I still dislike it."

"Good."

Ilyan looked at the dates.

Corren had approved Mara's independent authority three days before Silver Night.

He had later known her private authentication phrase.

Afterward, he had used that phrase to persuade Teren not to force open a witness-location chain.

Those facts could describe a man protecting a witness he had knowingly placed in danger.

They could describe a conspirator preserving a useful operative.

They could describe both.

People were inconvenient that way.

Sael pointed to the protected designator.

"The emergency continuity point."

Serrat shook his head before anyone asked.

"Not under this order."

Ilyan surprised himself by saying, "Don't request it yet."

Vaun looked at him.

"Why?"

"Because we don't need where she went. We need what she was told to carry."

Sael's expression changed slightly.

Approval, perhaps.

Or merely interest.

Ilyan continued.

"If the instruction survives without the destination, we can learn what her job was without opening her location chain."

Vaun nodded.

"Better."

Serrat checked the reference sequence.

The assignment had an instruction packet logged separately from the relocation material.

Its title was visible.

**Continuity instruction: preserve witness material if ordinary receiving authority fails.**

The packet itself was sealed under the same protected series.

Vaun requested it with destination and later-location references redacted.

Serrat took the request.

"Tomorrow."

Thessa groaned.

"I am beginning to hate tomorrow."

"Tomorrow is not aware of you," Serrat said.

She stared at him.

He left.

"That," she said, "was definitely a joke."

Ilyan laughed.

This time it did not hurt at all.

Damaris had finished her committee work before sunset.

The duplicated dredging payment had been formally recovered, both district offices had agreed on a single work-order numbering convention, and Lady Maeryn had made three clerks rewrite the new procedure until a fourth clerk could follow it without asking questions.

Damaris considered this a triumph of civilization.

Rhyl considered it evidence that civilization required hobbies.

They met the others at a narrow supper room near the canal where the proprietor knew Lyrion well enough to put them in the back without being asked.

Mireth and Caelen arrived late.

Caelen's collar was crooked.

Mireth's hair was immaculate.

Nobody commented.

"Your collar."

Caelen touched it.

Mireth drank her wine.

"It was raining," Caelen said.

Thessa looked toward the perfectly clear evening beyond the window.

"Terrible storm."

Mireth kicked her under the table.

The conversation moved on.

They talked about work first.

Caelen had spent half the day escorting a Crown petitioner who was convinced every corridor in the palace was a conspiracy because three of them led somewhere he had not intended to go. Mireth had helped adjudicate a living-memory dispute between two orchard families whose trees remembered the same boundary oath differently because one grove had been replanted after a blight. Lyrion had spent the afternoon persuading a minor envoy that being seated four places farther from the dais did not constitute a declaration of war.

Only after the food arrived did Ilyan tell them what Vaun had cleared.

The six capacities had been real operational authorities prepared before bearer selection.

Five bearers could be substituted by Academy staffing.

The sixth could not.

Mara had been the sixth.

Ardis nominated her.

Corren approved her.

And Corren specifically approved preserving her independent authority if the west-tower receiving chain failed.

Caelen stopped with his cup halfway to his mouth.

"Your father gave her authority to act around Academy command?"

"Without Academy countersignature if the receiving chain was compromised."

"Why?"

Ilyan looked at him.

Caelen grimaced.

"Right."

Damaris rested her chin against one hand.

"Does it change what you think of him?"

That was a harder question than the evidentiary ones.

Ilyan took his time.

"It changes what I don't understand."

Nobody tried to improve the answer.

Rhyl tore bread in half and passed one piece to Damaris without looking. She took it the same way.

Mireth watched Ilyan across the table.

"You thought he only wanted the disaster."

"I know he helped make it happen."

"That's not what I asked."

Ilyan looked down at his plate.

He had thought Corren's role was simple in the ugliest possible way. His father had believed the graduating class dangerous to the future arithmetic of the Houses, dangerous to the Valeric position, dangerous to whatever arrangement the adults had decided mattered more than the students under their protection.

That remained true as far as Ilyan knew.

But a man could help build a catastrophe and still try to preserve one person inside it.

A man could have reasons that did not absolve him.

"I thought I understood the shape of it," he said.

Mireth nodded.

That was all.

Damaris changed the subject to Lyrion's envoy.

Lyrion objected that the envoy was a serious diplomatic concern.

Thessa asked whether the fourth seat had survived.

By dessert, Caelen had fixed his collar and Mireth had stopped pretending not to notice when his hand rested against her thigh beneath the table.

The proprietor noticed.

She had the tact of a woman old enough to have seen several generations of newly graduated nobles discover that tablecloths were not invisibility spells.

She brought more wine and said nothing.

Ilyan caught Thessa watching him over the rim of her cup.

Not staring.

Watching.

When their eyes met, she smiled.

He smiled back.

No revelation followed.

No one needed one.

Not with the sealed instruction packet.

With a preservation notice.

Serrat's staff had located it.

The packet was intact.

Its destination references could be segregated without breaking the instruction text.

And one additional enclosure had been found beneath the original fold.

A bearer acknowledgment in Mara's hand.

**Ardis says this is because somebody may try to make the emergency look orderly afterward.**

Ilyan stood in the quiet of his room with the note between his fingers.

Ardis had expected the possibility of a compromised chain before Silver Night.

Corren had approved Mara's independent authority.

And somebody had designed an authentication rule that specifically prevented ordinary Academy command, household authority and even Contingency retrieval from simply taking the packet back from her.

The next question was no longer where Mara had gone.

It was what witness material had been important enough to give one young receiving worker the authority to refuse almost everyone in the building.

Ilyan folded Vaun's note and set it beside the lamp.

Tomorrow again.

He was still beginning to hate it.

## What Mara Carried

The witness packet did not contain testimony.

Ilyan had expected testimony.

He had spent most of the walk to the Hall constructing increasingly elaborate versions of what Mara Venn might have been trusted to carry through a collapsing receiving chain: a sealed deposition, a list of names, a confession, perhaps some dreadful little memorandum in Corren's hand that would finally make the entire thing simple enough to hate without qualification.

Instead Serrat laid three certified leaves on Judge Vaun's table and said, "Inventory only."

Thessa, who had arrived before Ilyan and was eating a pear with the proprietary air of someone who had stolen it from a more respectable breakfast, looked disappointed.

"We waited overnight for an inventory?"

"You waited overnight because the records office closes."

"That is less dramatic."

"Most things are."

Serrat sat.

Ilyan took the chair beside Thessa. She had left a second pear near his hand.

He did not ask whether it was for him. He ate it.

The certified extract preserved Mara's instruction packet while removing the emergency continuity point and every later relocation reference. Vaun had insisted on that boundary the previous evening. Serrat had apparently spent part of the morning making sure the redactions did not destroy the older document's internal meaning.

The first leaf described the packet itself.

**Continuity Witness Packet — WT-C/6.**

One sealed envelope of original witness intake slips.

One bound set of west-tower receiving corrections.

One sealed comparative sheet of sponsor and receiving impressions.

One emergency casualty-reconciliation list, provisional.

One memorandum of disputed substitutions.

And one small evidence pouch containing **three rejected credential fragments**.

Ilyan stopped at the last line.

Thessa leaned over his shoulder.

"Rejected?"

Sael read the next paragraph.

The fragments had been produced during the week before graduation while the emergency substitution procedure was being implemented. All three had failed ordinary issue for different reasons. One carried an incomplete inherited sponsor defect. One showed excessive secondary-stage broadening. The third had passed visual inspection but failed a receiving-pressure comparison.

CR-88 defects.

Not the finished method.

Failures of it.

Ilyan looked at Vaun.

"Ardis put the failed pieces in the witness packet."

"Yes."

"Why?"

Vaun tapped the second leaf.

There was no speech about what the evidence meant. There was only Ardis's instruction, copied into the protected record before Silver Night.

**If emergency credentials are later challenged, independent examination must include failed production examples. Successful specimens alone cannot establish whether observed uniformity arose naturally from ordinary preparation or from controlled defect inheritance.**

Thessa stopped eating.

"He was building a comparison set."

Sael nodded.

"For someone outside the chain."

The independent examiner was still not named on the first leaf. The protected designator occupied the space where a person ought to have been.

Ilyan read the sentence again.

Ardis had not merely feared that someone would tidy the paperwork after an emergency. He had anticipated a specific evidentiary problem: if the issued credentials later appeared suspiciously uniform, the people who had made them could point to the surviving successful credentials and say that uniformity was simply what competent emergency production looked like.

The failures made that argument harder.

They showed the process developing.

They showed what had been corrected.

They showed which defects had been deliberately carried forward and which had been discarded.

"He expected a dispute," Ilyan said.

"Yes," Vaun said.

"Before Silver Night."

"Yes."

That did not mean Ardis expected a massacre.

Ilyan did not need Vaun to say it. He had finally learned to leave a fact at the size it actually possessed.

Ardis expected the emergency credential system to be disputed. He expected ordinary custody to be vulnerable to later pressure. He arranged for an independent comparison set to survive outside that custody.

That was enough.

The second leaf contained Mara's handling instructions.

She was not to open the packet. She was not to add to it. If the ordinary west-tower chain remained intact through graduation, she was to return it to Ardis against the blue phrase and a second countersignature.

If the chain failed, she was to carry it to the independent examiner.

The examiner's designator had survived.

**VEIL-3.**

Ilyan stared at it.

Sael's expression went very still.

Thessa looked between them.

"That's yours."

"Truth Beyond the Veil," Ilyan said.

Serrat corrected him without pedantry. "A legacy cross-order designation. Not necessarily this Hall."

"But Veil."

"Yes."

The third leaf was a routing key.

It did not reveal Mara's destination. It did not reveal where she had been relocated after Silver Night. It did reveal which institution held the VEIL-3 appointment at the time the instruction was written.

**Independent examiner: Truth Beyond the Veil, external oath and credential review seat.**

No personal name appeared.

The appointment reference did.

Sael stood.

"That register is downstairs."

Serrat looked at him.

"The historical seat register is downstairs."

"You knew?"

"I know where our registers are."

Thessa bit into her pear again.

"I like him more every day."

Serrat did not dignify that.

Vaun requested the register.

This time nobody had to wait until tomorrow.

The historical seat register was heavy enough that the archive runner brought it on a small wheeled stand.

Ilyan found this reassuring. Important wrongdoing had once again acquired furniture.

The external oath and credential review seat was not a permanent office. Truth Beyond the Veil appointed an examiner to it when another institution required an independent person capable of reviewing oath language, authentication practice and credential disputes without belonging to the issuing chain.

In the month of Silver Night, VEIL-3 had been held by **Examiner Edrin Haleth**.

Ilyan did not know the name.

Sael did.

"He taught here."

"Past tense?" Thessa asked.

"He died eleven years ago."

Serrat turned the register toward himself.

Haleth's appointment had begun two months before Silver Night and ended nine days after it.

Not because he died.

Because he resigned the seat.

The reason field contained only a cross-reference.

**See recusation statement VEIL-3/R-9.**

Vaun asked for it.

Serrat sent the runner.

Ilyan felt the old impatience begin in his chest and made himself look out the window instead.

Bloomtide sunlight had finally burned the last dampness from the courtyard. Two junior clerks were carrying a box between buildings and arguing about which one of them had packed it badly. Somewhere beyond the wall a cart wheel hit a rut hard enough to produce a curse of admirable clarity.

Thessa nudged his ankle.

"You're doing the thing."

"What thing?"

"Trying not to think by thinking very intensely about not thinking."

"That's not a thing."

"It is on your face."

"My face is private."

"It is literally the least private part of you."

He looked at her.

She smiled around the last bite of pear.

There were days when Ilyan suspected Thessa had been placed in his life by a minor god whose domain was making solemnity difficult.

He was increasingly grateful for the god.

The runner returned.

Haleth's recusation statement was short.

He had not resigned because he objected to the work.

He had resigned because the packet never reached him.

**Continuity packet WT-C/6 expected under protected transfer. Bearer failed to report to VEIL-3 receiving point. No lawful substitute bearer authorized. Contingency office requested closure on grounds of presumed bearer death. I decline closure absent packet recovery, bearer confirmation, or independent evidence of destruction.**

Below that, in a different hand, someone had recorded the administrative outcome.

**Seat discontinued. Matter retained under protected unresolved custody.**

Ilyan read it twice.

Mara had not delivered the packet to Haleth.

At least not through the expected route.

Contingency had tried to close the matter by presuming her dead.

Haleth refused.

"Did he keep looking?" Ilyan asked.

Serrat checked the cross-references.

There were three.

Two were ordinary correspondence requesting confirmation from casualty reconciliation and protected witness handling.

The third was later.

Six months after Silver Night.

A private memorandum from Haleth to the then-Senior Examiner of Truth Beyond the Veil.

Access classification: internal historical, no living-witness location data.

Vaun did not need a new order.

Serrat retrieved it himself.

Haleth's memorandum contained no revelation about Mara's location.

It contained a complaint.

A very professional complaint, which in some institutions was simply anger with page numbers.

He wrote that casualty reconciliation had first listed Mara Venn among the dead, then amended her status to **identity unresolved**, then transferred the underlying correction into protected handling. Contingency nevertheless continued citing the original casualty presumption when seeking closure of WT-C/6.

Haleth considered that unacceptable.

He also recorded that, four days after Silver Night, someone had attempted to deliver a substitute packet to his office.

Not Mara's packet.

A reconstructed packet.

The inventory was almost identical.

Almost.

It contained successful credential exemplars.

It did not contain the three rejected fragments.

It did not contain the original disputed-substitution memorandum.

And the casualty-reconciliation list had been replaced by a clean certified copy produced after the first corrections were already underway.

Haleth refused it.

Thessa said, very softly, "Somebody did try to make it orderly afterward."

No one corrected her.

This time the document did.

Ilyan felt something cold move beneath his ribs.

Ardis's warning had not been paranoia.

Someone had attempted to give the independent examiner a cleaner evidence set after the disaster.

That still did not identify the person.

Haleth's memorandum did.

Not the architect.

The courier.

**Substitute packet tendered by Contingency administrative messenger under authority slip C-17. Messenger not implicated in packet preparation. Authority slip retained.**

The slip had a countersignature field.

Serrat followed the reference.

It was not in Truth Beyond the Veil custody.

It had been returned to the issuing office under protest because the messenger required proof of refusal.

The issuing office was the Dmitrigical Tribunal Contingency Secretariat.

The same Secretariat whose duplicate expense countersignatures had gone to River Ledger audit.

Damaris's world again.

Ilyan sat back.

The evidence had stopped behaving like a single road. It was becoming a net.

"Can the River Ledger copy identify the authority?" Thessa asked.

"Possibly," Vaun said.

"And the original?"

"Possibly."

"You are very committed to that word."

"It survives contact with facts."

Thessa looked offended by the principle.

Ilyan almost laughed.

Then he noticed the date on Haleth's memorandum.

Six months after Silver Night.

"Why didn't this go anywhere?"

Sael answered.

"It did."

He pointed to the final line.

**Referral submitted for cross-order inquiry. Returned without action: jurisdiction superseded by Revision Seventeen protected review.**

Revision Seventeen.

The same revision filed before Silver Night.

The same protected review chain into which CR-88 had been drawn.

The same chain whose successor restrictions Teren later renewed.

Ilyan closed his eyes.

Not because the answer was complete.

Because the shape was becoming unpleasantly coherent.

Someone had built an emergency credential system using a method capable of reproducing inherited defects. Ardis had preserved failures and disputed substitutions for independent examination. Mara had been given the packet and authority to bypass compromised command. Silver Night happened. Mara vanished from ordinary custody. A clean substitute packet appeared without the failures or the original disputed memorandum. Haleth refused it. His later referral was swallowed by the very protected review architecture connected to the emergency substitution revision.

And Corren had been close to more than one part of that system.

Close was still not the same as responsible for every part.

Ilyan held onto that distinction because he needed the truth more than he needed his father to become simple.

Vaun gathered the certified leaves.

"River Ledger first."

Ilyan looked up.

"Why?"

"Because the duplicate authority slip may tell us who authorized the substitute packet without opening any witness-location chain."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then we ask the next question."

Thessa sighed.

"Tomorrow."

Serrat looked at her.

"Ledgerday afternoon."

"That's worse. Now tomorrow has an appointment."

Damaris was not available merely because the investigation wanted her.

Ilyan discovered this when he sent a note to the River Ledger and received a reply in her hand telling him that the transport committee was sitting until third bell, that Maeryn had found another district using the old numbering convention, and that civilization had once again failed in a way requiring tables.

At the bottom she had added:

**If nobody is actively dying, wait. If somebody is actively dying, ask Rhyl whether they can do it more slowly.**

Ilyan showed Thessa.

"She's adapting well to public service," Thessa said.

So they waited.

Ilyan spent the afternoon on oath appeals. Thessa returned to the Wardens. Vaun went to court. Sael made him correct two indexing errors and refused to admit that one of them had been caused by a filing convention no sane person would anticipate.

She was still in committee clothes: river-blue jacket, narrow silver braid at the cuffs, hair pinned in a style that had begun the day precise and was now losing a disciplined war against humidity. Rhyl wore Red Hart leathers and carried her committee folio because she had apparently handed it to him somewhere between the Ledger and the Hall and neither had considered this remarkable.

Damaris put both palms on Vaun's table.

"Who has offended accounting?"

"Contingency."

"Again?"

Vaun showed her Haleth's authority-slip reference.

Damaris read it.

Her expression changed from amusement to concentration without ceremony.

"C-17 is not enough."

Rhyl set her folio beside her.

No one asked her to call in a favor.

Ilyan noticed that too.

He was becoming irritatingly attentive to the difference between access and leverage.

When the paperwork was done, Damaris leaned back against Rhyl without looking for him first. He put a hand at her waist with the easy certainty of practice.

"Anything else?"

Ilyan told her about Mara's packet.

Not the protected destination.

There was no need.

He told her about the rejected fragments, Haleth, the substitute packet and the missing authority slip.

Damaris listened without interrupting.

When he finished, she looked at Vaun.

"Someone reconstructed the packet without the ugly pieces."

"Yes."

"And tried to close the original because Mara was supposedly dead."

"Yes."

"While her casualty status had already changed."

"According to Haleth's memorandum."

Damaris exhaled through her nose.

Rhyl's thumb moved once against her side.

She covered his hand with hers.

"I'll tell Maeryn this is a judicial audit retrieval, not a family investigation."

"Thank you," Ilyan said.

"Don't thank me yet. She may charge you interest."

"On evidence?"

"She is a banker."

That evening the seven ate together again, though not all at once.

Mireth arrived first and Caelen twenty minutes later, apologizing because a guard captain had discovered that the fastest way to educate a new officer was apparently to give him three contradictory escort schedules and wait. Lyrion came directly from a diplomatic supper and stole food from Thessa's plate. She stabbed his hand with a fork hard enough to make him reconsider but not hard enough to injure him, which appeared to be an established boundary between them.

Damaris and Rhyl left early.

Nobody asked why.

The innkeeper, who had begun recognizing the seven as a recurring weather system, merely moved their untouched dessert into a covered box and handed it to Rhyl on the way out.

Mireth watched them go.

"They are not eating that tonight."

Caelen choked on his wine.

Thessa laughed so hard she had to put her head down.

Ilyan found himself laughing with them.

For several minutes Silver Night was not in the room.

That mattered.

Later, walking home beside Thessa, he realized he had spent an entire supper without once thinking about Corren.

The realization immediately made him think about Corren.

"Damn it," he said.

Thessa glanced at him.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"Convincing."

They walked beneath the dappled shadow of canal-side trees. Bloomtide leaves were still new enough to shine pale at the edges under the lamps. Somewhere across the water someone was playing a stringed instrument badly and with commitment.

At Ilyan's turning, Thessa stopped.

"You're all right?"

He considered the question.

"No."

She nodded.

"Better than yesterday."

"Yes."

"Good."

She kissed his cheek.

It was quick, warm and entirely unsurprising until after it happened.

Then she walked away.

Ilyan stood under the tree for several seconds.

He touched the place with two fingers.

There was no revelation.

No sudden reclassification of seventeen years of friendship.

Only the lingering warmth of her mouth and the inconvenient fact that he wanted her to do it again.

He went home before he could make the thought more complicated than it deserved.

## The Duplicate Slip

The River Ledger did not lose documents often.

It misplaced them with exquisite precision.

Damaris explained the distinction while leading Ilyan and Judge Vaun through a corridor whose windows looked down over the river quays. Morning light lay white on the water. Barges moved beneath the bridges in patient lines, their cargo awnings striped with house marks, guild marks, customs chalk and the less official symbols dockworkers used when they wished to communicate that a particular captain was an ass.

"Lost means nobody knows where it is," Damaris said. "Misplaced means three departments know exactly where it is and disagree about which one has the authority to admit it."

"That sounds worse," Ilyan said.

"It is much more respectable."

Maeryn had obtained the historical Contingency packet before breakfast.

She had also refused to let Damaris carry it out of audit custody.

That had annoyed Damaris for almost seven minutes, which Ilyan considered a remarkably short recovery time for being told no by one's own family.

The audit room waiting for them was narrow, cool and aggressively plain. No portraits. No ornamental screens. No upholstered chairs capable of concealing papers beneath cushions. The River Ledger apparently believed that comfort encouraged accounting irregularities.

Lady Maeryn Caladren-Voss sat at the end of the table with a sealed grey packet before her and a cup of tea at her right hand.

Jalen occupied the chair beside her.

He looked rested, which Ilyan found personally offensive.

"Judge," Maeryn said.

"Lady Maeryn."

Her eyes moved to Ilyan.

"You look worse."

"Thank you."

"It was not criticism."

Damaris sat opposite her aunt.

"Was it there?"

Maeryn put one finger on the packet.

"The month survived intact. Contingency's year-end reconciliation survived intact. The duplicate authority series survived intact."

Damaris's shoulders loosened.

"And C-17?"

"Is inside."

Nobody spoke for a moment.

Maeryn looked at Vaun.

"Your order permits inspection and certified copying of the duplicate authority slip and its attached accounting references. It does not permit removal of the original."

"Agreed."

"Good. I dislike arguing with judges before tea."

"After tea?"

"Much easier."

Damaris hid a smile in her hand.

Maeryn broke the archive seal.

The packet contained the usual dull machinery by which institutions remembered spending money: messenger fees, carriage reimbursements, sealing wax, clerical overtime, protected-room charges, two meals purchased at an hour sufficiently late that somebody had apparently considered hunger an administrative emergency.

C-17 was halfway through.

It was a narrow duplicate slip attached to a messenger disbursement voucher.

The messenger's name meant nothing to Ilyan.

The purpose line did.

**Protected evidentiary substitution delivery — VEIL-3 external review.**

The issuing office was Contingency.

The administrative authorization field bore a clerk's mark.

The personal countersignature beneath it was not Corren's.

Ilyan knew that before Jalen leaned closer.

He had seen enough of his father's writing over the past week to recognize the absence of it.

The hand was narrower. More vertical. The final stroke of the surname initial turned inward instead of cutting away.

Damaris did not ask him what he thought.

Jalen took out his comparison lens.

"Do we have a known sample?"

Maeryn slid another folder across the table.

"Three."

Ilyan looked at her.

"You already suspected someone."

"No. I already knew which offices had authority to countersign Contingency evidentiary movement at that date. That produced four possible officeholders. One was absent from the capital. One had delegated authority but no surviving hand sample in this archive. Two have ordinary financial signatures here. I brought what the Ledger possesses."

That was, Ilyan thought, the difference between investigation and guessing performed in expensive clothing.

Jalen worked without commentary.

He compared line pressure, initial construction, the shortened office flourish and the habit of crossing the final vertical through the preceding letter.

Then he sat back.

"Highly consistent with **Deputy Secretary Neral Voss**."

Damaris went very still.

Ilyan looked at her.

The surname was not Caladren-Voss.

Not necessarily.

Elaranthi surnames had an unfortunate habit of repeating themselves across three centuries of marriages, cadet lines, legitimizations and people who had simply been born with insufficient concern for future archivists.

Maeryn answered the question before anyone asked it.

"No close relation to my branch. Voss was his birth family. Minor river gentry. His daughter married into Caladren service later."

Damaris nodded once.

Jalen examined the second and third samples.

"I would want another examiner before putting it in a finding."

"Already requested," Vaun said.

Damaris glanced at her.

"You do enjoy arriving with tomorrow already arranged."

"Tomorrow is unreliable."

The duplicate slip had an attachment reference.

Not the substitute packet itself.

A justification code.

**C-17/J2 — evidentiary continuity correction following bearer failure.**

Below it, in a cramped accounting hand, someone had written:

**Charge to protected review implementation, Revision 17.**

Ilyan felt his jaw tighten.

There it was again.

Revision Seventeen had not merely swallowed Haleth's later inquiry. Its implementation account had paid for the messenger who brought him the cleaned substitute packet four days after Silver Night.

That still did not prove Revision Seventeen had been created for that purpose.

But the same administrative structure was no longer merely nearby.

It had paid the bill.

Maeryn turned the slip over.

There was writing on the back.

One sentence.

**Original continuity packet unrecoverable; substitute prepared from retained office copies to permit independent closure.**

Vaun read it twice.

"Prepared by whom?"

No name.

Only another reference.

**Preparation certificate: C-17/P.**

Damaris closed her eyes.

"Please tell me that survived."

Maeryn looked almost sympathetic.

"Not in the expense packet."

"Of course not."

"But the disbursement index gives its destination."

She turned the ledger toward them.

C-17/P had been returned after payment reconciliation to the **Contingency Secretariat implementation file**.

That file had later transferred into Revision Seventeen protected review.

Ilyan laughed once.

It was not amusement.

"Everything goes there."

Vaun looked at him.

"Several things go there."

He rubbed both hands over his face.

"I know."

Damaris nudged his boot under the table.

Not comfort exactly.

Contact.

He let his foot remain against hers for a moment.

Maeryn watched the exchange and said nothing.

Older adults in Elaranth had apparently developed the useful skill of noticing everything and choosing which fraction deserved speech.

Jalen returned to the accounting notation.

"There's something else."

He pointed to the charge allocation.

The messenger fee had been paid from Revision Seventeen implementation.

The packet-preparation labor had not.

That charge had been transferred from another cost center.

**Academy casualty reconciliation — emergency clerical support.**

Damaris frowned.

"Why would casualty reconciliation pay for rebuilding a credential packet?"

"It might not have known it was," Jalen said. "Cost centers are not descriptions of moral purpose. Someone could have assigned clerical hours there because the staff were already processing Silver Night records."

"Or because the casualty list was one of the things being replaced."

"Also possible."

Vaun tapped the code.

"Can you identify the transfer authority?"

Maeryn nodded toward a second ledger.

"That is why I asked for the month rather than the slip."

Damaris smiled despite herself.

"I forgive you for not letting me carry the packet."

"I was devastated by your displeasure."

They found the transfer six pages later.

It had been authorized the morning after Silver Night.

Not by Neral Voss.

By **Corren Valeric**.

Ilyan did not move.

His father's signature sat beneath a mundane allocation of clerical hours: twelve staff-days from Academy casualty reconciliation made available to Contingency evidentiary reconstruction and closure support.

The words were ordinary.

That was almost worse.

Corren had not signed the later messenger slip.

He had not personally countersigned the cleaned packet's delivery to Haleth.

But the morning after Silver Night, he had authorized the staff pool from which the reconstruction labor was paid.

"He knew they were rebuilding it," Ilyan said.

Vaun did not answer immediately.

"He knew he was assigning casualty-reconciliation clerical capacity to evidentiary reconstruction and closure support."

Ilyan looked at her.

"That's what I said."

"No."

He hated her a little for that.

Which was one of the reasons he trusted her.

Vaun continued, "We do not yet know what packet he believed they were reconstructing, what he knew had been omitted, or whether he saw the finished substitute."

Ilyan looked back at the signature.

Corren had already been guilty of enough.

There was no need to make him guilty of things the paper had not proved.

That discipline had become exhausting.

It was also the only thing separating investigation from inheritance.

Damaris's boot touched his again.

This time he pressed back.

---

The second handwriting examiner arrived before noon.

She agreed with Jalen.

Neral Voss had almost certainly countersigned C-17.

The finding went into Vaun's file with the usual careful language and none of the satisfaction that fiction assigned to identifying a name.

Neral had been dead for nine years.

His office papers had been distributed between the Tribunal administrative archive and a family deposit in a minor river-town record house.

Neither collection could be opened that afternoon.

There was, however, one living person who had worked directly beneath him.

Oren Savel.

Ilyan stared at the name when Maeryn found it in the personnel index.

Savel again.

The request-entry clerk who had remembered Pel Ardis.

The clerk who had remembered that the technical question came before the bearer names.

The clerk who had preserved notes because Ardis had become uneasy after Silver Night.

"Did he work for Neral?" Ilyan asked.

"Temporarily," Maeryn said. "Four months. Contingency borrowed clerical staff from three offices during graduation season."

Vaun looked at Ilyan.

"We speak to him again."

"Today?"

"If he agrees."

Thessa would have said something rude about miracles.

Ilyan missed her suddenly.

Not because he needed help with the evidence.

Because the morning had become full of his father again, and Thessa had developed the irritating habit of making rooms containing his father easier to breathe in even when Corren himself was only ink.

He sent her a note before they left the Ledger.

**Found the duplicate. Father authorized the clerical transfer. Not the delivery. Savel may know the countersigner. I am all right. Mostly.**

He stared at the last two words.

Then left them.

Her reply reached him before they crossed the second bridge.

**Mostly is allowed. Eat something.**

Beneath it:

**Also, if you get yourself murdered before supper I will be furious.**

He folded the note carefully and put it inside his coat.

Vaun pretended not to notice.

---

Oren Savel agreed to see them at fourth bell.

His rooms smelled of paper, old wood and the fennel broth simmering on a small kitchen stove. He had apparently decided that retirement meant no longer separating workspaces from soup.

Ilyan approved.

Savel read the certified copy of C-17 in silence.

When he reached Neral's countersignature, his mouth tightened.

"Yes."

Vaun waited.

Savel looked at her.

"You want more than yes."

"Usually."

He set the paper down.

"Neral signed those. Not all of them. Anything that moved outside Contingency after the incident went across his desk. He was furious for about a week."

"At what?"

"Everything."

"More useful than that."

Savel sighed.

"At Ardis. At the casualty office. At the Academy. At whoever had decided an emergency credential procedure needed three institutions and five kinds of seal. At Corren Valeric for changing instructions after the alarm. At Teren for asking questions. At Haleth for refusing closure. Pick a direction. Neral had enough anger for it."

Ilyan leaned forward.

"Changing what instructions?"

Savel looked at him.

Recognition passed through his face.

Not surprise at the surname.

Calculation about whether the answer belonged to the young man sitting before him.

Then he looked at Vaun.

"Can I answer that?"

"If you remember it from your own work."

"I do."

He rubbed his thumb along the edge of the table.

"The morning after the alarm, Corren changed the casualty-reconciliation priority."

"From what to what?"

"From body confirmation first to credential-and-bearer reconciliation first for the west tower."

Ilyan felt the room narrow.

"Why?"

"He said there was a bearer missing who might not be dead."

Mara.

Nobody said her name.

They did not need to.

"Did he identify the bearer?" Vaun asked.

"Not to me. He gave Neral a protected designator and told him not to close the west-tower continuity chain on casualty presumption. Neral argued. Corren won."

That did not fit neatly with the later attempt to close Mara as presumed dead.

Which meant either Corren changed position, someone else overrode him, or different parts of Contingency were working at cross-purposes.

Ilyan felt something almost like relief.

Not because the contradiction made his father better.

Because contradictions were real.

Real institutions contained people who disagreed, duplicated work, concealed things from one another, followed obsolete instructions and occasionally spent an entire morning defeating their own previous afternoon.

A conspiracy that behaved like a single mind was usually a story told after the fact.

"Did Neral prepare the substitute packet?" Vaun asked.

Savel shook his head.

"No."

"Who did?"

"I don't know. Neral ordered it prepared."

"On whose instruction?"

Savel's gaze moved to the certified slip.

"That was the argument."

"Which argument?"

"Haleth wouldn't close. Neral wanted something delivered that would let him examine the credential question without the missing bearer. Teren said the failed fragments mattered. Neral said they were unavailable. Teren said then the packet was incomplete."

Ilyan stared at him.

"Teren knew about the substitute packet?"

"He knew they were trying to reconstruct one. I don't know if he saw what they sent."

Another distinction.

Another irritatingly necessary distinction.

Savel continued.

"Corren came in halfway through."

"And?"

"He told Neral not to use the word closure."

Vaun's eyebrows rose slightly.

"Why?"

"Because, he said, there was nothing to close until the bearer was found or confirmed dead."

Silence.

Ilyan heard the broth simmering in the next room.

His father had helped build the disaster.

His father had authorized Mara's independent role.

His father had supplied Teren with Mara's authentication phrase later.

His father had also, the morning after Silver Night, resisted closing her continuity chain on a false casualty presumption.

And his clerical allocation had nevertheless enabled the reconstruction of the packet somebody later tried to use for precisely that closure.

People were unbearable.

Dead fathers especially.

"Then why did Neral send it?" Ilyan asked.

Savel looked tired.

"Because Neral thought Haleth was being impossible. Because the office was drowning. Because somebody above him wanted the matter reduced to something administratively containable. Because he believed the successful credentials were enough to answer the narrow technical question. I don't know which reason mattered most."

"Who was above him?"

"The Contingency Secretary."

"Name?"

Savel hesitated.

Then gave it.

**Secretary Avarn Dethril.**

The name was not Valeric.

Not Dmitrigical.

Not one Ilyan knew.

Vaun wrote it down.

"Living?"

"Last I heard."

"Where?"

"Retired north."

"That is not a place."

Savel almost smiled.

"It becomes one when you are old enough."

Vaun did not smile back, but the corner of her mouth considered treason.

"What did Dethril want?"

"I never heard him say."

"What did you observe?"

Savel thought.

"He wanted the emergency system defended as lawful. He wanted the Academy casualty process separated from the credential review. He wanted Mara treated as a casualty problem rather than a witness-continuity problem."

There it was.

Not motive.

Behavior.

"Did Corren agree?" Ilyan asked.

"Not about Mara."

The answer came quickly.

"About the rest?"

"Sometimes."

Ilyan looked down at his hands.

He had spent years wanting his father to become a shape he could understand.

Monster would have been easiest.

Martyr would have been worse.

The records offered neither.

Corren was becoming what most dangerous people eventually became under sufficient examination: a man who had made choices, some monstrous, some protective, some compromised, some perhaps merely bureaucratic, and who had been surrounded by other people making choices of their own.

It did not reduce the dead.

It did make hatred more work.

Savel rose to rescue his broth before it became evidence of another institutional failure.

When he returned, he brought bowls.

Vaun accepted one.

Ilyan blinked at her.

"What?" she said.

"Nothing."

They ate.

The soup was good.

That annoyed him too.

---

Thessa found him after dusk beneath the old oak near the Academy wall.

He had not asked her to come there.

He had only told her where he was going.

Apparently that distinction had become meaningless between them.

Bloomtide evening had softened the grounds. The grass smelled green and damp. Lanterns were beginning to appear along the paths, one by one, and somewhere in the residence court a group of new graduates were laughing with the heedless volume of people who had not yet learned how quickly adulthood could turn laughter into evidence, leverage or memory.

Thessa sat beside him without speaking.

Their shoulders touched.

Ilyan gave her the short version.

Not every reference number.

Not every signature.

Neral. The reconstructed packet. Corren's clerical transfer. Savel's memory that Corren had refused to close Mara as dead. Secretary Dethril's preference for separating the casualty problem from the credential problem.

When he finished, Thessa said, "That is inconvenient."

"Yes."

"You wanted him simpler."

"Yes."

"He's still guilty."

"Yes."

"And he still did something that may have kept Mara from being administratively buried."

Ilyan looked at her.

"You make it sound easy."

"No. I make it sound like two things."

He laughed despite himself.

She leaned her head against his shoulder.

For a while they watched the lanterns.

Then Ilyan said, "I kept your note."

"Which one?"

"The one threatening me over supper."

"That wasn't a threat."

"It said you'd be furious if I got murdered."

"A statement of future emotional condition."

"Very legal."

"I've been around you too much."

He turned his head.

Her hair brushed his cheek.

The previous evening she had kissed him there and left him with the startling discovery that he wanted repetition.

Now she was close enough that he could feel the warmth of her face without touching it.

He could have said something clever.

He did not.

He leaned the small remaining distance and kissed her.

Not dramatically.

Not with the desperate competence of lovers in songs who had apparently been born knowing where every hand belonged.

It was brief, uncertain and very soft.

Thessa went still.

Ilyan pulled back half an inch.

"Was that—"

She kissed him again before he could finish.

That answered enough.

Her hand came up to the side of his neck. His found her waist almost by accident. The second kiss lasted longer, still careful, carrying years of friendship into a new grammar neither of them had practiced together.

When they parted, Thessa rested her forehead against his.

"You took your time."

He stared at her.

"Yesterday I didn't know I wanted to do that."

"Yesterday you knew enough to look disappointed when I only kissed your cheek."

"I did not."

"Your face is the least private part of you."

He groaned.

She laughed against his mouth and kissed him a third time.

The old oak did not care.

The Academy windows did not darken in shock.

Somewhere on the path an elderly groundskeeper saw them, altered course by three polite steps and continued carrying a watering can with the serene indifference of someone who had spent a century watching graduates discover one another under trees.

When Thessa finally leaned back, her hand remained in his.

"What happens tomorrow?"

It was not a question about them.

Not only about them.

Ilyan looked toward the Hall lights beyond the wall.

"We find Dethril."

"And the preparation certificate."

"And the original casualty correction."

"And then?"

"Then we see which one lies less."

Thessa squeezed his fingers.

"Good."

They stayed beneath the oak until the lanterns were fully lit.

Tomorrow had acquired another appointment.

---

## The Preparation Certificate

The preparation certificate was exactly where every surviving record said it ought to be.

This did not make it easy to obtain.

By midmorning, Ilyan had acquired a new appreciation for the difference between a document being lost and a document being protected by three offices whose seals had outlived two of the people who invented them.

Judge Vaun stood at the counter in the Hall of Veils while Examiner Sael read the supplemental order for the second time.

"It names C-17/P," Sael said.

"Yes."

"It authorizes inspection of the preparation certificate, attachments incorporated by reference, and any source schedule physically filed with it."

"Yes."

"It does not authorize opening unrelated Revision Seventeen material merely because somebody filed it in the same box."

"I wrote it that way."

Sael looked over the paper at her.

"I know. I am enjoying the novelty."

Vaun's expression did not move.

Ilyan suspected she was enjoying herself too.

Thessa, beside him, leaned close enough that her sleeve brushed his.

"Do they always flirt like this?" she murmured.

Ilyan nearly choked.

Sael's eyes shifted toward them.

Thessa smiled with the perfect innocence of someone who had never possessed it.

Ilyan looked at the floor.

The previous night's kisses had altered nothing obvious about the Hall. The same lamps burned in their niches. The same clerks carried the same tied bundles through the same narrow doors. Sael remained irritating. Vaun remained constitutionally incapable of hurrying merely because everyone else wanted her to.

What had changed was that Ilyan could feel Thessa beside him with absurd precision.

The warmth of her shoulder.

The faint scent of cedar soap in her hair.

The place where their hands almost touched against the edge of the counter.

He had known all of those things before.

Now his mind had become professionally interested in them.

It was extremely inconvenient.

Sael handed the order to a clerk.

"Restricted implementation compartment. C-17/P only, plus incorporated attachments. Two witnesses. No loose copying until the document schedule is checked."

The clerk nodded and disappeared through the inner door.

Thessa waited until it closed.

"You are red."

"I am not."

"You are."

"This building is warm."

"It is made of stone."

"Warm stone."

Vaun turned her head slightly.

"If either of you requires a recess to resolve the thermal properties of the Hall, say so now."

Thessa bit the inside of her cheek.

Ilyan decided that dying would be simpler.

Sael's mouth twitched.

The clerk returned carrying a flat grey folder between both hands.

That ended the matter.

For the moment.

---

C-17/P was three pages.

The first was a source schedule.

The second was the preparation certificate itself.

The third was a correction sheet added two days later.

No conspiracy announced itself in red ink.

No one had written *we are now destroying the evidentiary value of this packet* across the top.

The source schedule was a list of ordinary records available to the reconstruction clerks four days after Silver Night.

Five returned emergency credentials.

Certified sponsor-seal exemplars.

Academy receiving-room entries.

A certified casualty roll dated the third morning after the incident.

Contingency's retained copy of the emergency substitution procedure.

The disputed-substitution memorandum was listed separately.

Its status read:

**Not incorporated — superseded procedural draft.**

Ilyan's finger stopped beside the line.

Thessa saw it too.

"That's one of the things Haleth said was missing."

"Yes."

Vaun leaned closer.

The three failed credential fragments were also listed.

**Not available to preparer — retained in protected continuity custody.**

That was technically true.

It was also the entire problem.

Mara had carried them away in the original continuity packet.

The reconstruction clerks had not possessed them.

Ilyan read the next line.

**Original provisional casualty list — excluded pursuant to casualty/credential separation directive. Certified casualty roll substituted.**

There was a directive number.

**AD-4.**

Thessa exhaled through her nose.

"Avarn Dethril."

"Probably," Ilyan said.

Vaun glanced at him.

He corrected himself.

"The initials are consistent with the name. The directive is not here."

"Better."

He disliked that he had begun hearing her corrections before she made them.

Sael turned the page.

The preparation certificate had been signed by a senior Academy casualty clerk named **Meral Tovin** and countersigned by Neral Voss.

Tovin certified that the reconstructed packet contained all materials made available to the preparation team and that excluded materials were identified on the source schedule.

That was not the language of somebody secretly slipping pages out of a folder.

It was worse in a quieter way.

The omissions had been written down.

The certificate did not pretend the failed fragments were present.

It did not pretend the disputed memorandum had been included.

It openly stated that the casualty material had been replaced under a separation directive.

"Haleth received this certificate?" Thessa asked.

Sael checked the attachment notation.

"No."

Ilyan looked up.

"What?"

"The messenger manifest names the substitute packet and authority slip. Not the internal preparation certificate."

"So the office documented the omissions for itself and sent Haleth a packet without the document that explained them."

Vaun said, "That is what these records presently indicate."

Thessa's face had gone hard.

"That is a remarkable way to preserve honesty."

Nobody answered.

The third page was the correction sheet.

It had been added after Haleth refused the substitute packet.

Meral Tovin's hand was different there—faster, the strokes compressed.

**Correction: independent examiner declined technical closure on grounds that packet did not preserve rejected comparison material or contemporaneous disputed-substitution record. Preparation team had no custody authority over rejected comparison material. Procedural memorandum excluded under AD-4. Matter returned to Contingency.**

Below it, somebody else had written:

**Do not reconstruct again without Secretary review.**

The initials were **N.V.**

Neral.

Ilyan sat back.

"He knew why Haleth refused."

"Afterward," Vaun said.

"Afterward."

That mattered.

Neral had sent an incomplete packet believing, perhaps, that it was sufficient for a narrow technical answer. Haleth had told him it was not. The correction sheet preserved the reason.

And then the matter had gone back to Dethril.

Sael tapped the directive number.

"AD-4 is not incorporated by reference. Your order does not reach it."

Thessa stared at him.

"You cannot be serious."

"I am almost always serious in this room."

"It explains why the original casualty record was removed."

"Which makes it relevant. It does not make it covered by an order that names incorporated attachments."

Thessa opened her mouth.

Ilyan touched two fingers lightly to her wrist.

She looked at him.

He did not need to explain.

They had spent enough time around Vaun now.

Do it properly.

Thessa's annoyance did not disappear. It merely acquired discipline.

"Fine," she said. "Then we get another order."

Vaun's gaze moved between them.

"Yes."

Sael looked almost disappointed that nobody had provided him with an argument.

---

The original casualty correction was easier.

That should have been suspicious.

Instead it was merely depressing.

The Academy memorial office had preserved every version of the Silver Night casualty roll because families had challenged names, spellings, household affiliations, and in three cases whether the person listed had been present at all.

Eiravel's memorial clerks did not erase earlier rolls when a correction was issued.

They tied them together.

The bundle was thick enough to require both hands.

Ilyan, Thessa and Vaun sat at a long table beneath a silver disc of the Sundered Moon while an elderly memorial clerk named Ysra Pell laid the first four versions in order.

"Do not put tea on them," she said.

"We have no tea," Ilyan said.

"Then you are already ahead of the last magistrate."

The first roll had been assembled before dawn after the attack.

Names were grouped by location and source: infirmary, dormitories, west tower, east court, receiving rooms, missing students, missing staff.

Mara Venn appeared under **west-tower receiving staff — status unconfirmed**.

The second roll, six hours later, still listed her as unconfirmed.

The third roll changed the entry.

**Mara Venn — presumed dead, body unrecovered.**

A correction mark sat beside it.

Not Corren's.

Not Neral's.

The clerk had copied the authority source into the margin.

**Contingency casualty liaison — per AD-4 separation.**

Thessa leaned forward.

"There it is again."

Ysra Pell adjusted her spectacles.

"There what is?"

Vaun answered, "A directive we need to read."

The fourth roll changed Mara back.

**Mara Venn — missing; death not established.**

The authority line beneath it was Corren Valeric's office.

Ilyan stared.

The date was the morning after Silver Night.

The same morning Savel remembered Corren ordering that the west-tower bearer chain remain open.

There was a note attached to the correction.

Not in Corren's hand.

A casualty clerk had summarized the instruction:

**Protected bearer designator remains unresolved. Do not infer death from non-return of credential or absence from receiving station.**

Thessa sat back slowly.

"So Savel remembered correctly."

"On this point," Vaun said.

Ilyan looked at the sequence again.

Unconfirmed.

Presumed dead.

Missing.

Three administrative truths in less than a day.

Mara herself had presumably been only one thing throughout.

Alive somewhere, if Teren's later authentication chain was right.

"Who changed her to presumed dead?" Ilyan asked.

Ysra Pell turned the third roll toward herself.

"The authority source is Contingency casualty liaison. The person entering the correction was Academy memorial staff."

"Can we identify the liaison?"

"Not from this copy."

Vaun pointed to AD-4.

"But the correction says the change was pursuant to the separation directive."

"Yes."

Thessa's jaw tightened.

"Dethril separated the casualty question from the witness-continuity question, and somebody used that separation to presume her dead."

Vaun looked at her.

"That is a reasonable working interpretation. We still need the directive."

Thessa gave her a look.

"I know."

Ilyan almost smiled.

Then he noticed another notation.

The third roll had been certified outward to three offices before Corren's correction arrived.

One copy to the Crown casualty desk.

One to the Academy household office.

One to the **Dmitrigical Tribunal Contingency Secretariat**.

The false presumption had therefore existed long enough to travel.

Not long.

But long enough.

"Could that be why the reconstructed packet used the certified roll?" Ilyan asked.

Vaun followed his finger.

"If the reconstruction team received this certified version before the correction propagated, yes."

"And if they received it after?"

"Then we need to know why they used the older one."

Ysra Pell cleared her throat.

"You may not need to speculate."

She lifted the next sheet from the bundle.

It was not another casualty roll.

It was a transmission receipt.

The memorial office had sent Corren's correction to every office that received the presumed-dead version.

The Crown desk acknowledged it before noon.

The Academy household office acknowledged it thirteen minutes later.

Contingency acknowledged it at second bell the following morning.

The recipient stamp was Neral Voss's office.

Ilyan felt the shape of the problem settle.

"The reconstruction happened after that."

"Yes," Vaun said.

The substitute packet was prepared four days after Silver Night.

Neral's office had possessed the correction for nearly three days.

Yet C-17/P listed a certified casualty roll in place of the original provisional list.

Which certified roll?

The source schedule gave a date.

Ilyan checked it.

The third roll.

The one that called Mara dead.

Thessa swore softly.

Vaun did not rebuke her.

"Now," Vaun said, "we have a narrower question."

Ilyan nodded.

Not why the whole conspiracy existed.

Not whether Corren had been secretly trying to save everyone.

Not whether Dethril had masterminded Silver Night.

A smaller question.

Why had a reconstruction team, working from an office that had already acknowledged the corrected casualty status, used the superseded roll that presumed Mara dead?

---

They obtained the AD-4 order shortly after midday.

Vaun's supplemental application took eleven minutes to write and nearly an hour to move through the appropriate desk because the appropriate clerk had gone to lunch.

Thessa found this offensive on constitutional grounds.

Ilyan bought them road pies from a stall outside the Hall while they waited.

He handed hers over without asking which filling she wanted.

Mushroom, sharp cheese and onion.

Her favorite when she expected to spend the afternoon indoors.

She looked at it, then at him.

"You remembered."

"You have eaten the same one after every long Academy examination since second year."

"That is disturbingly observant."

"I am training for an investigative order."

"You were doing it before that."

He took a bite of his own pie to avoid answering.

Thessa smiled.

Her shoulder settled against his while they ate on the Hall steps.

Neither of them mentioned the oak.

They did not need to.

The fact of it sat comfortably between them, new enough to be exciting and old enough in its foundations not to require immediate definition.

When Vaun returned with the amended order, she looked at their shoulders, the two half-eaten pies and Ilyan's expression.

"Finished?"

"Yes," Ilyan said too quickly.

"With lunch," Thessa added.

Vaun continued up the steps.

"I was asking about lunch."

Thessa waited until the judge was several paces ahead before whispering, "I like her."

"That is because she is cruel."

"That is not the only reason."

---

AD-4 was shorter than expected.

Avarn Dethril had issued it at first bell the morning after Silver Night.

Its purpose was administrative separation during mass-casualty reconciliation.

**Casualty determination shall proceed from physical confirmation, medical witness, and ordinary presence records. Credential-continuity disputes shall not delay publication of casualty rolls except where a protected bearer remains affirmatively known alive.**

The next paragraph created the problem.

**Where protected witness-continuity status cannot be disclosed to casualty staff, the casualty office shall proceed without access to such protected status.**

Thessa read it twice.

"So the casualty office was ordered to decide whether Mara was dead while being forbidden from knowing the information most relevant to whether she was alive."

Sael folded his arms.

"That is one consequence."

"It is an idiotic consequence."

"Those are not mutually exclusive statements."

Ilyan kept reading.

The directive had a stated reason.

Dethril feared that protected witness identities would spread through hundreds of temporary casualty workers if continuity files were opened to the memorial process.

The concern was not imaginary.

Silver Night had produced dozens of emergency clerks, family representatives, healers, runners, priests and Academy staff moving through the same rooms.

If Mara's protected role was supposed to keep her alive, putting it on the casualty desk would have defeated the point.

The directive was therefore not obviously absurd.

It was merely capable of producing an absurd result.

And it had.

At the bottom, in Dethril's own hand, was an added instruction:

**If protected continuity later contradicts casualty disposition, correct the roll prospectively. Do not circulate protected basis.**

Ilyan stared at it.

"He anticipated corrections."

"Yes," Vaun said.

"Then why did he want Mara treated as a casualty problem?"

"Because he appears to have believed the two systems should operate separately."

"Even when one could make the other wrong."

Sael said, "Institutions do that every day. Usually with less blood around them."

The line was not funny.

Nobody laughed.

AD-4 did not order Mara declared dead.

It did not order the old casualty roll used in the reconstructed packet.

It did not mention Haleth.

It did, however, create the wall that allowed a casualty office to be wrong without knowing why, and it instructed later corrections not to disclose the protected reason.

Someone had then used the wrong side of that wall after the correction had already reached Contingency.

That was no longer Dethril's directive acting automatically.

That was a choice made later.

"We need Meral Tovin," Ilyan said.

Sael checked the certificate.

"Dead. Six years."

"Of course."

"Her preparation log may survive."

Vaun shook her head.

"Later. First Dethril. He is living, and Savel places him above Neral when the substitute was ordered."

Thessa looked at the directive.

"Do we know where north is yet?"

Sael did.

Retirement had taken Avarn Dethril to **Larkfen**, a small upland town two days north by ordinary coach and considerably less by Crown post if a judge was willing to make several people unhappy.

Vaun was.

There was already a courier waiting when they left the restricted room.

Her request asked Dethril to appear voluntarily before Truth Beyond the Veil or accept examination under local oath in Larkfen.

Ilyan expected the answer the next day.

It arrived before supper.

Dethril's handwriting was broad and shaky with age but perfectly legible.

He would answer questions.

He would not travel south.

And he had one condition.

Vaun read the last line aloud.

"If the inquiry concerns the west-tower continuity failure, bring Teren Valeric. He knows why I refused to sign the second closure order."

Ilyan went very still.

Thessa's fingers found his beneath the edge of the table.

Vaun folded the letter once.

"Tomorrow," she said, "we ask your uncle whether he is willing to travel north."

Ilyan looked at Dethril's signature.

For once, the next question had supplied its own witness.

## The Second Closure

Teren agreed to go north before Ilyan finished asking.

That should have made the morning easier.

It did not.

They stood in Teren's breakfast room with rain whitening the windows and a pot of tea cooling between them. Judge Vaun had arrived early enough to offend the household cook. Ilyan had arrived earlier still, which offended Teren.

His uncle read Avarn Dethril's letter once, set it beside his plate, and said, "Yes."

Ilyan had prepared himself for argument.

"Yes?"

"He asked for me. I will go."

"You don't want to know why?"

Teren looked at him over the rim of his cup.

"I have spent eighteen years wanting to know why Avarn Dethril refused the second closure order. I can endure another two days without pretending otherwise."

Vaun sat opposite him with her gloves folded neatly beside her. "You remember the order?"

"I remember that there was one. I never saw it."

"Dethril says you know why he refused it."

"Then Dethril remembers something I do not."

Ilyan watched his uncle's face.

Teren noticed.

"You may keep doing that if it helps."

"Doing what?"

"Looking at me as though my eyebrows will confess before I do."

Ilyan looked down at his tea.

"They might."

For the first time since SV-12, Teren almost smiled.

It vanished quickly.

"When do we leave?"

"Within the hour," Vaun said. "Crown post has space as far as Larkfen junction. The local road is slower."

Teren nodded.

Ilyan said, "Thessa can't come."

It came out with more irritation than the fact deserved.

Teren's eyebrows, treacherous things, did something after all.

Vaun did not look up from the travel writ.

"Warden duty?"

"Western survey briefing. She has been gone from it too often already."

"Then she should attend it."

"I know."

Teren buttered a piece of toast.

"You sound disappointed."

Ilyan stared at him.

"Eat your breakfast."

"I am."

Vaun folded the writ.

"Excellent. We have established both the travel party and that Ilyan is twenty-three. Shall we proceed?"

Teren coughed into his napkin.

Ilyan decided that judges should not be permitted humor before noon.

---

Thessa met them at the north post yard anyway.

She was in Warden grey, hair tied back, gloves tucked into her belt. Two junior officers waited near the gate with the patient expressions of people who had already learned that their superior's private life might briefly delay departure but would not cancel work.

Ilyan stopped beside the coach.

"You're supposed to be at the survey office."

"I am on my way to the survey office."

"This is the post yard."

"It is between my house and the survey office."

"It is not."

"I took a scenic route."

Teren walked past them carrying his own case.

"I like her."

"Keep moving," Ilyan said.

Thessa waited until Teren was out of easy hearing.

"You have your notes?"

"Yes."

"Vaun?"

"Yes."

"Your uncle?"

"Unfortunately."

"Food?"

Ilyan lifted the wrapped parcel she had not yet noticed in his hand.

Her mouth curved.

"Good."

He had bought two road pies.

He handed her one.

"Mushroom?"

"Obviously."

She took it, then caught the front of his coat and kissed him.

Not the careful first kiss beneath the oak.

Not long enough to scandalize the post yard either, though one of the junior Wardens abruptly found the stable roof fascinating.

Thessa released him.

"Come back with all the parts you left with."

"It is Larkfen, not a battlefield."

"Old officials are worse. They have records."

"You are going to a survey briefing."

"Yes. I may die of boredom."

He wanted to kiss her again.

The realization arrived with enough force that he glanced toward Vaun.

The judge was checking the coach seal with a postmaster and demonstrating the mercy of pretending not to notice.

Ilyan leaned in and kissed Thessa once more.

This one was his idea.

She smiled against his mouth.

"Better."

"Go to work."

"You first."

They separated because they both actually had places to be.

Ilyan climbed into the coach feeling lighter for perhaps thirty seconds.

Then Teren said, "Very subtle."

Ilyan closed his eyes.

The coach rolled north.

---

The road climbed for most of the first day.

Bloomtide softened as they left the capital basin. Orchard walls gave way to wet pasture, then to dark stands of fir and alder where last winter's snow still survived in dirty seams beneath the shade. The post road was good enough for speed and bad enough to punish anyone attempting to read.

Vaun attempted it anyway.

Teren slept.

Ilyan watched the country pass.

By midday the coach had crossed three stone bridges and one temporary timber span built where spring water had taken half the old road into a ravine. Selvara's little lantern shrines stood at each crossing, their offering shelves crowded with ribbon, bent coins, dried flowers and the occasional heel of bread left by travelers who preferred divine redundancy.

At the second change station, Vaun bought coffee strong enough to qualify as an accusation.

Teren woke for it.

"Do you remember Dethril well?" Ilyan asked when the coach moved again.

Teren considered.

"Professionally. He was older than your father and younger than the people who thought that made him harmless."

"Did you trust him?"

"No."

Ilyan waited.

Teren sighed.

"That was not an indictment. I did not trust most Contingency officers. Their work depended on controlling who knew what. Mine depended on making records survive people who preferred otherwise. We were naturally irritating to each other."

"Did he ever lie to you?"

"Certainly."

Vaun looked up from her papers.

"About material matters?"

"I don't know. That is the difficulty with successful lying."

"Did you catch him?"

"Twice. Both times about witness locations I had no right to know."

Ilyan frowned.

"Then why call it lying?"

"Because he told me they had not been relocated. They had. He could have said the information was protected."

"Did that bother you?"

"Enormously."

"Even if it protected them?"

Teren looked out the window.

"Especially then. A necessary lie remains a lie. The necessity may justify it. It does not change its nature."

Ilyan thought of his own oath.

Truth that remained truth under necessity.

He wondered whether Teren heard the echo too.

His uncle did not say.

---

They reached Larkfen late the following afternoon.

The town occupied a long shelf above a reed-dark lake, its roofs steep against winter snow and its lanes paved in local slate. Water ran everywhere: beneath bridges no longer than a dining table, along stone channels beside houses, through mill races swollen by spring melt. The air smelled of wet wood, peat smoke and the first crushed green things of the year.

Avarn Dethril lived above the town in a narrow house with blue shutters and an orchard that had not yet decided whether spring could be trusted.

He was older than Ilyan expected.

Not merely elderly. Ancient.

His hair had gone almost completely white, and his left hand trembled badly enough that a younger man might have tried to hide it. Dethril did not. He opened the door himself, looked first at Vaun, then Ilyan, then Teren.

"You came."

Teren removed his hat.

"You asked."

Dethril stared at him for several seconds.

"You got old."

"You started first."

Dethril laughed once.

"Come inside before I remember why I disliked you."

The front room was warm and crowded with books. Not archive books. Used books. Gardening manuals lay beside old tribunal commentaries. A volume of devotional poems had been employed to level one leg of a side table. The room's largest window looked down toward Larkfen and the lake beyond it.

A woman perhaps in her seventies brought tea, introduced herself as Dethril's granddaughter, and left after informing him that if he became tired he was to stop regardless of judicial dignity.

Dethril watched the door close.

"My descendants have become tyrants."

"You trained administrators," Teren said. "This is what happens."

Vaun placed the local oath instrument on the table.

The old man's humor disappeared.

"Yes. Let's do this properly."

He swore.

Vaun began with AD-4.

Dethril did not defend it immediately.

That surprised Ilyan.

He listened while Vaun summarized the casualty sequence: unconfirmed, presumed dead, corrected to missing; the correction reaching Neral's office; the later reconstruction nevertheless using the superseded roll.

Dethril's face tightened at the last part.

"That was not the purpose of the directive."

"What was?" Vaun asked.

"We had protected witnesses scattered through a casualty operation staffed by half the city. People were walking out with names on scraps because families were desperate. Some were honest. Some were not. I had three living protected persons whose status would have identified where they had been placed if the casualty desks received the continuity files."

"Mara was one?"

Dethril looked at Teren.

Then at Ilyan.

"I did not know Mara's name then."

Ilyan frowned.

"You issued the directive that affected her."

"I issued a general separation directive. Protected bearer identities were compartmented below my office unless a conflict required escalation. W-5 was a designator to me until later."

Vaun said, "When did it require escalation?"

"When Teren objected."

Teren leaned forward.

"I objected to several things."

"Yes. You were exhausting."

"Be specific."

Dethril's eyes sharpened.

For a moment the old secretary appeared beneath the old man.

"You sent me a sealed exception notice after the first closure attempt. You wrote that the missing bearer had been confirmed alive through protected reassignment and that no casualty disposition could close the credential review while the bearer remained unavailable to the examiner."

Teren went still.

"I sent that to Contingency review."

"Which was me."

"I never knew it reached you personally."

"It did."

Dethril looked at Vaun.

"That is why I ordered the casualty roll corrected prospectively without exposing the protected basis. It is also why I refused the second closure."

Ilyan felt the pieces move.

"What was the second closure?"

Dethril stood carefully.

His granddaughter appeared in the doorway as if summoned by the movement alone.

"Cabinet," he told her. "Green folio."

She looked at Vaun.

"Ten more minutes before he rests."

Dethril muttered something impolite.

She ignored him and brought a narrow green folder from the next room.

Vaun did not touch it until Dethril explained its provenance.

"My retained retirement papers. Lawful personal copies of decisions bearing my signature or refusal. I had them reviewed when I left office. The second closure is there."

He opened the folder himself.

The paper inside had yellowed at the edges.

At the top was a title:

**WEST-TOWER EMERGENCY CONTINUITY — ADMINISTRATIVE CLOSURE REQUEST TWO.**

Ilyan read the date.

Five days after Silver Night.

One day after Haleth refused the reconstructed packet.

The request asked Dethril to certify three things.

That casualty reconciliation had sufficiently resolved the identity of west-tower staff.

That the outstanding emergency credential could be treated as lost in the incident.

That independent technical review had failed to establish an actionable defect and could therefore be closed without further witness examination.

Ilyan read the third line twice.

"Haleth didn't fail to establish a defect. He refused the packet."

"Correct," Dethril said.

"Teren's review was incomplete because he couldn't interview Mara."

"Correct."

"Then this was false."

Dethril shook his head.

"Not exactly."

Ilyan stared at him.

"How?"

"Because it was written to be true in the narrowest possible administrative sense. No *completed* independent technical review had established an actionable defect. Haleth refused closure. Teren's review was incomplete. Therefore there was no completed adverse finding."

Teren's mouth twisted.

"I remember why I disliked you now."

"I refused to sign it."

"I know. I am broadening the complaint."

Dethril ignored him.

"The sentence was designed to turn absence of a completed finding into evidence that nothing required further action. That is not the same proposition."

Vaun read the signature block.

"Who prepared it?"

"Neral's office."

"On whose instruction?"

Dethril's gaze moved to Ilyan.

"That is the part you came north for."

The room became very quiet.

Dethril turned the page.

Attached behind the request was a routing slip.

**Requested for Secretariat closure review by Deputy Neral Voss. Supporting necessity instruction received from Joint Emergency Coordination.**

No personal name.

No signature from Corren.

No convenient confession.

Ilyan felt almost relieved.

"Joint Emergency Coordination," Vaun said. "Who sat there?"

"Half the people who mattered and twice as many who thought they did," Dethril said. "Crown emergency staff. Academy command. Tribunal Contingency. casualty coordination. Joint Order liaison. House security representatives where their people were involved. It was temporary and badly documented because everyone believed it would last three days."

"Did Corren sit on it?"

"Yes."

Ilyan's stomach tightened.

"Did Neral?"

"As my deputy, yes."

"My father?"

"Yes."

"Teren?"

"Not as a member. He appeared when credential custody was discussed."

Teren nodded slowly.

"I remember the room. I did not know it had a name."

Dethril gave him a tired look.

"It barely did."

Vaun tapped the routing line.

"Who gave Neral the supporting necessity instruction?"

"I do not know."

"Did you ask?"

"Yes."

"And?"

Dethril reached for the next sheet.

His handwritten refusal occupied half a page.

**Declined. Bearer status remains unresolved for credential purposes. Independent examination has not failed; it has been prevented from completion by witness unavailability and deficient comparison material. Closure would convert administrative incompleteness into technical exoneration.**

Below it he had added:

**Return to Joint Emergency Coordination. Identify necessity authority if renewed.**

The request came back the following morning.

This time a new slip was attached.

Not an approval.

A direction.

**Do not seek casualty closure. Hold technical review incomplete. Seal materials pending security necessity determination.**

Ilyan knew the language.

It was almost exactly the disposition attached to Teren's report.

"So when you refused closure," he said, "they stopped trying to close it."

"Yes."

"They sealed it instead."

"Yes."

"Who ordered that?"

Dethril looked at the bottom of the slip.

"That is why I asked you to bring Teren."

Teren had gone pale.

"I have never seen this."

"Look at the routing notation."

Teren leaned closer.

There were three marks beneath the instruction.

One was Neral Voss's receipt.

One was Dethril's acknowledgment.

The third was a small custody mark: two intersecting lines beside a numeral.

Teren stared at it.

"That's mine."

Ilyan looked at him.

"You said you never saw this."

"I didn't."

"Your mark is on it."

"That is not my signature. It is my custody countermark."

Vaun said, "Difference?"

Teren sat back.

"A custody countermark means my office received a sealed direction affecting material in my control. It does not mean I read the direction. The outer runner sheet would carry the countermark before the sealed instruction went into the compartment file."

Dethril nodded.

"Exactly."

"How do you know?"

"Because I sent it."

Teren looked at him.

Dethril's voice had lost its edge.

"You were already objecting to Contingency controlling the witness. I did not trust my own office to give you the instruction unaltered. I sent the sealed disposition through tribunal custody. Your office marked receipt."

Teren's eyes remained on the old countermark.

"I never opened it."

"No. You could not. It was addressed to the presiding custody authority, not the examiner."

Ilyan felt anger rise and then stall for lack of a clean target.

The paper carried Teren's mark.

Teren had touched the chain.

Neither fact meant he had known the contents.

That distinction was becoming the shape of his life.

Vaun asked, "Who held presiding custody authority?"

Dethril answered immediately.

"The Joint Order liaison chair for emergency review."

"Name."

"There were two during that week."

Of course there were.

Dethril rubbed his trembling hand with the other.

"The chair changed at midnight after the second closure refusal. The first was Pel Ardis. He recused himself from technical review because of his earlier involvement. The successor was appointed under emergency authority."

Ilyan said, "Who?"

Dethril looked at him.

"I don't know."

Ilyan nearly stood.

Dethril raised a hand.

"I know that sounds convenient. It is not. The appointment was sealed because the chair also assumed protected witness-routing authority. I was told the office, not the name."

Vaun's pencil moved.

"Where would the appointment survive?"

"Crown emergency appointments register. Not Contingency. Not the Tribunal. The Crown secretary's emergency book."

Teren frowned.

"That should have been copied to Joint Order administration."

"It should have."

"Was it?"

"I never found a copy."

There it was.

Not an answer.

A place to look.

Vaun closed the green folio.

"One more question before you rest. Why did you refuse the second closure personally? You could have sent it back on technical grounds and let Neral resubmit."

Dethril looked toward the window.

The orchard beyond it was silver with rain.

"Because by then I knew somebody was trying very hard to make several different offices arrive at the same comfortable sentence without allowing any of them to see the whole problem."

"Which sentence?"

"That nothing actionable had survived Silver Night."

He looked at Ilyan.

"Your father did terrible things. I knew enough of him to know that even then. But on the morning after the attack, his office corrected Mara back to missing. Teren refused to close the credential review. Haleth refused the substitute packet. Ardis had preserved rejected fragments. None of those facts fit the sentence Neral's closure request wanted me to sign."

Ilyan said, "Did you think Neral was part of it?"

"I thought Neral wanted the emergency to become administratively finished. That is not the same thing."

"He used the wrong casualty roll."

"Yes."

"After his office had the correction."

"Yes."

"That sounds worse than wanting his desk clean."

"It may be worse."

Dethril met his eyes.

"Find out."

His granddaughter appeared again.

This time nobody argued when she ended the interview.

---

Teren did not speak until they were halfway down the hill.

Larkfen's roofs lay below them, dark with rain. Somewhere near the lake a mill wheel knocked steadily against swollen water.

Vaun walked several paces ahead, giving them privacy without pretending not to know they were talking.

"I remember the countermark," Teren said.

Ilyan kept walking.

"You just said you didn't remember the paper."

"I remember marking a sealed custody direction. I did hundreds. I did not know this was that one until I saw the date and routing."

"Do you remember who took over the chair?"

"No."

"Try."

Teren stopped.

Ilyan stopped too.

His uncle's face hardened.

"Do not do that."

"Do what?"

"Turn memory into a moral test. I can invent you a name if you need one badly enough. It will still be invented."

Ilyan's anger flared.

Then he heard Vaun in the sentence without Vaun having said anything.

Not enough.

He looked away.

"Fine."

"It isn't fine."

"No."

Teren came closer.

"I remember Ardis leaving the chair. I remember being furious because his recusal meant I lost the one person in the room who understood why the rejected fragments mattered. I remember a sealed appointment arriving. I remember being told the successor had protected-routing authority. I do not remember a face attached to that appointment. If I ever knew it, I cannot retrieve it honestly now."

Ilyan nodded once.

That was all he could ask.

They resumed walking.

At the inn, a post packet waited for Vaun.

One envelope carried the Hall of Veils seal.

Another carried Western Warden grey wax.

Vaun handed the second to Ilyan without comment.

Thessa's note was six lines.

The survey briefing had been tedious.

A retaining wall near the western silver road had failed.

She would be in mud for most of tomorrow.

Lyrion had sent flowers to the Warden office for reasons she refused to explain in writing.

She missed Ilyan.

And if he came back with another ancient bureaucrat attached to the investigation, he was responsible for feeding them.

Ilyan read the fifth line twice.

Teren, infuriatingly, noticed.

"Good news?"

"Mud."

"Ah."

Ilyan folded the note.

"Say anything else and I will leave you in Larkfen."

"Your affection is overwhelming."

Vaun opened the Hall envelope.

Her expression changed.

That ended the teasing.

"What?" Ilyan asked.

She handed him the single enclosed sheet.

Examiner Sael had anticipated the next question after receiving their first courier summary. He had checked the Hall's surviving cross-order administration index for emergency appointments touching Revision Seventeen.

There was no appointment copy.

There was, however, an accession reference.

**Crown Emergency Appointments Register — Bloomtide, 264 S.M. — Book II, leaf 73.**

Beside it, in the Hall index, someone eighteen years ago had written:

**Successor chair appointed after Ardis recusal. Identity restricted to Crown book.**

Ilyan looked up.

Vaun was already reaching for her travel case.

"We leave at first post."

Teren sighed.

"Back south?"

"Back south."

Ilyan slipped Thessa's note into the inner pocket of his coat.

For the first time in two days, the next question was not hiding behind a dead clerk, a missing witness, or a memory that might never return.

It had a book.

It had a leaf number.

And unless someone had been very unlucky with fire, water, war, mice or public administration, it had a name.
