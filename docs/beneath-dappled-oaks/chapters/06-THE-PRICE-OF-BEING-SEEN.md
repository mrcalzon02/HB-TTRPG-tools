# Chapter Six — The Price of Being Seen

Lady Maeryn Caladren-Voss owned a dining room designed to make debt feel attractive.

The windows were tall enough to flatter the river, the silver was old without being ostentatious, and the pale blue porcelain had been painted with tiny barges carrying grain, wool and wine toward increasingly prosperous horizons. Even the flowers appeared solvent. White climbing roses stood in a low bowl at the center of the table with no visible bruising, insect damage or evidence that weather had ever happened to them.

Damaris had eaten here before.

That was part of the problem.

She knew which chair Maeryn gave to people she intended to reassure, which chair she used for people she intended to observe, and which chair faced the western windows so that an afternoon guest spent the meal squinting into reflected river light while Maeryn remained comfortably in shade.

Rhyl had been given the reassuring chair.

Damaris had been given the observing one.

She looked at her aunt's cousin across the table.

Maeryn smiled.

Damaris smiled back.

Rhyl unfolded his napkin.

"Should I be worried?"

"Always," Damaris said.

Maeryn's smile widened.

"She was such a pleasant child."

"I was not."

"You were when asleep."

Rhyl made the mistake of laughing.

Damaris turned to him.

"You have chosen a side remarkably early."

"I was told there would be lunch."

"There is," Maeryn said. "I am not a barbarian."

The first course arrived: chilled pea soup with mint, soft white cheese and a scattering of edible blue flowers. Bloomtide food. Pretty enough for courtship season and practical enough for a banker who disliked wasting an afternoon on anything that required sawing through a roast.

Maeryn waited until the servants withdrew.

Then she looked at Rhyl.

"What do you intend to do with my cousin?"

Damaris closed her eyes.

Rhyl swallowed his soup.

"That was quick."

"Interest compounds."

"Maeryn."

"Damaris."

Rhyl put down his spoon.

Damaris opened her eyes again because she wanted to see whether he would try to become clever.

He did not.

"I intend to keep seeing her," he said.

Maeryn waited.

Rhyl waited too.

The silence became almost pleasant.

"That is all?" Maeryn asked.

"No."

"Good."

"It is all you get before she does."

Damaris looked at him.

His ears had gone slightly red.

That made it worse.

Or better.

She had not decided.

Maeryn leaned back.

"Excellent."

"You approve?" Damaris asked.

"Of the answer. I have not yet audited the man."

"He is sitting here."

"Convenient."

Rhyl looked between them.

"Is this how your family flirts?"

"No," Damaris said.

Maeryn said, "Sometimes."

Damaris stared at her.

Maeryn took another spoonful of soup.

Older relatives remained a social catastrophe.

The second course brought river trout with sorrel, young potatoes and a sharp green sauce. Maeryn allowed them three minutes of ordinary conversation before returning to the purpose of the invitation.

"You were seen at Seven Lamps."

Damaris looked down at her plate.

"Yes."

"Not merely together. Together deliberately."

Rhyl's hand rested beside his wineglass.

Damaris could have denied nothing and still made the answer evasive.

She did not.

"Yes."

Maeryn nodded once.

"Then people will price it."

Rhyl frowned.

"Price what?"

"Access. Probability. Future behavior. Whether Damaris can be induced to favor a road, contract, posting or household because it matters to you. Whether you can be induced to favor a river interest because it matters to her. Whether either family has begun negotiations. Whether one of you is being used to reach the other. Whether the attachment is durable enough to matter and fragile enough to exploit."

She said it without cruelty.

That was why Damaris listened.

Maeryn did not think love was false because other people could calculate around it. She thought calculation was what people did around anything valuable.

Rhyl considered this.

"And what have you priced it at?"

Maeryn's eyebrows rose.

Damaris felt a ridiculous surge of pride.

"Unseasoned," Maeryn said. "Publicly legible. Probably genuine. Too new for either of you to know its limits. More dangerous to Damaris than to you in the immediate term because Caladren business touches more committees than Red Hart routes do. Potentially more dangerous to you later because wilderness postings make private access and correspondence easier to manipulate."

Rhyl nodded slowly.

"Fair."

"Do not encourage her," Damaris said.

"I am trying to survive lunch."

"Poorly."

Maeryn ignored them.

"I did not invite you to tell you to stop."

Damaris had expected that.

Rhyl had not.

"You didn't?"

"If I intended to stop it, I would not begin by inviting you to a pleasant meal and explaining the threat model. I would speak to Damaris privately, then to the relevant family elders, then make your schedules inconvenient until one of you became tired."

Rhyl stared at her.

Maeryn cut a potato in half.

"You asked."

"I did not."

"Your face did."

Damaris bit the inside of her cheek.

Rhyl glanced at her.

"Do not laugh."

That ended any possibility of restraint.

She laughed.

He looked offended for approximately two seconds before his mouth betrayed him.

Maeryn let them recover.

"There is another reason I wanted you here together. Damaris, your use of Meris's River Ledger cipher has now produced enough audit noise that three people outside her immediate office have asked why a Caladren-Ilyra credential is touching old West Tower records."

Damaris's amusement vanished.

"Who?"

"A treasury compliance clerk, a transport-contract examiner and Jalen."

"Jalen asks about everything."

"Yes. That is why we pay him."

Rhyl said, "Do any of them know what she found?"

"Not from the audit trail alone. They know where she looked, approximately when, and under whose authority."

Damaris thought of the certified abstract, the council packet, the old transfer records, the accumulating chain of perfectly ordinary paperwork that had become dangerous only because someone had bothered to compare it.

"Can the searches be hidden?"

Maeryn looked at her.

Damaris felt embarrassed before the answer came.

"Yes," Maeryn said. "Which is why you will not do that."

Rhyl's mouth twitched.

Damaris pointed at him.

"Nothing from you."

"I said nothing."

"Your face did."

Maeryn looked pleased to have been quoted.

Damaris sighed.

"Why not?"

"Because the audit is also protection. If someone later claims you fabricated the request, exceeded the cipher, altered the abstract or obtained records through an unofficial channel, the trail shows otherwise. Privacy and deniability are not the same thing. You currently have very little of the first and a useful amount of the second."

That was unpleasantly sensible.

"So what do I do?"

"Use your own authority where you have it. Use Vaun's where the investigation requires it. Use Meris's only when Meris actually intends to own the request. Stop borrowing family standing merely because it is faster."

Damaris nodded.

It was not a reprimand.

It still landed like one.

Maeryn looked at Rhyl.

"And you."

"I was afraid of that."

"Do not become her private courier merely because you can move through places clerks cannot."

Rhyl's expression changed.

Not anger.

Attention.

"I haven't."

"Good. Keep not doing it. If the Red Hart begins carrying River Ledger evidence informally, both institutions will eventually discover the arrangement and each will assume the other knows more than it does."

Damaris thought of the west service gate.

Of Rhyl's routes.

Of how easy it would be to ask him to carry something just once.

Maeryn saw her thinking.

"Exactly."

Damaris hated competent relatives.

Dessert was honey custard with the first strawberries of Bloomtide.

Maeryn waited until Damaris had eaten half of hers before delivering the final injury.

"If this becomes serious, tell your mother before someone else does."

Damaris stopped with the spoon halfway to her mouth.

"It is not—"

Rhyl looked at her.

She looked at him.

Maeryn drank her tea.

Damaris lowered the spoon.

"I hate this family."

"No, you don't."

"Intermittently."

"That is healthier."

Rhyl reached under the table and found her hand.

He did it where Maeryn could see the movement.

Not performatively.

Not secretly either.

Damaris laced her fingers through his.

Maeryn noticed.

Of course she did.

She merely asked whether they wanted more tea.

That, somehow, was worse than commentary.

---

Outside, the river smelled of wet stone and spring weeds.

Damaris and Rhyl walked without a carriage because the rain had stopped and because Damaris needed the city around her rather than another upholstered compartment in which relatives could become strategic abstractions.

They crossed a narrow bridge crowded with flower sellers preparing for the evening market.

Rhyl bought her nothing.

She liked him for it.

"Well?" she asked.

"Your family is terrifying."

"Correct."

"I like her."

"Traitor."

He squeezed her hand.

They walked another twenty paces.

Then he said, "Was she right?"

Damaris knew which part he meant.

"About us being priced? Yes."

"No."

She looked at him.

He had stopped beside the bridge parapet.

People moved around them carrying baskets, ledgers, folded awnings and bundles of cut flowers.

"About telling your mother if this becomes serious."

Damaris looked at the river.

A barge was turning below them, its towline dark with water.

"Probably."

"And?"

"And I don't know what serious means yet."

"Neither do I."

That helped.

She turned back.

"I know I want you tomorrow."

His expression softened.

"Good."

"And the day after."

"Ambitious."

"Do not ruin it."

"Sorry."

She stepped closer until the front of his coat touched hers.

"I know I don't want my family deciding what this is before we do."

"Then they don't."

"You say that as if houses are weather."

"Weather is less organized."

"Debatable."

His thumb moved across her knuckles.

"Damaris."

"Mm?"

"I meant what I said at lunch."

Her stomach tightened pleasantly.

"Which part?"

"The part Maeryn didn't get."

She looked at his mouth.

That was becoming a problem of its own.

"Good," she said.

He kissed her beside the bridge while three flower sellers, one elderly ferryman and a child carrying onions were in perfectly adequate positions to notice.

The world did not end.

It merely acquired more witnesses.

When they separated, Damaris rested her forehead against his.

"That was politically irresponsible."

"I can do worse."

"I know."

"Tomorrow?"

She smiled.

"Ambitious."

---

Ilyan found Teren in the practice court behind the Valeric town house.

He had delayed until afternoon exactly as promised.

He had also spent the morning deciding what not to ask.

That proved more useful.

Teren was teaching a boy of perhaps fourteen how to stop overcommitting his shoulder during a downward cut. The boy was not family. Ilyan knew because Valeric children trained in the inner court and because Teren used a different voice with them.

"Again," Teren said.

The boy raised the wooden sword.

Teren corrected his elbow with two fingers.

"Your opponent is not under the floorboards. Stop trying to reach him there."

The boy tried again.

Better.

Teren saw Ilyan over the boy's shoulder.

Nothing changed in his face.

That hurt more than it should have.

"Five minutes," Teren told the boy. "Water. Then footwork."

The boy bowed and ran toward the side passage.

Teren set down the practice blade.

"You came."

"I said I would."

"You said tomorrow afternoon. I have known Valerics to interpret that as any time before winter."

Ilyan almost smiled.

Almost.

Teren noticed.

"What happened?"

Ilyan took the certified copy from inside his coat.

He did not hand it over.

"Do you remember renewing a restriction on Revision Seventeen after Silver Night?"

Teren went still.

There.

Not guilt.

Recognition.

Ilyan felt his pulse in his throat.

"Yes," Teren said.

The answer was too easy.

Ilyan had prepared for denial.

"Why?"

"Because I was instructed to."

"By whom?"

"The Joint Office for Order and Credential Integrity."

Ilyan looked at him.

Teren looked back.

"You remember the office."

"Of course I remember the office."

"It has been dissolved for years."

"That does not make me forget working with it."

Ilyan took a breath.

This was why he had delayed.

He wanted Teren to be evasive because anger was easier when it had a shape.

"What did they tell you?"

Teren's eyes dropped briefly to the paper in Ilyan's hand.

"That material connected to an active security review had been exposed by the West Tower disaster and that existing restrictions required renewal under surviving delegated authority."

"Did they say what material?"

"No."

"Did you ask?"

"Yes."

"And?"

"I was told I did not have compartment access."

Ilyan laughed once.

There was no humor in it.

"So you renewed it anyway."

"Yes."

"Why?"

Teren's expression tightened.

"Because that was my office."

"That is not an answer."

"It is the answer you dislike."

The boy returned carrying a cup of water.

Teren turned.

"Inside. Tell Master Edrin I am occupied."

The boy looked between them and disappeared without argument.

When the door closed, Teren said, "Ilyan, I was a delegated seal officer. I authenticated requests that arrived through recognized channels. I did not possess the authority to open every compartment whose restrictions I renewed."

"You could have refused."

"Yes."

That stopped him.

Teren folded his arms.

"I could have refused. The request would have gone to another seal officer. I had no evidence the request was unlawful. I had no evidence the underlying review was fraudulent. I had a dead academy tower, emergency orders across half the city and a Joint Office request carrying the proper countersigns. So I renewed it."

Ilyan hated that too.

Because it sounded possible.

Because it sounded ordinary.

Because terrible systems did not require every person inside them to be secretly delighted by the result.

"Did my father ask you to do it?"

"No."

"My uncle?"

"No."

"Did either of them discuss Revision Seventeen with you?"

"Not then."

Ilyan caught the last word.

"Later?"

Teren looked toward the empty doorway.

"Your uncle asked whether the restrictions remained intact."

The practice court seemed suddenly too bright.

"When?"

"Several weeks after the disaster."

"What did you tell him?"

"That the renewal had been executed."

"Did he say why he cared?"

"No."

"Did you ask?"

"Yes."

"And?"

"He told me it concerned protected review material and that curiosity was not a virtue merely because I possessed it."

Ilyan's hand tightened around the certified copy.

Teren watched him.

"You recognize that sentence."

"I recognize him."

Neither spoke for a moment.

A sparrow landed on the practice wall, reconsidered the family atmosphere and left.

Ilyan said, "Do you know Mera Holt?"

Teren frowned.

"No."

"Selwyn Arven?"

"The examiner? I knew the name. I do not remember meeting him."

"Tal's comparison die?"

Nothing.

Not even recognition.

"Whose?"

Ilyan watched him carefully.

Teren's irritation sharpened.

"Do not do that."

"Do what?"

"Look at me as though you have already decided what my face means."

Ilyan felt heat rise into his own.

"You renewed a restriction tied to the same office that removed evidence from an old counterfeit investigation."

Teren's anger vanished.

"What?"

That was different.

Ilyan had known Teren most of his life. He knew surprise could be performed. Teren had taught him how.

This did not prove anything.

It still felt real.

"Eighteen years ago," Ilyan said. "The Joint Office took custody of a returned comparison die from North Quay under a lawful warrant. The return field is blank. The same office later requested the restriction renewal you executed."

Teren sat down on the low stone bench.

For the first time since Ilyan arrived, he looked old.

Not ancient. Not weak.

Simply like a man discovering that an administrative act he had filed away as one bad week among many had acquired roots extending backward beyond his knowledge.

"I did not know that," he said.

Ilyan believed that Teren believed what he was saying.

That was not the same as knowing it was true.

Examiner Sael would have approved.

The thought irritated him enough to be useful.

"Do you still have anything from the Joint Office?"

Teren looked up.

"Records?"

"Letters. Seals. Routing slips. Anything."

"Official records went to successor custody when the office dissolved."

"Personal papers?"

Teren was quiet.

Ilyan waited.

"Possibly."

"Where?"

"Do not make that face."

"What face?"

"The one where you become twenty years older because someone said possibly."

Ilyan nearly told him not to joke.

Then he remembered Teren correcting his grip when he was eight, smuggling him sweet buns after funerals, teaching him that a wooden sword still hurt if one behaved stupidly with it.

Affection did not disappear because suspicion arrived.

That was the worst part.

"Where?" Ilyan asked again.

"My old service chest. Country house. If I kept personal routing notes, they would be there."

"Will you look?"

Teren's gaze stayed on him.

"Yes."

"With me?"

A longer pause.

"Yes."

Ilyan exhaled.

Teren leaned forward, forearms on his knees.

"Now you answer one."

Ilyan did not like that.

"Ask."

"How much does your father know about what you are doing?"

"I don't know."

"That is not the same as nothing."

"I know."

"And your uncle?"

"I don't know."

Teren swore quietly.

"What?"

"If the Joint Office request intersects something they were protecting, you may already have made yourself visible."

"I was visible when I refused the oath language."

"To the family. Not necessarily to everyone else."

Ilyan stared at him.

"Everyone else who?"

Teren shook his head.

"I don't know. That is the point."

For once, Ilyan believed him without effort.

Teren stood.

"Do not go to the country house alone."

"I wasn't planning to."

"Good."

"Do not tell my father."

Teren's face closed.

There it was.

Family.

Duty.

The old fracture.

"Ilyan—"

"Do you intend to tell him?"

Teren looked at him for a long time.

"No."

Ilyan's chest loosened.

Then Teren continued.

"Not today."

The relief disappeared.

"That is not what I asked."

"It is what I can promise."

Ilyan hated the answer.

He also understood it.

That was becoming an exhausting habit among the people he loved.

He tucked the certified copy back into his coat.

"Tomorrow morning. Your service chest."

Teren nodded.

"Bring someone you trust."

Ilyan thought of the Seven.

"I will."

As he turned toward the passage, Teren said his name.

Ilyan stopped.

"I did renew it," Teren said. "Do not make me innocent because you love me."

Ilyan looked back.

Teren's face was tired now.

"And do not make me guilty because it would be simpler."

Ilyan nodded once.

Then he left.

---

By evening the Seven had gathered in the back room of a tea house Lyrion liked because the proprietor understood that diplomats, lovers and investigators all paid extra for doors that shut properly.

Damaris arrived with Rhyl.

Nobody commented.

That was suspicious.

Mireth lasted twelve seconds.

"How was lunch?"

"Educational."

"Did Maeryn ask his intentions?"

Damaris stared at her.

Mireth brightened.

"She did."

Caelen covered his mouth with his cup.

Thessa said, "What did he say?"

"None of you are my friends."

"We survived school together," Lyrion said. "That is not the same thing."

Rhyl sat beside Damaris with the serenity of a man who had decided humiliation was weather.

"I said I intended to keep seeing her."

Mireth made a small delighted sound.

Damaris pointed at her.

"I will tell your mother everything."

"She already knows everything."

"Then I will invent something."

For a few minutes they were young enough to be ridiculous.

Then Ilyan put the certified renewal copy on the table.

The room changed.

Not completely.

Damaris's knee remained against Rhyl's. Caelen still had Mireth's ribbon tied beneath his Fellowship clasp. Thessa stole a sugared plum from Lyrion's saucer without asking.

Their lives did not stop being themselves because the investigation entered the room.

Ilyan told them what Teren had said.

He did not improve it.

He did not call Teren innocent.

He did not call him complicit.

When he reached the part about his uncle asking whether the restrictions remained intact several weeks after Silver Night, nobody interrupted.

Caelen finally said, "That matters."

"Yes."

"It still doesn't tell us why."

"I know."

Mireth asked, "Who is going with you tomorrow?"

Ilyan looked around the table.

Rhyl said, "Not Damaris."

Damaris turned on him.

"Excuse me?"

"You have a River Ledger appointment tomorrow morning. You told me on the bridge."

She had.

That was infuriating.

"I hate when you remember things."

"I can try less."

"Don't."

Thessa said, "I'll go."

Everyone looked at her.

She shrugged.

"Wardens spend half their lives opening old military chests and discovering someone thought mildew was an archival method. I know how not to destroy paper."

Lyrion touched two fingers to her wrist.

"And if Teren decides he dislikes the company?"

"Then I shall survive his disappointment."

The corner of Ilyan's mouth moved.

"Thank you."

Thessa nodded.

No speech.

No declaration of eternal friendship.

She had offered.

He had accepted.

That was enough.

Damaris looked around the table.

They were no longer students protected from the games of their elders. Maeryn had been right about that much: people would price them now. Their friendships, their work, their kisses, their routes through the city, their access to archives and households and old service chests.

But pricing a thing was not the same as owning it.

Rhyl's hand found hers beneath the table.

Across from them, Ilyan was asking Thessa what time she could leave.

Mireth was already arguing with Caelen about whether he could obtain successor-custody information without exceeding his Fellowship remit. Lyrion had gone quiet in the particular way that meant he was remembering who owed him a favor.

Tomorrow had begun arranging itself.

Damaris squeezed Rhyl's fingers.

This time, when he looked at her, she did not look away.
