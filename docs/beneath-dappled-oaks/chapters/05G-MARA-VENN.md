# Chapter Five G — Mara Venn

Talia was mending a cuff when Damaris found her.

Not because the household lacked seamstresses. Talia had simply reached the age at which watching someone else repair a thing she could repair herself felt inefficient.

She sat near the east-room window with Aereth's dark house coat across her lap and a little wooden box of thread beside her. Rain silvered the glass behind her. Aereth slept in the adjoining room, properly this time, after a night without another vanished hour.

Damaris stopped in the doorway.

Talia looked up.

Her needle paused.

"What?"

There was no useful gentle route into it.

"Do you know a Mara Venn?"

The needle slipped from Talia's fingers.

It landed on the coat without making a sound.

Damaris closed the door behind her.

Talia stared at the black wool in her lap.

"My mother."

Damaris had prepared herself for cousin, aunt, elder sister, no relation at all.

Not that.

She crossed the room and sat opposite her.

"Is she alive?"

"Yes."

"Where?"

"Southbank. Near the old ferry stairs."

Talia picked up the needle but did not resume sewing.

"Why are you asking me about my mother?"

Damaris took out the copied stores chit.

She did not hand it over immediately.

"Eighteen years ago she worked for River Ledger Credential Review."

"I know."

"She borrowed a damaged sponsor die from North Quay stores. Joren Tal's."

Talia's face changed.

Not recognition.

Calculation.

"The dead assessor?"

Damaris nodded.

"The same seal number that appears on Eren Vale's credential."

Talia looked toward Aereth's closed door.

"Does Judge Vaun know?"

"She knows the record. I asked her to give me enough time to speak to you first."

"Why?"

"Because you're here. Because you've been here through all of this. Because I didn't want a tribunal officer arriving to ask whether your mother stole a seal before you knew why her name had appeared."

Talia's mouth tightened.

"Did she?"

"I don't know."

That answer seemed to hurt less than a reassurance would have.

Talia set the coat aside.

"She hated that job."

"Why?"

"She said everyone wanted credentials issued quickly until something went wrong, and then suddenly every clerk was expected to remember which thumb touched which piece of brass six years earlier."

Damaris almost smiled.

"That sounds plausible."

"She left when I was small."

"You remember that?"

"I remember her coming home angry. I remember the blue coat. I remember being told not to touch the wax in her work case."

Damaris's attention sharpened.

"Work case?"

Talia noticed.

"Don't."

"I'm not doing anything."

"Your face is."

Damaris leaned back.

Fair.

Talia rubbed both hands over her skirt.

"She brought things home sometimes. Papers. Impression sheets. Never active seals, as far as I know. She worked late and I was a child."

"Did she keep anything from the job?"

"A box."

The room became very quiet.

"What kind of box?"

"Tin. Green paint. She keeps old employment papers in it. Letters, pay chits, certificates."

"Have you seen it recently?"

"Last winter."

"Where?"

"Her house."

Damaris breathed out slowly.

Talia's eyes narrowed.

"You're going there."

"Vaun will want to."

"I'm coming."

"Talia—"

"No."

It was not loud.

Damaris stopped.

Talia picked the coat up again, folded it once and set it carefully on the chair beside her.

"If you are going to my mother's house because something she did eighteen years ago may be tied to what happened here, I am coming."

Damaris considered telling her it might be easier if she did not.

It probably would be.

That was not the same as having the right to decide for her.

"All right."

Talia looked faintly surprised.

"That easily?"

"I am trying a new and difficult practice called not ordering competent adults around when I become frightened."

"How is it going?"

"Poorly."

Talia laughed despite herself.

The sound reached the adjoining room.

Aereth stirred but did not wake.

For a moment both women looked toward the door.

Then Talia said, "Give me ten minutes."

---

Mara Venn lived above a stationer's shop that sold paper by the sheet and ink in little square bottles.

The old ferry stairs were visible at the end of the lane, descending into brown river water beneath a line of dripping willows.

Vaun arrived separately. Rhyl came with her. Damaris had expected that and was still absurdly pleased when she saw him.

He kissed her cheek beneath the awning.

Talia pretended not to notice.

Vaun did not bother pretending.

"Mrs. Venn knows we're coming?"

Talia nodded.

"I told her a judge wanted to ask about River Ledger."

"How did she react?"

"She said she had retired specifically so nobody would ever say that sentence to her again."

Rhyl looked at Damaris.

"I like her already."

"You like difficult women."

"Evidence suggests that."

Talia looked between them.

"You two are exhausting."

Damaris smiled.

"Apparently that's contagious."

Mara opened the upstairs door before they knocked.

She was smaller than Talia, with the same dark eyes and none of her daughter's instinct for hiding irritation. Her hair was iron grey and cut bluntly at the jaw. She looked first at Talia, then at Vaun's tribunal clasp, then at Damaris.

"Caladren."

"Ilyra branch."

"Worse. Come in."

The sitting room was warm and smelled of tea, paper and beeswax. Books filled one wall. A half-finished puzzle occupied the dining table.

Mara pointed Vaun toward a chair.

"If this is about something I certified eighteen years ago, I reserve the right to complain about your filing practices."

"It is about something you borrowed."

That stopped her.

Vaun placed the copied chit on the table.

Mara read it standing.

Her eyes moved once across the employee number.

"Yes."

Talia stared at her.

Mara sat down.

"I remember this."

Vaun said, "Tell me."

Mara looked at her daughter first.

"We had a run of counterfeit bonded-runner credentials. Not good ones. Good enough. The impressions had damage marks that matched retired or spoiled dies."

Damaris felt Rhyl become still beside her.

"Someone was copying damaged seals?"

"We thought someone was using the damage as camouflage. An active sponsor die makes a clean impression. A copied die is never perfect. But if you copy one already chipped, scratched or cancelled, defects stop looking suspicious."

Vaun said, "Tal's die?"

"One of several I borrowed for comparison."

"Did you return it?"

Mara frowned.

"Yes."

"You are certain?"

"I signed the return."

"Do you have a copy?"

"Possibly."

Talia said, "The green box?"

Mara looked at her.

"You remember that?"

"You told me not to touch it approximately every third day of my childhood."

"You touched everything."

"I was six."

"You were industrious."

Mara stood and went into the bedroom.

Talia closed her eyes.

"This is going better than expected."

Rhyl murmured, "What were you expecting?"

"Fire."

Mara returned carrying a green-painted tin box.

She put it on the table and opened it.

Inside were employment certificates, old pay statements, two faded ribbons, a child's drawing Talia immediately tried to hide, and a packet tied with string.

Mara untied it.

The return chit was there.

**J. Tal sponsor die — returned North Quay Stores. Received: M. Holt.**

Mera Holt.

The dead supervisor who had held one of the scrap-cage keys.

Vaun copied the chit.

"Why did you keep this?"

Mara's expression hardened.

"Because six months later someone accused our office of losing a borrowed customs die. Not Tal's. Another one. It turned up in the wrong drawer after three days, but by then I'd learned that a clerk without paper is simply the nearest available culprit."

Damaris thought of Pellin. Of Talia. Of every servant and clerk who had become suspicious merely by being close enough to touch something.

"The counterfeit runner credentials," she said. "What happened to that investigation?"

Mara looked at her.

"Closed."

"Solved?"

"No. Closed."

Vaun's pencil stopped.

"By whom?"

"Credential Review sent it upward because the counterfeit impressions crossed offices. River Ledger, customs, two bridge authorities. We were told a joint review had assumed jurisdiction."

"Which joint review?"

Mara's gaze moved toward Talia again.

"I don't remember the full title. Something to do with order liaison."

Damaris and Rhyl looked at each other.

Vaun asked, "Dmitrigical Review?"

Mara's face went blank with effort.

"Maybe."

"Maybe is not yes."

"Then maybe. It was eighteen years ago."

Good.

No convenient recovered certainty.

Vaun asked, "Do you have the closure notice?"

Mara searched the packet.

She did not.

She had a note to herself instead.

**Case transferred. Do not pursue duplicate-damage set without authorization. Ask Sel about missing comparison sheet.**

"Who's Sel?" Rhyl asked.

Mara stared at the handwriting.

"Selwyn Ar. No. Arven. Selwyn Arven. He was senior examiner."

"Alive?"

"I don't know."

"What missing comparison sheet?"

"I don't remember."

Talia touched her mother's wrist.

"Don't guess."

Mara looked at her daughter.

Something softened.

"No. I don't remember."

Vaun closed her notebook.

"That's enough for today."

Mara looked surprised.

"It is?"

"Yes."

"You don't want the box?"

"I want copies of the relevant records. You may keep your daughter's drawing."

Talia made a wounded sound.

Mara smiled for the first time.

"Absolutely not. Tribunal evidence now."

"Mother."

Rhyl looked at the ceiling.

Damaris bit the inside of her cheek.

Even Vaun failed to remain entirely expressionless.

---

They left Mara's house with three copied records and one new name.

Talia stayed behind for tea.

Damaris was glad.

The rain had stopped. Water ran along the gutters toward the river, carrying petals, straw and the city's smaller failures downhill.

Rhyl walked close enough that their sleeves touched.

"You were right to tell her first," he said.

"Talia?"

"Yes."

Damaris glanced back at the upper window. Mother and daughter moved behind the glass, indistinct through the rain.

"I nearly didn't."

"But you did."

She took his hand.

Ahead of them, Vaun was already reading the copied return chit again.

Mera Holt had received Tal's damaged die back into North Quay custody.

That did not explain how the later scrap sale lost weight.

It did not explain Eren Vale.

It did not explain the Academy delivery.

But Mara Venn was no longer a blank space in an old personnel register. She had borrowed the die for a legitimate counterfeit investigation, documented its return, and been ordered away from a broader duplicate-damage case after it crossed several offices.

The shape was becoming larger without becoming clearer.

Damaris disliked that.

She disliked something else more.

"Ilyan."

Rhyl's thumb moved across her knuckles.

"Still at Reedglass."

"Good."

"He wants to speak to Teren."

"I know."

"Will he wait?"

Damaris looked at him.

"Until tonight, probably."

The Hall of Seven Lamps reception waited at the end of the day.

Teren Valeric might be there.

So might half the institutional world that had spent the last eighteen years filing papers over one another until nobody could tell which layer had been meant to hide something and which had merely been Tuesday.

Damaris tightened her grip on Rhyl's hand.

"Then we'd better get dressed."
