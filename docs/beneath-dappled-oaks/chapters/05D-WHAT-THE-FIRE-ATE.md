# Chapter Five D — What the Fire Ate

Sera Pell lived above a cooper's shop where Lower Reed narrowed toward the old lock stairs.

The morning smelled of wet oak, river mud, and yesterday's rain trapped in brick. Rhyl had been expecting an office. Judge Vaun had apparently expected breakfast.

She stopped at a street cart and bought two hot buns without interrupting her reading of the copied destruction receipt.

Rhyl watched her pay.

"Is that part of judicial procedure?"

"Yes."

"Which part?"

"Breakfast."

He accepted one of the buns.

It contained onion, sharp cheese, and enough pepper to make his eyes water pleasantly.

The cooper below Sera's rooms was already working. Each strike of his mallet traveled through the building with a dull wooden certainty. A child sat on the front step arranging barrel hoops into a pattern only she understood.

She looked up when Vaun and Rhyl approached.

"Mam said if you're from the school I have my slate."

Vaun crouched just enough to meet her eyes.

"We're not from the school."

The child considered this.

"Debt?"

"No."

"Temple?"

"No."

"Then she's upstairs."

Rhyl glanced at Vaun.

Vaun glanced at him.

Neither commented on the taxonomy.

Sera Pell opened the door before they knocked a second time.

She was perhaps fifty, broad through the shoulders, with dark hair braided hastily and a pencil tucked through it. She wore an office skirt under an old kitchen apron. One cuff had been buttoned and the other forgotten.

Her gaze went first to Vaun's tribunal pin, then to Rhyl's Red Hart clasp.

"Has something happened at the transfer office?"

"Not this morning," Vaun said. "May we come in?"

Sera looked behind them toward the street.

"Is this about my daughter?"

"No."

That answer changed her face more than any reassurance could have.

She stepped aside.

The rooms were small and meticulously used. A school satchel hung beside a freight clerk's coat. Three jars of preserves occupied the windowsill. Someone had repaired the back of a chair with packing cord and considerable confidence.

The child came in after them, carrying one barrel hoop.

"Nessa," Sera said.

"I'm quiet."

"You are visibly carrying part of Master Oren's stock."

Nessa looked at the hoop.

"He has lots."

"Downstairs."

The child sighed with the patience of someone burdened by unreasonable adults and departed.

Vaun waited until the door closed.

Then she put the copied receipt on the table.

Sera did not touch it.

She read the date first.

Then the name.

Her expression went still.

"Joren."

"You remember this?" Vaun asked.

"Of course I remember it."

No elaboration followed.

Vaun let the silence stand.

Sera pulled out a chair and sat.

"He'd been dead nearly a month. His widow kept finding office things in the house. Seals, old tariff cards, keys. Joren took work home even when he wasn't supposed to."

Rhyl leaned against the wall rather than taking the chair opposite her.

"The stamp was among them?"

"Two stamps. His ordinary assessment stamp and the sponsor stamp. The assessment stamp was cancelled and archived. Sponsor authority dies with the officer, so that one was destroyed."

"You saw it destroyed?"

Sera looked at him.

"I held the tongs."

Vaun's eyes lifted from the receipt.

"Tell me."

Sera rubbed her thumb against the edge of the table.

"Stores furnace. End of Forgeday. I remember because Nessa had fever and I wanted to go home. Pellis Arden was duty clerk. He checked the number. I checked the number. We struck the face with a cold chisel first. Four cuts. Then the stamp went into the furnace with three cancelled customs dies and a bridge inspector's plate."

"And you watched it melt?"

Sera frowned.

"No."

Rhyl felt the room narrow.

Vaun did not move.

"Why not?"

"Because that's not how stores disposal worked. We loaded the destruction tray, recorded the pieces, and gave the tray to the furnaceman. He signed the batch sheet after the melt."

"Who?"

Sera looked back at the receipt.

"I don't remember."

She said it without drama.

Then her face changed.

"Wait."

She stood and went to a narrow cupboard beside the stove. Bills, school papers, folded notices and household accounts came out in a disciplined stack.

"What are you looking for?" Rhyl asked.

"A complaint."

"About the furnace?"

"About my pay."

Vaun's mouth almost moved.

Sera found the paper.

"The office docked us half a shift that month because the stores furnace was out for repairs and we were sent home early twice."

Rhyl stopped chewing the last of his bun.

Vaun held out her hand.

Sera gave her the notice.

The date was two days after the destruction receipt.

"Out for repairs when?" Vaun asked.

"I don't know. The flue cracked sometime that week."

"Could it have been out on the day you put Tal's stamp on the destruction tray?"

Sera's face tightened.

"It could."

"Would that stop the receipt being signed?"

"It should have."

"Would it?"

Sera looked angry now, but not at Vaun.

"Apparently not."

Rhyl looked at the destruction receipt again.

A dead man's stamp had not climbed out of molten brass. It did not need to.

Someone only needed a tray nobody watched all the way to the furnace.

Vaun folded the pay notice beside the receipt.

"Pellis Arden. Where is he now?"

"North Quay customs. Unless he finally retired."

"And the furnaceman?"

"The batch sheet will have him."

"Where are the old sheets kept?"

"Municipal stores archive."

Sera stared at the papers between them.

"What was done with Joren's seal?"

Vaun regarded her for a moment.

"We don't know."

Sera looked relieved by the answer.

Rhyl understood why.

A lie would have meant Vaun had come to trap her. Certainty would have meant something terrible was already settled. Not knowing was frightening, but it left Sera outside the story rather than pinned inside it.

"Do I need a lawyer?" she asked.

"You may have one if you want one," Vaun said. "At present I need the destruction batch sheet and Pellis Arden."

Sera nodded.

Then she looked toward the door through which Nessa had vanished.

"Don't come to her school."

"I won't."

That was all.

---

At Reedglass, Damaris discovered that waiting was an occupation invented by cruel people.

She had three ledgers open, one letter half written, and no idea what any of them said.

Across the table, Meris was doing actual work.

This was offensive.

"You have read that freight summary four times," Meris said.

Damaris looked down.

"Have I?"

"You turned back to the first page after the third."

"Perhaps I admire the prose."

"Then your condition is worse than I feared."

Damaris threw a folded scrap of blotting paper at her.

Meris caught it without looking.

Aereth was asleep upstairs. Talia had bullied him into eating eggs, bread and stewed apples before allowing him to retreat. Mireth had gone to Green Covenant duty with the laboratory report copied under seal. The six preserved delivery records had arrived before breakfast and now sat locked in Vaun's evidence chest.

Damaris had been told not to open them without the judge.

She had obeyed.

She resented everyone for making obedience possible.

Meris finally closed her own ledger.

"You could go home."

"No."

"You could bathe."

Damaris looked at her sleeve.

There was still a faint mark where Rhyl's wet coat had brushed her yesterday.

"No."

Meris followed her gaze.

"Ah."

"I will have you thrown into the canal."

"Not in those shoes."

Damaris looked at her aunt.

Meris smiled with the serenity of a woman who had survived other people's young love before Damaris was born.

"How long?" she asked.

"How long what?"

"Since you decided he was no longer merely handsome."

Damaris opened her mouth.

Closed it.

The answer was embarrassingly difficult because there had never been a clean boundary. Rhyl had been Rhyl at the Academy: sun-browned when everyone else was pale from examinations, perpetually smelling faintly of cedar and cold air, easy to tease because he pretended not to notice teasing until the exact moment he chose to return it.

Then there had been his hand in hers after graduation.

His mouth.

The infuriating steadiness with which he let her make decisions he could easily have made for her.

"I don't know," she said.

Meris accepted that.

No lecture followed.

Damaris glanced at her.

"That's all?"

"You seem disappointed again."

"I am suspicious of restraint in this family."

"Sensibly."

The front door opened downstairs.

Damaris was out of her chair before she heard Rhyl's voice.

Meris looked down at her ledger and very carefully did not smile.

Damaris hated her.

Rhyl came into the accounting room with Vaun behind him and rainless morning air still clinging to his coat.

"Well?" Damaris asked.

He looked at Meris.

Then back at her.

"The stamp was put on a destruction tray. Sera saw it damaged first. She didn't see it melted."

Damaris's pulse quickened.

Vaun laid the pay notice beside the old receipt.

"The stores furnace was under repair that week. We are obtaining the destruction batch sheet and locating the duty clerk."

Meris rose.

"So the stamp may have survived."

"May have."

Damaris looked at Rhyl.

He was already looking at her.

She wanted to touch him.

Meris was standing three feet away.

That had stopped being a meaningful deterrent sometime yesterday.

Damaris crossed the space between them and put her hand flat against his chest.

Rhyl's eyebrows lifted.

"You came back."

"I said I would."

"Yes."

She kissed him.

It was not the first time and therefore should have been less catastrophic.

It was not.

His hand settled at her waist, warm and careful. The kiss lasted just long enough for Damaris to become aware of the exact number of people in the room and stop caring about any of them.

When she stepped back, Meris had become deeply interested in the destruction receipt.

Vaun had not looked up from her notes.

Damaris suspected both women of criminal levels of self-control.

Rhyl's thumb brushed once against her side before his hand fell away.

"Good morning," he said.

"It is now."

Meris made a small sound into her fist.

Damaris pointed at her without turning.

"Canal."

"Shoes," Meris reminded her.

---

The destruction batch sheet arrived shortly after midday.

Pellis Arden had signed as duty clerk.

The furnaceman's line was blank.

Not crossed through. Not marked absent. Blank.

Vaun laid the sheet beside the receipt.

"That should have failed audit," Damaris said.

Meris leaned over her shoulder.

"It did."

She pointed to a tiny notation in the lower corner.

**Corrected under Stores Variance 14-B.**

Damaris felt the hairs rise along her arms.

"Where is fourteen-B?"

Vaun's clerk was already moving.

The variance file came from the municipal archive an hour later.

It was thin.

Too thin.

Three destruction batches had been regularized after the furnace outage. Two had attached remelt certificates from the following week.

Joren Tal's batch did not.

Instead there was a single sentence in Pellis Arden's hand:

**Cancelled dies rendered unusable by prior mechanical defacement; remelt waived under stores discretion.**

Rhyl read it twice.

"Four chisel cuts counted as destroyed."

"For the record," Vaun said.

Damaris looked at the old receipt.

"Which means the stamp never had to reach the furnace."

Vaun nodded once.

That was as far as she went.

Pellis Arden had not retired.

He had been promoted.

North Quay Customs now listed him as deputy stores supervisor, with authority over bonded seals, confiscated dies, temporary credential blanks, and disposal certification.

Meris read the office description over Damaris's shoulder.

"Convenient."

"Maybe," Damaris said.

Her aunt looked at her.

Damaris met the look.

"I am learning."

"Painfully."

"From excellent teachers."

Vaun closed the file.

"We speak to Arden before we touch the six deliveries."

Rhyl nodded.

Damaris did not.

"Why?"

Vaun looked at her.

"Because if the seal was diverted through stores, the courier credential may be one use of a larger weakness. If we begin knocking on seven doors first, anyone who knows that weakness exists has time to close it."

Damaris thought of the Academy line.

Four days before Silver Night.

It sat in her mind like a hook.

She wanted to pull.

That was exactly why she did not.

"All right."

Vaun's attention lingered on her for half a second.

Approval from the judge was so small a thing that Damaris was irritated by how much she wanted it.

---

The Seven met that evening in the room above the tea shop where Lyrion had somehow convinced the proprietor that seven newly graduated nobles, order initiates and professional nuisances required privacy more than paying customers required the good upstairs table.

No one believed his explanation.

Everyone benefited from it.

Caelen arrived first with Mireth, carrying a garment box he had apparently been ordered not to crush. Thessa and Lyrion came together, arguing about whether a diplomatic reception could reasonably require three separate forms of footwear. Ilyan arrived last, pale from another day in the archive and carrying the certified oath extract under his coat.

Damaris watched them settle.

For a moment they looked almost like students again.

Then Caelen put his guard gloves beside the tea tray. Mireth had laboratory dust under one thumbnail. Thessa's Warden insignia was pinned correctly for once. Ilyan checked the door before sitting.

Not students.

Not anymore.

Rhyl's knee touched Damaris's beneath the table.

He left it there.

Lyrion noticed within seconds and had the decency to say nothing for almost a full minute.

"I am proud of you," he said at last.

Damaris kicked him under the table.

He looked wounded.

"Violence against diplomacy."

"An ancient tradition," Thessa said.

Caelen opened the garment box.

Inside lay a dark formal coat with silver-green embroidery at the collar.

"My cousin says if I arrive at Seven Lamps in field uniform my mother will interpret it as a declaration of war."

Mireth touched the embroidery.

"Your mother has excellent strategic instincts."

"You encouraged her."

"I like you alive."

The exchange required no explanation. Caelen smiled at her, and Mireth's fingers remained on the collar longer than cloth required.

Damaris looked away before they caught her watching.

Ilyan unfolded the certified extract.

"What happened at Reedglass?"

The room settled.

Damaris told them.

Not everything Vaun had sealed. Not Meris's private words. Not Aereth's fear in ways that would turn it into entertainment.

The buried powder had tested as mnemonic salt. Aereth's tincture had not shown an unexpected mnemonic compound. The runner credential used a dead freight assessor's sponsor number. The destruction record for the physical stamp had a hole large enough to walk through. Seven deliveries existed. One had gone to the Academy four days before Silver Night.

Ilyan went very still at that.

Mireth watched him.

"We don't know what was delivered."

"I know."

"We don't know it had anything to do with Silver Night."

"I know."

He sounded angry.

Not at her.

At the need to say it.

Damaris let him have the anger.

Thessa leaned forward.

"What do we do?"

"Nothing with the Academy delivery yet," Rhyl said. "Vaun is tracing the seal through stores."

"Nothing?" Caelen asked.

"We preserve what we have," Damaris said. "And we go to the reception tomorrow night."

Lyrion stared at her.

"You say that as though the second thing follows from the first."

"It does."

"I am delighted to hear the theory."

Damaris looked around the table.

"Seven Lamps will have half the people who can make records disappear and the other half who will notice if they do. We were already invited. If we cancel now, we announce that something frightened us."

Thessa's mouth curved.

"So we dress beautifully and pretend not to be hunting anyone."

"We are not hunting anyone," Ilyan said.

Thessa looked at him.

"Fine. We dress beautifully and pretend not to be terrified by administrative irregularities."

"Much better," Lyrion said.

Ilyan tried not to smile.

Failed.

The room eased.

Only a little.

Caelen poured tea.

"Do we tell our institutions?"

That was harder.

Mireth looked at the steam rising from her cup.

"The laboratory result is already tribunal record. My order will know the material was tested. They don't necessarily know why it matters to us."

Thessa said, "Avera knows we're looking at Silver Night generally. Not this."

Lyrion tapped one finger against his saucer.

"Silver Bough knows I am socially incapable of minding my own business. That is less useful than it sounds."

Rhyl looked at Damaris.

She knew what he was asking.

Meris.

"My family knows I queried the runner registry," she said. "My aunt knows there is a serious credential problem. She does not know the route details from me."

Ilyan nodded.

No accusation.

That mattered.

"And Valeric?" Caelen asked him.

Ilyan's face closed.

"Nothing."

The answer remained his.

No one pushed.

Outside, evening traffic rattled over the canal bridge. Someone below laughed too loudly. Cups clicked against saucers.

Ordinary sounds.

Damaris rested her hand under the table.

Rhyl found it without looking.

Tomorrow they would stand beneath the lamps in formal clothes while Houses assessed marriages, appointments, loyalties and weaknesses.

Somewhere before that, Judge Vaun intended to ask a promoted customs supervisor what had happened to a dead man's seal.

And six preserved delivery records waited in a locked chest.

The Seven had spent their last night at the Academy believing adulthood began at graduation.

Damaris was beginning to suspect adulthood was mostly discovering how many doors had already been open around you while you were still being told not to enter.
