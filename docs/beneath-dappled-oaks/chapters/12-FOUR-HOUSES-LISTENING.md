# Chapter Twelve — Four Houses Listening

Corren answered before breakfast.

Not in person.

That would have been easier.

A man standing in a room could be watched. He could hesitate, reach for water, look toward a door, become angry at the wrong noun. Paper had none of those courtesies.

Judge Vaun's clerk brought the response into the Hall while Ilyan was still eating toast over a petition docket. The envelope was cream, unadorned except for the narrow black impression of a private Valeric seal and the Hall receipt mark placed beside it.

Sael set it down without ceremony.

"Your father has waived objection to opening the explanatory statement."

Ilyan stopped chewing.

"That's all?"

"No."

Of course not.

Sael passed him a second sheet.

Corren's answer was six lines long.

He acknowledged the notice. He did not contest the authenticity of his old emergency seal. He did not contest that he had accepted temporary custody of the black selector. He waived the archival waiting period. He requested that any protected-person identity, destination or route geometry remain sealed under the original order. He offered no explanation for his conduct.

At the bottom, in the same hand Ilyan remembered from birthday notes, school congratulations and one particularly stern letter about borrowing a horse without permission, Corren had written:

**The record should be opened.**

Ilyan read it twice.

Sael waited.

"He could have objected," Ilyan said.

"Yes."

"He could have delayed it."

"Probably."

"He could have appeared."

"He still may. Waiver isn't silence forever."

Ilyan folded the paper once along the existing crease.

"I hate when he does something reasonable."

Sael's mouth twitched.

"That sounds inconvenient."

"It is."

The clerk returned ten minutes later with Judge Vaun's order to open the retained explanatory portion.

The Crown archive answered before noon.

---

The explanatory statement was disappointingly short.

Ilyan had begun to understand that history's most consequential paperwork often looked as though someone had filled it out while thinking about lunch.

The certified extract occupied less than half a page.

**Temporary continuity custody accepted pending establishment of successor receiving authority. Selector use restricted to preservation or transfer of protected witness material already admitted to emergency continuity. No new witness admission authorized. No destination disclosure authorized.**

Then Corren's old signature.

Then a later entry, two days afterward:

**Protected route confirmed independently viable. Selector not exercised. Returned to Crown Emergency Secretariat.**

Ilyan stared at the last three words.

Selector not exercised.

Damaris, seated across the table, said nothing.

That was how he knew she was giving him time to become irritated before she corrected him.

"Say it," he said.

"It is a statement by the custodian."

"I know."

"Not an independent proof of non-use."

"I know."

"But it is evidence."

"Damaris."

She raised both hands.

Rhyl, beside her, was studying the certified extract as though it might develop tracks if he stared long enough.

"What would use leave behind?" he asked.

Vaun looked to the Crown archivist.

"Depends on the receiving mechanism," the archivist said. "The selector did not itself contain a register. It enabled the Joint side of a paired opening. The receiving side should have recorded an exercise if the mechanism was actually completed."

"Should have," Rhyl repeated.

The archivist sighed.

"Yes."

Ilyan looked at the statement again.

Corren had accepted the selector lawfully. He had been instructed not to admit anyone new. He claimed not to have used it. Two days later he returned it after the protected route was independently viable.

None of that made Silver Night smaller.

None of it changed the fact that his father had helped engineer the disaster.

It did, however, remove another satisfying version of the story.

Ilyan had imagined the black selector as a secret thing Corren had taken into his hand for some concealed purpose.

Instead the old system had handed it to him, documented the handoff and received it back.

Teren watched Ilyan from the end of the table.

"You look disappointed."

"I am."

"Good."

Ilyan frowned.

Teren leaned back in his chair.

"Disappointment is what happens when evidence refuses to flatter you. Better than the alternative."

"You sound like Vaun."

"I am very old. Eventually everyone sounds like a judge."

Vaun ignored this.

"We verify the receiving side."

Sael was already writing the request.

---

By midday, four Houses knew something was happening.

Not the same thing.

That was the important part.

House Caladren knew the River Ledger had received a lawful historical inquiry touching emergency custody records, because Damaris had used an ordinary professional channel to locate a paired-account reference. Her aunt Meris knew Damaris had been spending unusual time with Hall examiners because Meris managed enough of the family's commercial obligations to notice when one of her younger relatives repeatedly moved meetings.

House Eris knew a Western Warden officer had been assigned bounded historical retrieval work for Judge Vaun. Thessa's captain had approved the assignment. That approval had entered a duty roster. Duty rosters existed specifically so commanders knew where their officers were, which made secrecy an unreasonable thing to demand from them.

The Crown knew Caelen had twice requested old emergency-office access rules for Ilyan. Caelen had not hidden the requests because hiding Crown records requests was an excellent way to make them interesting.

And by the time Ilyan left the Hall for lunch, House Sylvaren knew somebody was asking questions about an old protected route.

He learned this because Lyrion was waiting outside with a parasol.

The rain had stopped.

"Why do you have that?" Ilyan asked.

"It completes me."

"It is not raining."

"That is why I can carry it without inconvenience."

Lyrion fell into step beside him.

Bloomtide had turned the streets bright despite the wet stone. Flower sellers crowded the corner near the Hall. Two Academy students in pale spring coats were arguing over sugared violets with the seriousness of treaty negotiators. A carriage rolled past slowly enough for its occupants to inspect Ilyan without appearing to do so.

Lyrion waited until they were beyond the Hall gates.

"Someone from the Silver Bough archives asked whether I knew why Crown protected-routing references from the Silver Night period were being cross-checked against old diplomatic courier exemptions."

Ilyan stopped.

"Are they?"

"Apparently."

"By us?"

"Not that I know of."

That was worse.

Lyrion twirled the parasol once.

"I told them I had no idea. This was true and therefore deeply unsatisfying to everyone."

"Who asked?"

"A deputy archivist who probably asked because her superior asked because someone else noticed a reference moving through a catalog. No hooded cabal required."

Ilyan resumed walking.

"Four Houses."

"At least."

"How?"

Lyrion gave him a look.

"Ilyan, you and your friends have spent days asking old institutions for records concerning the most politically sensitive disaster of our generation. Did you expect the institutions not to contain people with cousins?"

"I was hoping for professional restraint."

"You have professional restraint. What you do not have is professional invisibility."

They bought lunch from a street stall because Lyrion refused to attend another formal meal that day. The road pies were filled with lamb, leek and pepper. They ate beneath the awning while carts hissed through puddles.

"What does Sylvaren think we're doing?" Ilyan asked.

"Nothing coherent yet. One version says you are reopening the casualty roll. One says Teren is challenging an old Crown appointment. One says the Western Wardens have discovered an unlawful witness transfer."

"None of those are true."

"Exactly."

"What does your family think?"

Lyrion wiped grease from his thumb.

"My family thinks incomplete information is a social invitation."

"That's appalling."

"We are diplomats."

Ilyan laughed despite himself.

Lyrion's expression softened.

"You need to tell the others. Not everything. Just that people are listening now."

Ilyan looked back toward the Hall.

"We tell each other everything."

"We used to."

The words were gentle.

That made them harder.

---

Thessa came to Ilyan's rooms after sunset carrying mud on her boots and an invitation in her pocket.

He had been attempting to cook.

The attempt had produced onions, mushrooms, three pans and a smell suggesting one of the pans had developed political objections.

Thessa stood in the doorway and looked toward the kitchen.

"Should I send for help?"

"No."

"A priest?"

"Cruel."

She kissed him before taking off her coat.

It was not the quick kiss of the Hall corridor or the teasing brush of her mouth against his temple. She caught his jaw in one hand and kissed him until he forgot what he had been about to defend.

When she drew back, she sniffed.

"Something is burning."

"I hate you."

"No, you don't."

He turned toward the kitchen.

She caught his belt and pulled him back just far enough to kiss the side of his neck.

"Thessa."

"Pan."

"You are actively preventing me reaching it."

"I know."

He managed to save dinner anyway.

They ate on the floor beside the low table because Ilyan had covered both chairs with records. Thessa tucked one bare foot beneath his thigh for warmth and stole the best mushrooms from his plate without asking.

Halfway through the meal she handed him the invitation.

It was from Lady Maevra Eris.

Not a command. Not formally.

Lady Maevra was hosting a small supper three nights hence for officers, magistrates and younger members of several Houses concerned with western transport security. Thessa was invited in her own right as a Warden officer recently engaged in Crown-Hall liaison work.

A second line invited her to bring a guest.

Ilyan read it again.

"That's me."

"Probably."

"She knows."

"She knows I'm seeing you. Half my family knows. The other half has eyes."

"No. She knows about the work."

Thessa leaned back on one hand.

"Yes."

"How much?"

"My captain's request is ordinary record. My assignment is ordinary record. Your name isn't on it."

"But the guest line—"

"Could be because my aunt knows I am sleeping with you."

Ilyan nearly inhaled a mushroom.

Thessa watched him cough.

"There," she said. "That face."

"You cannot say that while I'm eating."

"Why? It is true."

"That isn't the point."

"The point is that older relatives are not idiots."

She took his cup, drank from it, and handed it back.

"Or she knows enough to think inviting you is useful. Or both."

Ilyan looked at the card.

"Do you want to go?"

Thessa considered the question instead of giving him the answer she thought he wanted.

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because Maevra is one of the people who decides whether young officers get interesting work or spend five years counting bridge nails. Because western transport security actually matters to me. Because I want to know what she thinks I have been doing. And because if my family has decided you are useful, I would rather watch them discover you are also difficult."

"Romantic."

"Extremely."

He set the invitation aside.

"Then we go."

Thessa studied him.

"As what?"

"Your guest."

"Not as an examiner?"

"No."

"Not as Teren's nephew?"

"I can't stop being that."

"You know what I mean."

He did.

At the Hall he had asked her to attend any confrontation with Corren as Thessa, not as a Warden. Now the distinction had turned around. Her family was inviting him into a room partly because of her professional visibility and partly because they were lovers.

Love was not separate from politics.

Neither, apparently, was dinner.

"Your guest," he said again.

Thessa smiled.

"Good."

Then she climbed into his lap with complete disregard for the plates.

The invitation slid onto the rug.

For several minutes Ilyan found it very easy not to care who was listening.

---

The Seven met the next evening in Mireth's borrowed Green Covenant workroom.

It was not secret. They had stopped pretending that seven recognizable graduates entering the same building could become invisible by choosing a side door.

Mireth brought tea. Caelen brought a Crown memorandum he was not permitted to show anyone and therefore left folded inside his coat. Damaris brought numbers she could discuss but not the underlying client records. Rhyl brought bread. Lyrion brought gossip. Thessa brought the Eris invitation. Ilyan brought Corren's waiver and the certified explanatory extract.

They had once been students who knew nearly everything important about one another because almost everything important happened in the same classrooms, corridors and dormitories.

Adult life had begun to produce locked doors.

Ilyan explained Corren's response first.

Mireth read the waiver.

"He wants the record opened."

"Apparently."

"Do you think that's good?"

"I think it's information."

Caelen nodded once.

That answer would have pleased Vaun.

Damaris explained what the River Ledger could lawfully verify about paired receiving records and what she could not disclose from unrelated accounts. Thessa explained that her Warden assignment could cover historical movement records requested by Vaun but did not give her permission to browse operational Warden material for parallels. Caelen said there were Crown custody procedures he could describe generally and specific emergency records he could not bring them.

Lyrion listened to all of it.

Then he said, "We have a problem."

Rhyl tore the bread in half.

"Only one?"

"A new one."

Lyrion looked around the table.

"We keep speaking as if friendship gives us a common clearance."

Nobody answered immediately.

The rain ticked against the high windows.

Mireth wrapped both hands around her tea.

"It used to."

"At school," Damaris said.

"At school we didn't have clearances," Caelen replied.

That earned a small laugh, but not enough to remove the problem.

Ilyan looked at the six people who had survived with him.

He wanted to tell them everything.

The wanting was almost physical.

It came from years of shared rooms, shared meals, examinations, grief, stupid jokes, borrowed clothes, the night the west tower burned, the days afterward when none of them had known what to do except remain near one another.

But Mara's protected route was not his to give away.

Teren's compartmented medical history was not social currency.

Damaris had Ledger clients. Thessa had Warden duties. Caelen served the Crown. Mireth worked with living-memory testimony. Lyrion's profession depended on knowing things he could not repeat. Rhyl had field sources who trusted him precisely because their names did not travel.

"We need rules," Ilyan said.

Rhyl grimaced.

"That sounds terrible."

"Yes."

"Fine."

They did not solve it that night.

They did something more useful.

They admitted it existed.

Damaris proposed the first boundary: nobody would ask another member of the Seven to violate an oath, client confidence, protected source or lawful custody restriction for the sake of the group.

Caelen added that saying **I can't tell you** would not itself be treated as evidence of betrayal.

Mireth wanted a distinction between withholding protected information and deliberately misleading one another.

Lyrion wanted them to stop using family gossip as though it were verified intelligence.

Thessa wanted everyone to understand that lovers did not inherit each other's institutional authority.

Rhyl, after a long silence, said sources who had not chosen the Seven had not consented to become the Seven's sources.

That quieted the room.

Ilyan wrote the principles down on the back of an old Covenant seed inventory.

Not a pact.

Not yet.

A beginning.

When they finally rose to leave, Caelen touched Ilyan's shoulder.

"You all right?"

Ilyan looked at the folded waiver in his hand.

"No."

Caelen waited.

"But I know what I don't know."

"That's something."

"Apparently everyone keeps telling me that."

Outside, the rain had stopped again.

The city smelled of wet stone, horse sweat and flowering trees.

Thessa slipped her hand into Ilyan's as they walked.

Across the street, a carriage bearing Eris colors rolled slowly past and did not stop.

Farther down, a Caladren factor stood beneath an awning speaking to a Crown clerk. Neither looked toward them.

At the corner, Lyrion paused to greet a Silver Bough secretary he claimed not to know well enough to dislike.

Four Houses listening.

Probably more.

Not because somebody had gathered them into a conspiracy.

Because the Seven had become adults with offices, lovers, relatives, duties and records of their own.

Their friendships now cast shadows into institutions.

Ilyan squeezed Thessa's hand.

"Your aunt's supper is going to be awful."

"Almost certainly."

"Will there be good food?"

"Yes."

"Then I remain persuadable."

Thessa leaned into him as they walked.

"I knew there was a reason I kept you."

He kissed her hair.

Behind them, the Hall bells marked the hour.

Ahead, somewhere beyond a dozen ordinary desks and ordinary people doing their jobs, the request for the selector's paired receiving record was already moving.

And now enough people had noticed the movement that whatever came back would not belong to a quiet investigation anymore.
