# Chapter Six A — The Service Chest

The Valeric country house was not in the country so much as at the point where the city became embarrassed by its own density and began pretending orchards counted as wilderness.

Thessa approved of it immediately.

There were stone walls, a practical stable, a rain cistern large enough to matter and no ornamental pond placed where a horse could fall into it. Whoever had laid out the property had expected people to live there rather than merely arrive beautifully.

Ilyan noticed her inspection as they came through the gate.

"You are judging the drains."

"They are good drains."

"You sound surprised."

"Your family has curtains worth more than a cavalry remount. I had concerns about priorities."

Teren, walking ahead of them, looked back.

"Those curtains belonged to my grandmother."

"Were they waterproof?"

"No."

"Then I stand by my assessment."

Teren gave Ilyan a look.

Ilyan shrugged.

"I brought someone I trust."

"So I see."

The house had been opened for the season but not inhabited properly. White covers still lay over several pieces of furniture in the entrance hall. A maid had lit the kitchen range and one downstairs fire before their arrival, then retreated to some safer part of the estate after being told that three adults intended to spend the morning opening old government papers.

The service chest lived upstairs in what had once been Teren's room.

Ilyan had slept there as a child.

He remembered being seven and furious after losing a practice bout to a cousin four years older than he was. Teren had made him sit on the edge of the bed and explain, in painful detail, why being smaller had not forced him to attack from the same angle six times.

He remembered sweet buns hidden in the desk.

He remembered rain against the western windows.

He remembered believing Teren knew the answer to everything worth asking.

The room was smaller now.

Or he was.

Teren crossed to a wardrobe, knelt and pulled out a long iron-bound chest with both hands.

Thessa crouched beside it.

"Mildew?"

"Probably."

"Mice?"

"Possibly."

"Explosive seal?"

Teren looked offended.

"I was a civil officer."

"That was not an answer."

Ilyan laughed before he could stop himself.

Teren glanced up.

For a moment the morning became something else: an uncle on the floor, a friend beside him, a house full of old summers.

Then Teren took a key from his pocket.

"No explosive seal."

"Good."

"One privacy thread. Broken when the chest opens."

Thessa held out a hand.

"Wait."

Teren stopped.

She examined the lid without touching the thread.

"Original?"

"Yes."

"Has anyone else had the key?"

"Not knowingly."

"Has the chest been moved?"

"Three times. From my city rooms to the Joint Office quarters, from there to the Valeric town house after dissolution, then here."

"Anyone inventory it?"

"The official material was inventoried before I retained the chest. Personal papers were not."

Thessa nodded.

"Open it."

Teren did.

The thread snapped with a tiny dry sound.

Nobody spoke.

There were no secrets visible from the top.

There were shirts.

Ilyan stared.

Thessa stared.

Teren said, "I did warn you this was personal storage."

"You kept government routing notes under shirts?" Ilyan asked.

"Nobody steals old shirts."

Thessa lifted one between two fingers.

"Someone should have."

Teren took it from her.

"I liked that shirt."

"You were wrong."

Beneath the clothes lay two pairs of boots, a leather sword belt gone stiff with age, a cracked shaving case, three bundles of letters tied by sender, a small wooden box, and six thin notebooks.

Teren sat back on his heels.

"Those."

Ilyan reached for the notebooks.

Thessa caught his wrist.

"Table first. Dry hands. One at a time."

He looked at her.

"You volunteered for this because you enjoy giving orders."

"That was among the reasons."

They moved to the writing table beneath the window.

Teren opened the shutters. Bloomtide light spilled across dust, faded wood and the pale edges of paper that had not been handled in years.

The notebooks were not diaries.

Ilyan was grateful.

Reading Teren's private thoughts would have felt different from reading the professional residue Teren had explicitly offered. Instead each volume contained dates, docket numbers, initials, brief reminders, expenses, names of people Teren intended to chase for missing countersigns and occasional insults directed at forms.

Thessa found the system first.

"Left margin is incoming. Right is what you did with it."

Teren nodded.

"Usually."

"Usually?"

"I was younger."

"You were employed."

"The two conditions are not mutually corrective."

Ilyan turned pages.

The years moved beneath his fingers.

Ordinary work filled almost everything.

Permit renewals. Witness-screen maintenance. Restricted testimony transfers. A dispute about whether a sealed botanical sample could legally be transported in the same carriage as a magistrate's lunch. Three pages of irritation about temporary credentials issued during a flood.

The Joint Office had not spent every day conspiring against the Realm.

It had spent most days being an office.

That mattered.

It also made the dangerous parts harder to see.

Teren leaned over his shoulder.

"There."

Ilyan stopped.

The date was six days after Silver Night.

The entry was brief.

**JOCI / protected review / R17 restriction renewal. Delegated seal. Countersigns verified. No compartment access.**

In the right margin Teren had written:

**Executed. Copy to successor packet if office closes before review terminates.**

Ilyan read it twice.

"Successor packet."

Teren nodded slowly.

"I had forgotten that."

"What was it?"

"A dissolution precaution. Joint offices were temporary by design. Anything still active when one closed had to be assigned somewhere permanent."

Thessa looked up from the next notebook.

"Where?"

"Depends on the material."

"Revision Seventeen?"

Teren rubbed his jaw.

"Truth-order material should have gone to Hall custody. Security review material could have gone to Crown Integrity, Hall of Veils, or a tribunal annex depending on who owned the originating authority."

Ilyan felt impatience rise.

"So the successor packet might tell us who inherited it."

"If it survives."

"Where would it be?"

"Not here."

Thessa said, "Then find the dissolution year."

Teren looked at her.

She tapped the notebooks.

"If you kept notes when the office closed, you may have written where the boxes went."

They moved forward three volumes.

The work became slower.

Teren had been reassigned during the dissolution and his notes grew crowded with arrows, crossed-out room numbers and abbreviated destinations that meant nothing to Ilyan.

Thessa made him explain each one.

She did not let him turn memory into certainty.

"You think HRA means Hall Restricted Annex?"

"Yes."

"Think or know?"

Teren frowned.

"Think."

"Then say think."

Ilyan looked at her.

Thessa did not notice.

Or pretended not to.

Examiner Sael would have liked her.

That was unfortunate.

Near midday they found a folded sheet tucked into the back cover of the final notebook.

It was not a secret memorandum.

It was a packing list.

Teren laughed once when he saw it.

"Of course."

"What?" Ilyan asked.

"We spent three weeks preparing the formal transfer register. I kept the sheet telling the porters which rooms received which crates."

Thessa flattened it carefully.

The paper listed twenty-three crates.

Most were dull even by their current standards.

Personnel files.

Budget records.

Expired warrant books.

Seal molds for destruction.

Closed administrative correspondence.

Then:

**Crates 14–16 — active protected-review continuations — Hall of Veils, Restricted Annex C. Transfer against master successor register.**

Below it:

**Crate 17 — retained comparative property / credential exemplars — Hall of Veils, Restricted Annex C pending disposition.**

Nobody moved.

Ilyan read the two lines again.

Thessa said, "Same destination."

"Yes."

Teren had gone pale.

Ilyan looked at him.

"Did you know?"

"No."

"You packed it."

"I helped close an office with twenty-three crates, four successor institutions and half the staff already reassigned. I knew comparative property went somewhere. I did not know your Revision Seventeen renewal and North Quay exemplars had ended in the same annex."

Ilyan wanted to accuse him of remembering too little.

He stopped himself.

Eighteen years of administrative debris did not become memorable merely because it had become important to Ilyan yesterday.

Thessa pointed to the bottom of the sheet.

"Master successor register."

Teren nodded.

"That is the document that matters. This only tells us where porters were told to put crates. The register should identify originating dockets and final receiving custody."

"Hall of Veils?" Ilyan asked.

"If Annex C still exists under that name."

"Sael can find it."

Teren's expression changed.

"Do not ask him to find it unofficially."

Ilyan bristled.

"I wasn't going to."

"Good. Because if the annex contains active or inherited restrictions, an informal search by a newly sworn examiner with a family connection to one of the records will be noticed for all the wrong reasons."

Thessa folded her arms.

"He's right."

"I know."

"You sound delighted."

"I am surrounded by people being right at me."

Teren's mouth almost smiled.

Ilyan looked back at the packing list.

The paper did not prove that Tal's die had remained in Crate Seventeen.

It did not prove that Revision Seventeen had been in Crates Fourteen through Sixteen.

It did not prove that anyone at the Hall had connected the two categories.

It gave them a place where the successor register ought to have gone.

That was enough for one morning.

Thessa was reading the rest of the sheet.

"Who signed the porter copy?"

Teren leaned closer.

At the bottom were three signatures.

One was Teren's.

One belonged to a property clerk he remembered only after several seconds.

The third was an archive receiving officer.

The name was still legible.

**Aric Saren.**

Ilyan's chair scraped the floor.

Teren looked at him.

"You know him?"

"Not personally."

Thessa's eyes narrowed.

"North Quay."

Ilyan nodded.

"He witnessed Tal's die leaving the disposal lot."

Silence settled over the table.

Teren reached for the sheet, then stopped before touching it.

"The same Saren?"

"We need to verify that."

Thessa gave Ilyan an approving glance.

He resented it less than he expected.

Teren sat down.

"If it is the same man, he moved from North Quay stores into archive receiving work."

"Or there were two Aric Sarens," Thessa said.

"Yes."

"Or the signature is someone signing on his authority."

"Possible."

"Or—"

Ilyan held up a hand.

"I understand the exercise."

Thessa smiled.

"Do you?"

"Unfortunately."

They copied the packing list in full.

Teren refused to surrender the original without process.

Ilyan did not argue.

That surprised both of them.

Before they closed the chest, Teren picked up the small wooden box.

He turned it in his hands.

"This has nothing to do with the Joint Office."

Ilyan waited.

Teren opened it.

Inside lay a child's wooden practice token, painted blue and worn smooth at the edges.

Ilyan recognized it.

He had won it from Teren when he was nine.

Not honestly.

Teren had let him.

At nine, Ilyan had known that and pretended not to.

At twenty, he had forgotten the token existed.

Teren looked at him.

"You were unbearable for a week."

"You cheated."

"In your favor."

"Still cheating."

Thessa looked between them.

"Should I leave?"

"No," both men said.

That made her laugh.

Teren put the token back.

He closed the box.

Affection remained.

So did the packing list on the table.

Neither cancelled the other.

---

They ate bread, soft cheese and cold chicken in the kitchen because none of them wanted to wait for a formal meal.

Teren made tea badly.

Thessa told him so.

"You are remarkably comfortable criticizing people in their own houses," he said.

"Western hospitality requires honesty."

"Does it?"

"No."

Ilyan nearly choked on his tea.

The laughter left him unexpectedly raw.

He set down the cup.

Teren noticed.

He did not ask whether Ilyan was all right.

For that, Ilyan was grateful.

Instead Teren said, "Your uncle will know the Hall has a successor register if he ever cared enough to ask where the office records went."

Ilyan looked at him.

"Do you think he did?"

"I don't know."

"Would my father?"

"I don't know."

Thessa tore bread in half.

"Then the useful question is who can see a lawful request for it."

Teren nodded.

"Hall records staff. Possibly the successor custodian. Depending on classification, whoever currently owns the inherited restriction."

"And family?"

"Not automatically."

Ilyan thought of his father's face.

His uncle's voice.

The oldest oak.

The Seven listening while he told them what his family had done.

"I want Vaun involved."

Thessa nodded.

Teren did not.

Ilyan looked at him.

"Problem?"

"No."

"You made a face."

"Apparently everyone has one now."

"Teren."

His uncle rubbed a hand over his mouth.

"A judge turns curiosity into a record. That is not a reason not to do it. It is a reason to understand what you are doing."

"I do."

"Do you?"

Ilyan's temper stirred.

Then he looked at the copied packing list beside his plate.

"Enough to know I don't want this hidden in my coat."

Teren studied him.

Then nodded.

"Good."

It was not forgiveness.

It was not permission.

It was simply one adult recognizing another adult's choice.

Ilyan found that he wanted it anyway.

---

Judge Vaun read the copy that afternoon.

Damaris arrived halfway through because her River Ledger appointment had ended early. Rhyl came ten minutes later with mud on one boot and no explanation anyone required.

Mireth and Caelen were still at their respective duties. Lyrion had sent word that he was chasing a different archive question and would join them after dusk.

Vaun read the packing list once.

Then again.

"Aric Saren," Damaris said.

"Possibly," Vaun replied.

"The name matches."

"The name matches."

Damaris sighed.

Ilyan felt a small, vicious pleasure that someone else was being corrected.

Thessa saw it.

She kicked his boot under the table.

Vaun continued.

"We verify Saren's employment history. We request the master successor register and the receiving inventory for Restricted Annex C. Separately."

Rhyl leaned over Damaris's shoulder to read the copied sheet.

"Why separately?"

"Because if one survives and the other does not, I want to know that before someone tells me they were always one record."

He nodded.

Damaris pointed to Crate Seventeen.

"If Tal's die was still retained comparative property when the Joint Office dissolved—"

Vaun looked at her.

Damaris stopped.

"If."

"Good."

"I dislike you."

"Frequently useful."

Ilyan sat back.

The room smelled of tea and wet wool.

Damaris's shoulder rested against Rhyl's arm as though it had begun doing so without requiring discussion. Thessa had stolen Ilyan's pencil and was making a clean list of the record requests. Teren had returned to the country house with the original packing sheet and the ugly shirt Thessa had insulted.

Tomorrow the Hall would receive a formal request.

Aric Saren would be located if he could be located.

And somewhere inside the Hall of Veils, perhaps under another name, perhaps under another custodian, perhaps already destroyed according to a perfectly valid schedule, there had once been a Restricted Annex C that received both active protected-review continuations and retained credential exemplars from the dead Joint Office.

Ilyan looked at the copied signature again.

Aric Saren.

One name appearing at two different custody boundaries years apart.

Not an answer.

A person to ask.
