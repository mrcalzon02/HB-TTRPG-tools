# Chapter Five — Something Buried Near the House

By morning, the dead rosemary had acquired three witnesses, two legal seals, one offended gardener, and a small canvas awning because rain, unlike the law, did not respect evidence.

Damaris Caladren-Ilyra stood beneath the east arcade of Reedglass House and watched Judge Lethra Vaun argue with a man who had spent forty-seven years making wealthy people believe plants died only with his permission.

Master Oren Pell was not winning.

He was, however, making defeat extremely dignified.

"I am telling you," he said, "the roots were healthy six days ago."

Judge Vaun crouched beside the circular bed without touching it. Her dark judicial coat had been pinned back from the mud. A bronze broken-circle badge rested at her throat. Beside her, a junior court clerk held a wax tablet beneath his cloak and looked as though he had been born disappointed by weather.

"Healthy by what measure?" Vaun asked.

"By being alive."

"Useful. Anything more specific?"

Oren drew himself up.

"The rosemary along this wall is twelve years established. It drains well. No standing water. No root rot. No grey mould. No insect webbing. No salt burn. The western bed was cut from the same stock and is untouched."

Vaun looked toward the western wall.

"Same gardener?"

Oren's expression became dangerous.

"Yes."

"Same soil?"

"Near enough."

"Near enough is a phrase barristers purchase houses with."

The clerk's mouth twitched.

Oren noticed and glared at him.

Damaris pressed her lips together.

She had slept badly.

That was not unusual after Silver Night. It was becoming unusual for reasons to compete.

The River Ledger cipher still sat in the inner pocket of her morning coat.

Her aunt knew she had used it.

Rhyl knew it existed.

Ilyan knew her family wanted her close to his investigation.

And now a prohibited-magic judge was kneeling in her aunt's garden over a perfect ring of dead rosemary while something wrapped in silk waited under the soil.

There were mornings when adulthood seemed less like a threshold and more like being handed several unrelated knives and told they were all yours.

Lady Meris Caladren-Ilyra stood a few paces away beneath the awning.

She wore river-blue wool instead of silk, plain enough to imply that she had dressed for inconvenience rather than visitors. Her hair was pinned without jewels. She had not asked Damaris why she was present.

That worried Damaris.

Meris always asked questions she already knew the answer to.

Rhyl Orthell-Faen was at the edge of the garden with his coat unfastened and mud on one knee.

He had arrived before Damaris.

That had surprised her until she learned why.

Judge Vaun had wanted someone who understood ground disturbance and did not belong to the household.

The Red Hart had sent Rhyl because the Red Hart apparently considered "look at some suspicious dirt for a judge" an entirely reasonable use of a newly graduated vigil member.

Damaris had found him on his hands and knees beside the old foundation, smelling soil.

She had decided not to comment.

He had looked up and said, "Good morning."

She had said, "Is it?"

He had looked back at the dead rosemary.

"No."

That had been the whole conversation.

So far.

Mireth arrived through the glass garden door carrying a leather case and wearing a Green Covenant field mantle over a dark dress.

Damaris felt relief before she could stop herself.

Mireth saw it.

Of course she did.

"Scholar Silae sends her regrets," Mireth said.

Judge Vaun stood.

"Why?"

"She says the Covenant archive has discovered a filing emergency."

Vaun's face remained perfectly still.

"That is not a thing."

"She used more words."

"I am sure."

Mireth held up the case.

"She sent threshold test papers, sterile sample envelopes, binding thread, and written authorization for me to assist with botanical contamination screening under your direction."

"Can you cast a curse diagnostic?"

"No."

"Can you break a sympathetic binding?"

"No."

"Can you identify every prohibited working someone might bury in a garden?"

"No."

Vaun nodded.

"Excellent. You may be useful."

Mireth blinked.

Damaris almost laughed.

Judge Vaun turned toward Rhyl.

"You."

Rhyl straightened.

"Judge."

"What can you do?"

"Tell you whether the ground was disturbed, approximately when if conditions permit, whether someone approached through the garden rather than the house, and whether the gardener is lying about ordinary soil work."

Oren made a strangled sound.

Rhyl looked at him.

"I don't think you are."

Oren relaxed by perhaps one degree.

Vaun looked at Damaris.

"And you?"

Damaris had not expected the question.

"I live here."

"That is not a skill."

"In a noble household it occasionally is."

Meris's gaze moved to her.

Damaris continued.

"I know which doors servants use. Which family members use the east rooms. Which gardeners are allowed keys. Which visitors are expected to cross the winter garden without being announced. Which household shrines are maintained by staff and which by family. I know where deliveries are left when the river gate is closed."

Judge Vaun nodded once.

"That is a skill."

Mireth looked at Damaris.

There it was again.

The thing they had started learning after graduation.

A friendship could become access.

A household could become evidence.

A person's ordinary knowledge of where the clean linen was kept could suddenly matter more than a noble title.

Damaris did not like how quickly she was becoming good at noticing it.

Vaun faced them all.

"Then we begin with the first rule."

Rhyl glanced at the rosemary.

"Don't touch it?"

"No. The first rule is that we do not begin by deciding it is a curse."

She pointed at the dead circle.

"It may be poison. It may be blight. It may be drainage. It may be an object buried by a child, a servant, a lover, a thief, a gardener, an ancestor, or an idiot. It may be harmless folk practice. It may be malicious sympathetic work. It may be something designed to look like malicious sympathetic work so that this household embarrasses itself in public."

Meris spoke for the first time.

"Which possibility do you consider most likely?"

"I have not examined the object."

"You have examined the plants."

"I have examined dead rosemary."

Meris's expression remained serene.

Damaris knew that expression.

It meant her aunt had found someone she could not hurry and was deciding whether to admire them or have them killed in a very distant future.

Probably admire.

For now.

Judge Vaun continued.

"Second rule: expertise is not permission. I know a great deal about prohibited magic. I am not going to demonstrate any of it for your entertainment."

Oren looked faintly offended that anyone thought he wanted entertainment.

"Mireth has lawful training in memory preservation and botanical handling. That does not make her a curse-worker. Rhyl can read ground disturbance. That does not make him a forensic magistrate. Lady Meris controls the house. That does not give her authority to alter an evidence site after petitioning my court."

Meris inclined her head.

"And Damaris?" Mireth asked.

Vaun looked at her.

"Damaris is going to tell us who could have reached this garden without appearing to trespass."

Damaris's stomach tightened.

"That may be a long list."

"Good."

"Good?"

"Short suspect lists are usually written by people who have already chosen a suspect."

Rhyl looked toward the house.

Reedglass House was beautiful from the garden.

Damaris had never before considered that a flaw.

Its eastern face rose four stories above the canal wall, pale stone broken by tall green-tinted windows. The winter garden projected from the lower floor in old bubbled glass, each pane bending the morning into watery distortions. Ivy climbed around carved drain mouths. The east foundation was older than the visible house and showed where newer stone had been laid over the bones of an earlier residence.

The dead rosemary sat against that seam.

A perfect circle perhaps four feet across.

At its center, a small square of soil had sunk during the night.

The silk was no longer visible.

The gardener had covered it as soon as he realized what he was seeing.

Judge Vaun had approved of that.

She approved of very little else.

"Master Pell," Vaun said, "show us exactly what you did yesterday."

Oren stepped beneath the awning.

He pointed with a pruning knife but did not approach the bed.

"I came through after second rain. Checked the wall herbs. Saw the circle. Thought somebody had poured wash water."

"Why?"

"Shape."

"Wash water makes circles?"

"If poured from a bucket while standing still."

Rhyl nodded.

Oren looked vindicated.

"I tested the outer sprigs. Brittle. The inner ones worse. I scraped the surface here."

He indicated the center.

"With?"

"The narrow hoe."

"Where is it?"

"In the tool room. Untouched since."

"Good."

"I felt cloth."

"With the hoe?"

"Yes."

"You did not lift it?"

"No."

"Why?"

Oren looked at her as though she had asked why he did not eat the garden wall.

"Because people do not generally bury silk under rosemary for sensible reasons."

For the first time, Judge Vaun smiled.

"Master Pell, I withdraw several private criticisms."

"I am relieved."

"Do not be. I have more."

The clerk recorded the exchange with suspicious dedication.

Rhyl crouched again at the edge of the bed.

"Someone came through here."

Damaris moved closer.

"Where?"

He pointed at the gravel path.

She saw nothing.

Then he shifted his hand.

A pale crescent marked the darker grit beside the foundation.

"Boot?"

"Probably."

"Probably?"

"Partial heel edge. Rain took most of it."

Vaun approached.

"Age?"

Rhyl frowned.

"Before yesterday's second rain. After the gravel was raked."

Oren said, "It was raked four mornings ago."

"Then four days at most."

"Could a gardener have made it?" Damaris asked.

"Yes."

Oren inhaled.

Rhyl continued before he could object.

"So could you. So could I. So could half the household."

Damaris looked at the mark.

"Servants usually use the flagged path."

"Usually?"

"Unless carrying soil, ash, pruning waste, coal for the winter garden stove, or something they do not want dripping across the floor."

Vaun looked at her.

"Better."

Damaris disliked being praised by judges. It felt like being marked for later use.

Mireth walked slowly along the rosemary circle.

"The plants didn't all die at exactly the same rate."

Oren looked at her.

"No."

"The center first?"

"I think so."

Mireth crouched.

The dead sprigs nearest the center were blackened at the tips. Farther out, the plants were brown but still held a little green close to the woody stems.

"Something spread outward."

"Or was strongest at the center," Vaun said.

Mireth nodded.

She opened Silae's case.

Inside were strips of pale paper divided by fine green lines.

"These react to common alchemical salts, grave compounds, blood residues, several preservation agents and some sympathetic carriers."

Damaris stared.

"Some?"

Mireth looked at her.

"The Green Covenant does not put 'detect evil' on stationery."

"Unhelpful."

"Reality often is."

Caelen would have liked that answer.

The thought came to Mireth with such immediacy that she nearly smiled.

Then she remembered the green-and-silver ribbon still lying on the archive table.

Not his.

The kiss had been.

The distinction remained warm in her memory.

She put on thin handling gloves.

Judge Vaun watched.

"Outer soil first."

Mireth sampled beyond the dead circle.

Nothing.

Then the edge.

One strip darkened faintly near the bottom.

"Preservation salt," she said.

Oren frowned.

"I use none."

"Could it be fertilizer?"

"Not mine."

Mireth sampled nearer the center.

The next strip darkened almost black.

A second line turned violet.

Judge Vaun's expression changed.

Not fear.

Attention.

"What is violet?" Damaris asked.

Mireth looked at the case legend.

"Protein residue."

Rhyl looked at the buried center.

"Blood?"

"Could be. Could be milk. Egg. Meat wash. Gelatin. Several alchemical binders."

Vaun nodded.

"Do not fall in love with blood. People hear blood and immediately become stupid."

Damaris glanced at her aunt.

Meris remained still.

Too still.

Damaris knew then that Meris had thought the same thing she had.

Sympathetic magic.

Hair.

Blood.

Nails.

Clothing.

Food.

Objects touched often enough to learn a person.

Mireth's lesson from two days ago returned with unpleasant clarity.

The dangerous question was not always who could enter a warded room.

It was who could touch the laundry.

"Who uses this side of the house?" Vaun asked.

Damaris looked up.

"The east family rooms are above us. My aunt's morning room. Two guest suites. The old blue chamber. The winter garden connects to the small dining room."

"Who sleeps in the guest suites?"

"One is empty."

"And the other?"

Meris answered.

"My cousin, Lord Aereth Caladren, when he is in Aurelith."

"Is he?"

"Yes."

Damaris looked at her.

She had not known.

Meris met her eyes.

"He arrived late three nights ago."

Three nights.

The partial heel mark could be four days old at most.

Judge Vaun noticed the exchange.

"Politically relevant?"

Meris's mouth tightened.

"Everyone in this family is politically relevant if you ask them."

"I am asking you."

"He sits as a junior voting member on the River Transit Assessment Council. He is expected to speak next week on the emergency canal levy."

Damaris felt the morning become colder.

The levy had been ugly for months.

Flood repairs, bridge reinforcement, military freight exemptions, grain-barge priorities.

Enough money to make principled people discover principles they had never previously needed.

"Has he been ill?" Vaun asked.

Meris hesitated.

Damaris stared at her.

"Aunt."

"Headaches," Meris said. "Poor sleep. Irritability."

Rhyl's eyebrows rose.

Damaris almost laughed despite herself.

"That describes half the National Council."

"His clerk reported lapses."

"What kind?"

"He misplaced a briefing packet."

Damaris stopped smiling.

Meris continued.

"He accused the clerk of moving it. The packet was later found in Aereth's own locked writing case."

"Anything else?"

"He dismissed a chamber attendant yesterday morning for stealing a signet."

"Did she?"

"The signet was found in his washroom."

Damaris looked toward the house.

"Was she reinstated?"

"Yes."

"Apologized to?"

Meris's silence answered.

Damaris's jaw tightened.

"Aunt."

"This is not the moment."

"It is precisely the moment if we're discussing damage."

Judge Vaun raised one hand.

Both women stopped.

Not because they wanted to.

Because she was very good at making a raised hand feel like architecture.

"Lord Aereth is an adult politically relevant member of this household," Vaun said. "He has recently displayed memory lapses, suspicion, misplaced objects and interpersonal volatility."

Meris nodded.

"Those symptoms prove nothing magical."

"Correct."

Damaris looked at the dead rosemary.

"But if something buried under his windows was meant to make him distrust everyone around him..."

"Then whoever did it chose a subtle weapon," Mireth said.

Rhyl looked toward the upper windows.

"One that makes the victim damage his own relationships."

Judge Vaun's gaze sharpened.

"Now you are thinking usefully. Stop before you begin pretending that usefulness is proof."

She pointed at the center of the circle.

"We recover the object."

The clerk produced a numbered evidence cloth.

Oren produced the narrow hoe.

Judge Vaun made him set it down.

"No."

Oren looked offended.

"You said—"

"You are the witness who first contacted the object. You are not the excavator."

She looked at Rhyl.

"Can you remove soil without touching the cloth?"

"Yes."

"Do it."

Rhyl knelt.

Damaris watched his hands.

That annoyed her.

There was a potentially illegal curse beneath her family's garden, her aunt had withheld the arrival of a politically useful cousin suffering suspicious lapses, and Damaris's attention still found time to notice Rhyl rolling his sleeves above his wrists.

Human feeling—or elven feeling, technically—was an appallingly unserious mechanism.

He used a flat wooden planting blade rather than metal.

One layer.

Then another.

Wet soil came away dark and rich.

The silk appeared slowly.

Pale grey.

Not expensive.

Not household livery.

A square bundle perhaps the size of two fists pressed together.

Thin black thread crossed it seven times.

Mireth inhaled.

Judge Vaun said, "What?"

"The knots."

"Recognize them?"

"No."

"Then say what you know."

Mireth leaned closer without crossing the marked boundary.

"They are tied in alternating directions. Left over right. Right over left. Each turn reversed."

Rhyl stopped digging.

Damaris looked at the thread.

Seven crossings.

The number meant nothing.

Numbers had become difficult lately.

Six witness screens.

Thirteen mnemonic plates.

Two cases of memory salt.

Seven survivors.

Now seven black turns around buried silk.

She refused to let the number become evidence merely because it frightened her.

Judge Vaun looked approvingly at Mireth.

"Anything else?"

"The thread has been waxed."

"Common?"

"For sewing, yes. For garden binding, no."

Oren said, "Nothing in my tool room uses black waxed thread."

Vaun looked at the clerk.

"Record that as his statement, not fact."

The clerk wrote.

Rhyl removed the final soil from around the bundle.

"There's something under it."

No one spoke.

He shifted the planting blade.

A small copper disk appeared beneath the silk.

Not attached.

Placed flat.

Its surface bore a stamped mark.

Damaris knew it.

So did Mireth.

Neither said anything immediately.

Judge Vaun noticed.

"Who recognizes it?"

Damaris answered first.

"Transit assay token."

Meris stepped forward.

The judge's hand rose again.

Meris stopped.

Damaris stared at the copper.

River Ledger freight offices used tokens like it when goods changed custody at certain bonded canal warehouses. Cheap, numbered, difficult to alter without leaving marks.

Not rare.

Not secret.

But not something that belonged beneath rosemary.

"What does it mean?" Rhyl asked.

Damaris swallowed.

"It means someone wanted a piece of the transport system in the bundle."

"Or wanted us to think they did," Vaun said.

Mireth was still staring at the black thread.

"Judge."

"Yes?"

"The wax."

"What about it?"

"I smelled that yesterday."

Damaris looked at her.

"Where?"

Mireth's face had gone still.

"The Hall of Veils."

Judge Vaun did not move.

Mireth continued carefully.

"On the custody thread around one of the certified folders Scholar Silae showed us. Black sealing thread. Resin and bitter myrrh."

Damaris's pulse quickened.

"Same?"

"I did not say same."

Judge Vaun's eyes remained on her.

Mireth corrected herself.

"Similar odor. Similar appearance. I have not compared samples."

"Good."

Rhyl looked at the silk-wrapped object.

"The Hall uses it?"

"The Hall uses several tamper threads," Vaun said. "So do tribunals, licensed memory practitioners, archive couriers and anyone who buys surplus from an authorized supplier."

Damaris felt the shape of Chapter Four's lesson settle over them.

Not the answer.

A shape.

An access network.

A material that moved through institutions.

Something ordinary enough to be available.

Specific enough to trace.

Judge Vaun pointed at the bundle.

"Bag the soil separately. Bag the token separately. The silk remains closed until we are indoors, under witness, with a proper sympathetic screen."

Meris said, "Can you tell whether Lord Aereth is the target?"

"No."

"Can you tell whether this is connected to Silver Night?"

"No."

"Can you tell me anything useful?"

Vaun looked up at her.

"Yes."

Rain tapped against the canvas overhead.

"Someone buried a deliberately wrapped object beside your old foundation recently enough that the disturbance survived in the path. The surrounding plants show a center-out pattern inconsistent with your gardener's normal treatment. The soil carries preservation residue and protein material. The wrapping uses deliberate reversed knotting. A bonded-transit token was placed beneath it. And one component resembles a class of sealing material used across several evidence and memory-handling institutions."

She paused.

"That is useful."

Meris looked at the bundle.

"It is not comforting."

"No," Judge Vaun said. "Comfort is a different profession."

Rhyl lifted the evidence cloth beneath the silk without touching the wrapping itself.

Damaris watched him.

Then she looked up at Reedglass House.

At the green windows.

At the servants' passages behind them.

At the guest room where Lord Aereth had spent three nights accusing innocent people of theft.

Her own home had become an evidence site.

Worse, it had become an evidence site in exactly the way Mireth had warned them about.

Not breached gates.

Not masked intruders.

Access.

Laundry.

Gardens.

Deliveries.

People who belonged.

Damaris felt Rhyl stand beside her.

He did not touch her.

That was almost more intimate now.

"You knew he was here?" he asked quietly.

"No."

"You believe your aunt didn't tell you because of the investigation?"

"I don't know."

Rhyl nodded.

He did not fill the silence for her.

Damaris hated that she was grateful.

Across the garden, Lady Meris watched the evidence bundle disappear into a numbered case.

Then she looked directly at Damaris.

There were conversations families postponed because they loved one another.

There were conversations families postponed because they feared one another.

Damaris was beginning to suspect the most dangerous families were the ones where both explanations were true.

Judge Vaun sealed the case.

"Inside," she said. "Now we find out what your house has been touching."

The east doors opened.

Reedglass House waited beyond them, warm and elegant and full of people who had keys.

The room Judge Vaun chose had once been a breakfast parlor.

Damaris knew this because her grandmother had hated it.

Too little morning sun, Grandmother Ilyra had complained. Too much canal glare. The table was the wrong height. The fireplace smoked whenever the eastern wind turned. The ceiling roses looked like cabbages.

The family had eventually surrendered and converted the room into a household accounting chamber.

That made it ideal for forbidden magic.

Not aesthetically.

Administratively.

It had one door, two tall windows, a stone floor, a broad central table and enough shelving that Judge Vaun could make half the room look official simply by putting numbered evidence slips on it.

Lord Aereth had not been invited.

He had objected.

They heard him objecting now from somewhere beyond the door.

"I am perfectly capable of attending an examination concerning my own household."

A man's voice answered too quietly to distinguish.

Then Aereth again.

"My memory is not failing."

Damaris looked at her aunt.

Meris looked at the evidence case.

Rhyl looked at the door.

Mireth looked at Damaris.

Judge Vaun did not look at anything except her clerk.

"Time?"

"Third bell and seventeen."

"Record Lord Aereth's request to attend and my refusal pending preliminary examination."

The clerk wrote.

Damaris folded her arms.

"You could have told him yourself."

"I did."

"Then why is he still arguing?"

"Because hearing and accepting are different legal categories."

Rhyl coughed into his fist.

Vaun looked at him.

"Do you disagree?"

"No, Judge."

"Good."

The outer argument ended with the unmistakable sound of someone walking away while trying to make every footstep legally actionable.

Damaris closed her eyes.

"He is usually charming."

Meris said, "He believes he still is."

That hurt more than it should have.

Judge Vaun opened the evidence case.

Inside, the grey silk bundle rested on white cloth.

The copper transit token had been placed in a separate shallow tray. Soil samples stood in sealed paper packets beside it.

Vaun did not touch the bundle.

Instead she removed a folded square of dark mesh from her own case.

The mesh looked disappointingly ordinary.

Damaris had expected forbidden-magic law to involve more silver.

Mireth leaned forward.

"Cold iron?"

"No."

"Lead thread?"

"No."

"What is it?"

"Expensive."

Mireth stared.

Vaun unfolded it over the table.

"Silver, ash fiber, undyed linen, powdered glass fixed in gum, and a legally tedious amount of work by people who charge by the inch."

"Sympathetic screen?"

"Passive isolation screen."

"Difference?"

"A sympathetic screen is a category. This is one design within it. Do not let barristers teach you that nouns are mechanisms."

Mireth nodded.

She would remember that.

Damaris suspected Judge Vaun knew she would.

The mesh was pinned at four corners around the evidence bundle, leaving the object visible through a faint dark grid.

Vaun placed three small ceramic markers around it.

No glow appeared.

No ominous whisper filled the room.

Nothing turned green.

Rhyl glanced at Mireth.

Mireth shrugged.

Vaun noticed.

"You are disappointed."

"No."

"You are both disappointed."

Rhyl said, "I expected at least one candle."

"That is theater."

"Does theater never help?"

"In court? Constantly. In evidence handling? Rarely."

She put on gloves.

"Now. The wrapping."

The black thread crossed the grey silk seven times, each turn reversing direction.

Vaun examined the final knot through a small magnifying lens.

"Not a closing knot."

Mireth moved nearer.

"What is it?"

"A counting knot."

Damaris felt something tighten behind her ribs.

"Seven?"

"Seven turns. The knot itself is not seven."

Vaun indicated the tiny structure where the waxed thread ended.

"Three loops. One closed. Two open."

"What does that signify?" Meris asked.

"It signifies three loops, one closed and two open."

Meris gave her a look.

Vaun returned it.

"I am not being difficult for pleasure, Lady Meris."

Damaris doubted that.

"I am refusing to invent meaning because the object has successfully made us nervous."

She cut the thread.

Everyone in the room stopped breathing.

Nothing happened.

Judge Vaun waited.

Then she cut the second crossing.

Then the third.

Each severed length went into its own numbered envelope.

Mireth frowned.

"Separate?"

"If the wax differs between lengths, I want to know."

The fourth.

The fifth.

The sixth.

The seventh.

The silk loosened.

Beneath it was another wrapping.

Not silk.

Linen.

White once, now stained brown at one corner.

A smell entered the room.

Not rot.

Lavender.

Damaris knew it immediately.

Her stomach dropped.

"No."

Judge Vaun froze.

"What?"

Damaris stepped closer.

"That is house linen."

Meris's eyes narrowed.

"You cannot know that from color."

"Not the cloth. The scent."

Mireth inhaled carefully.

Lavender, yes.

Under it something sharper.

Soap.

Rosemary ash.

Damaris looked at her aunt.

"Our laundry stores use lavender water in the final rinse for family bed linen."

Meris said, "So do many houses."

"Not like that."

Damaris pointed.

"Smell beneath it."

Rhyl did.

His face changed.

"Smoke?"

"Rosemary ash," Damaris said. "The laundry mistress adds it to white linen in wet weather. She says canal damp makes cloth smell dead."

Meris looked toward the door.

"Maela."

Damaris's pulse quickened.

"Do not summon her like a suspect."

"I was going to summon her as the woman responsible for the laundry."

"Your voice disagreed."

Judge Vaun held up one finger.

Meris stopped.

Again.

Damaris was beginning to consider hiring the judge for family dinners.

Vaun said, "Who has access to family bed linen?"

Damaris answered.

"Maela Sorn, the laundry mistress. Four laundresses. Two linen maids. Chamber attendants can collect clean sets. Dressers sometimes enter the linen rooms for pressing cloths. Housekeepers have master keys."

"Family?"

"Technically we can go anywhere."

"Practically?"

"We don't."

Rhyl looked at her.

"Why not?"

"Because entering the laundry to prove you can is how you discover thirty employees think you are an ass."

Mireth smiled.

"Healthy institution."

"Occasionally."

Vaun looked at Meris.

"Tradespeople?"

"Soap merchant. Lavender supplier. Furnace sweep. Seamstress when repairs overflow the household staff."

"Outside washerwomen?"

"During major receptions."

Damaris thought of the Hall of Seven Lamps reception two nights away.

Not here.

Still.

Households expanded their access networks around ceremonies.

More temporary hands.

More borrowed staff.

More deliveries.

More reasons for someone to belong.

Vaun peeled the linen open.

Inside lay five objects.

A narrow strip of blue cloth.

Three strands of pale hair tied together.

A sliver of sealing wax impressed with half a crest.

A tiny folded paper packet.

And a shirt button.

Damaris stared.

The button was mother-of-pearl with a silver rim.

Caladren household formalwear.

Meris whispered something very impolite.

Judge Vaun looked at her.

"I will omit that from the formal record."

"Thank you."

"Unless it becomes relevant."

"Of course."

Mireth leaned toward the blue cloth.

"Blood?"

A dark brown stain crossed one edge.

Vaun did not answer.

She used forceps to lift the cloth into a shallow glass dish.

Then the hairs.

Then the wax.

Then the packet.

Then the button.

Each separated.

Each numbered.

Rhyl looked at the arrangement.

"That seems almost too obvious."

Vaun nodded.

"Good."

Damaris looked at him.

"Good?"

"He noticed."

Vaun pointed with the forceps.

"If someone wished merely to establish sympathetic contact with Lord Aereth, why include five recognizable household-linked objects?"

"Redundancy?" Mireth asked.

"Possible."

"Strength?"

"Possible."

"To make sure we identify him?"

Slowly.

Again.

People who cooked.

There.

Who changed the sheets.

Down.

A small courtesy, giving the anger privacy without pretending not to hear it.

Failed.

Cracked enough to let air through.

Family.

Rhyl found Pellin in a room that smelled of boiled onions, damp plaster, and the particular medicinal bitterness of people who had been poor for long enough to become experts at being ill cheaply.

The building stood three streets back from the Lower Reed canal, where respectable warehouses gave way to cooperages, rope lofts, rented rooms and little shops whose signs had been repainted so often the wood had become thicker than the letters.

He had not needed to ask many questions.

A dresser in Caladren-Ilyra service dressed better than most men in the district even on his day away, and Pellin had been seen carrying a basket into number fourteen less than an hour earlier.

Rhyl had watched the building first.

Judge Vaun had said observe.

So he observed.

A woman leaned from the second-floor window twice to shake crumbs from a cloth. A boy delivered coal. An old man came out carrying a chamber pot with the solemnity of a priest bearing relics. Pellin appeared once at the upstairs window, sleeves rolled, hair loose, and poured something steaming into a basin.

No flight.

No hidden carriage.

No meeting in an alley.

No immediate proof of anything except that people continued to have lives while investigators wished they would arrange themselves into cleaner categories.

Then he knocked.

She was thin enough that the bones of her hands showed clearly beneath the skin. A wool blanket had been wrapped around her shoulders despite the mild Bloomtide morning. Her breath carried a faint wet rasp.

"Yes?"

"Is Pellin here?"

Her eyes narrowed.

"Who wants him?"

"Rhyl Orthell-Faen."

That meant nothing to her.

Good.

"Red Hart."

That meant something.

Not necessarily good.

Her shoulders tightened.

"He's done nothing."

Rhyl leaned against neither door nor frame.

"I didn't say he had."

"You people don't come to this street because someone has done well."

Fair.

"I need to ask him a question."

"About the House?"

Rhyl noted the capital letter in her voice.

People who served Great Houses often spoke of them the way sailors spoke of weather.

"Yes."

She looked over her shoulder.

He saw Rhyl.

Stopped.

Not dramatically.

Just enough.

"Pellin?"

"Master Rhyl."

So he knew him.

Also useful.

She said, "If they want to drag you back because some lord misplaced his cufflinks again—"

"They don't."

"Not about cufflinks, anyway."

Pellin's mouth tightened.

"Come in."

Not miserable.

Pellin's sister sat.

Rhyl did too.

"You left Reedglass before second bell."

"Yes."

"Because your sister is ill."

"Yes."

"Marsh lung. Since I was fourteen. If you'd like me to cough for official purposes, I can."

Rhyl almost smiled.

"No."

"What? I practice."

Mostly.

"Name?"

"He's telling you the truth."

Mireth leaned in.

"WT-3."

Damaris stopped breathing.

Ilyan was not there.

Caelen was not there.

None of the others were there to make the connection for them.

They did not need them.

West Tower.

Rhyl said it first.

"West Tower."

Vaun looked at him.

"Likely."

Damaris almost snapped at her.

Likely.

Always likely.

Always possible.

Always not enough.

Then she remembered what certainty had done to Aereth.

What suspicion had done to Talia.

What a frightened family could do with a convenient servant and half a fact.

She let the anger pass.

"Can we establish the code?"

"Yes," Vaun said. "From Academy receiving procedure."

Talia spoke.

"I can."

Everyone looked at her.

She rubbed her palms against the borrowed shawl.

"WT was west tower. The number was floor."

"Three?"

"Third-floor internal receiving cage."

Mireth went very still.

"What was kept there?"

Talia shook her head.

"Whatever departments on three were expecting. It wasn't one office."

Vaun nodded.

"Good."

Talia looked offended.

"Good?"

"You did not improve the answer to make it useful."

That landed.

Talia's face changed.

Damaris saw pride arrive through grief.

Small.

Real.

Vaun turned to the clerk.

"Certified copy of this receipt. Preserve the credential plate. Preserve all seven route receipts. No contact with the other delivery sites until I issue instructions."

Meris frowned.

"Why wait?"

"Because if the same fraudulent credential was used to place material at several locations, sending seven nervous households into the streets at once may destroy exactly what we need."

"And if someone is in danger?"

Vaun looked at the laboratory report.

"Then we do not wait long."

She pointed to the noble townhouse and the physician's cooperative.

"Those two receive quiet welfare checks first. No accusation. No mention of cursecraft unless there is evidence."

Then the council offices.

"Administrative preservation orders."

Then the tribunal annex.

Her mouth tightened.

"I will handle that one."

Damaris looked at Reedglass on the route list.

"And us?"

Vaun looked toward Aereth.

"Protective containment. Voluntary if Lord Aereth agrees."

Aereth folded his arms.

"I dislike that phrase."

"So do I."

"What does it mean?"

"It means you remain at Reedglass tonight, you do not attend council, you do not sign levy papers, you do not dismiss servants, and you do not make irreversible decisions while we determine whether your memory is being interfered with."

His face hardened.

"You are asking me to declare myself incompetent."

"No."

"That is what the council will hear."

Meris stepped forward.

Damaris expected her to argue.

Instead her aunt said, "The council can hear that you developed a fever."

Aereth stared at her.

"You want me to lie."

"I want you to postpone one meeting."

"With a lie."

Meris's expression softened.

"With privacy."

That stopped him.

Damaris watched them.

Another room she had never entered.

Another history between adults who had known one another longer than she had been alive.

Aereth looked at the route receipt.

At the laboratory report.

At Talia.

His shame appeared when he looked at her.

That mattered.

"Talia."

She stiffened.

"My lord."

"I accused you."

"Yes."

"I was wrong."

The room went very quiet.

Talia looked at him for a long time.

"Yes."

Aereth swallowed.

"I am sorry."

She did not rescue him.

Damaris loved her for that.

Finally Talia said, "Thank you."

Not forgiveness.

Not yet.

Enough.

Aereth turned back to Vaun.

"One night."

"One night," Vaun agreed.

"And tomorrow?"

"We know more tomorrow."

He looked exhausted.

"Everyone keeps saying that."

"Because tomorrow is useful."

Aereth almost smiled.

Almost.

Damaris felt Rhyl beside her before he touched her.

His knuckles brushed the back of her hand.

This time she caught his fingers.

No one pretended not to notice.

No one made it important either.

That was better.

The clerk gathered the papers.

Mireth stood over the Academy receipt.

Four days before Silver Night.

West Tower, third floor.

A woman who noticed bad corners had signed for it.

Then died with thirty-five students and five other servants.

Mireth said, "Ilyan needs to see this."

Damaris nodded.

"So do the others."

Vaun looked at them.

"After certification."

"Of course."

"Together, if possible."

Rhyl glanced toward the rain-streaked windows.

"The seven of us haven't managed together since yesterday."

"Welcome to adulthood," Meris said.

Damaris looked at her aunt.

Meris looked back.

There was affection there.

And calculation.

And worry.

All at once.

Damaris was beginning to understand that loving someone did not simplify them.

It only made the complications expensive.

Judge Vaun closed the red laboratory case.

"Get the certified records."

She looked at the route list again.

"Then find me the other six doors."
