# Chapter Five — Something Buried Near the House

Damaris had slept at Reedglass House because her aunt had asked her to, which was not the same as being ordered and therefore somehow more difficult to refuse.

She woke to rain ticking against the old green glass and the smell of coffee drifting beneath her door.

For perhaps ten seconds she remembered none of it.

Then the west tower returned.

Six witness screens. Thirteen black-glass plates. Two cases of memory salt. An emergency custody hold opened before Silver Night. Her aunt's cipher in the pocket of yesterday's coat. Rhyl asking her to tell him what the access cost before he had to guess.

And something buried beneath dead rosemary in the east garden.

Damaris stared at the ceiling.

"Wonderful."

The ceiling offered no correction.

She dressed quickly and found the household already moving around the problem.

Nobody had dug anything up.

That improved her opinion of everyone involved.

The east garden lay behind the winter room, where old panes of bubbled glass made the wet morning look submerged. A canvas awning had been stretched from the arcade to keep rain off the patch beside the foundation. Beneath it, the rosemary had died in a near-perfect ring.

The rest of the bed was green and sharp-scented from the rain.

The dead patch was brown at the edge and almost black near the center.

Master Oren Pell, the gardener, stood several paces away with his arms folded.

"I did not kill it," he told Damaris before she asked.

"Good morning to you too."

"Your aunt asked whether I had changed the soil."

"Did you?"

He looked personally wounded.

"No."

Damaris crouched at the edge of the paving.

The center of the circle had sunk slightly. Yesterday's rain had exposed a narrow fold of grey fabric under the soil.

She did not touch it.

"Who found the cloth?"

"Me."

"How?"

"I scraped the topsoil with the narrow hoe. Felt it catch. Stopped."

"And then?"

"Sent for the steward. The steward sent for your aunt. Your aunt sent for half the city."

"Only half?"

"It is early."

Damaris smiled despite herself.

Footsteps sounded beneath the arcade.

Rhyl came through the garden door carrying his wet cloak over one arm. His hair was dark with rain and had begun to curl at the ends. He saw Damaris, and something in his face eased before he looked toward the rosemary.

That tiny change was becoming dangerous to her.

"You stayed," he said.

"Apparently I live here."

"I had heard rumors."

Oren looked between them.

Damaris ignored him.

Rhyl came to the edge of the bed and crouched beside her, close enough that the wet leather at his shoulder brushed her sleeve.

"Don't step there," he said.

She looked down.

A shallow crescent disturbed the gravel beside her boot.

"That?"

"Maybe a heel. Rain took most of it."

"Maybe?"

"If I say definitely, someone will write it down."

Damaris glanced at him.

"You are learning."

"Terrifying, isn't it?"

His hand settled briefly against the small of her back as he leaned past her to study the ground.

The contact was practical.

It was also warm through two layers of clothing, and Damaris became annoyed with her own body for treating forensic gardening as an opportunity.

Rhyl's fingers left her.

She missed them immediately.

"Gravel was raked recently," he said.

Oren answered from behind them. "Four mornings ago."

Rhyl nodded. "Then the mark is newer than that. Could be household staff. Could be you. Could be me if I had been here."

"You were not."

"Useful."

"I am always useful."

"You are a gardener."

Oren stared at him.

Rhyl looked up. "That was admiration."

"Work on your delivery."

Damaris covered a smile with her hand.

A carriage stopped in the lane beyond the wall.

Judge Lethra Vaun arrived without ceremony, wearing a dark rain coat over judicial green. A junior clerk followed with a case tucked beneath his arm. Mireth came behind them in a Green Covenant field mantle, carrying a second case and looking as though she had been awake long enough to resent the entire concept of morning.

Damaris rose.

Mireth saw Rhyl beside her, saw the expression Damaris had not quite finished hiding, and lifted one eyebrow.

Damaris gave her a look that promised consequences.

Mireth's mouth twitched.

Judge Vaun walked directly to the dead bed.

"Who touched the exposed cloth?"

"No one," Oren said.

"The soil?"

"Me. Narrow hoe. Yesterday. Tool is locked in the garden room."

Vaun nodded to her clerk, who wrote it down.

She looked at Mireth. "What did Silae send?"

"Sampling papers, sterile envelopes, binding thread, gloves, and a note saying I am permitted to assist with botanical contamination screening and nothing more interesting."

"Good."

Mireth looked disappointed on principle.

Vaun's attention shifted to Damaris.

"Your aunt says you know household access."

"I know the house."

"Who uses this side?"

Damaris looked up at the windows.

"My aunt's morning room. Two guest suites. The blue room. Winter room below. Staff use the east service corridor, but deliveries normally come through receiving unless they're garden supplies, coal, ash, soil, or something wet enough that the housekeeper refuses to let it cross the good floors."

"Garden gate locked?"

"At night."

"Keys?"

Oren named himself, two assistants, the steward, the housekeeper and Lady Meris.

Damaris added, "And any senior servant can fetch one from the steward's board during the day."

Vaun looked at her.

"Recorded?"

"Usually."

"Usually is the interesting word."

Damaris nodded.

Mireth had knelt at the edge of the dead circle. She pulled on gloves and tested soil from outside the damaged bed first. The paper remained pale.

At the outer edge of the dead rosemary, one line darkened.

Nearer the center, it went almost black. A second mark bloomed violet.

Oren leaned forward.

"What does that mean?"

"Preservation salts and protein residue," Mireth said. "Possibly. The papers are broad tests."

"Blood?" Damaris asked.

"Could be. Could be several other things."

Vaun gestured toward the center. "We recover the bundle before we invent a story for it."

Rhyl used a flat wooden planting blade from Mireth's kit. He worked slowly, taking wet soil away from the edges until the grey cloth showed itself.

It was a square bundle, no larger than both his fists together, wrapped in dull silk and bound with black waxed thread.

Seven turns.

Damaris noticed the number and hated herself for noticing it.

Rhyl glanced at her.

He knew.

Of course he knew.

Seven survivors had made the number poisonous.

He said nothing.

That helped.

Mireth leaned closer. "Alternating knots."

Vaun looked at her.

"Left over right, then right over left. Deliberate pattern. I don't recognize a specific working."

"Good."

"You keep saying that when I fail to know things."

"It makes you less dangerous."

Mireth considered that and accepted it.

Rhyl cleared the final soil from beneath the bundle.

A copper disk lay flat under it.

Damaris knew the stamp before anyone asked.

River Ledger transit assay token.

Her stomach tightened.

The token was cheap, numbered, and common around bonded warehouses. It had no business under rosemary beside her aunt's foundation.

Vaun looked at her. "You know it."

"Transit assay token. Used when bonded cargo changes custody."

"Rare?"

"No."

"Traceable?"

"Sometimes. Number and issue series."

Rhyl did not lift it. The clerk photographed the arrangement with a small plate camera before Vaun allowed either object to be moved.

Only when the bundle and token were separately bagged did Lady Meris come out from the house.

She had dressed simply in river-blue wool, but Damaris had known her too long to mistake simplicity for lack of intention.

Meris looked at the evidence bags.

Then at Damaris.

"You slept?"

"Enough."

"That means no."

"It means enough."

Meris let it go.

Damaris felt the old affection beneath the irritation. Her aunt had sat beside her through childhood fevers. Had taught her how to read grain contracts by letting her find the clauses designed to cheat tenants. Had once crossed half the city in winter because Damaris, at fourteen, had been too embarrassed to admit she had bled through a pale dress at a recital.

None of that had stopped Meris from giving her the cipher.

None of the cipher erased any of it.

Damaris was discovering that adulthood contained far fewer clean replacements than she had expected.

Vaun asked, "Who is in the east guest suite?"

Meris's gaze moved to the upper windows.

"Aereth."

Damaris turned. "Cousin Aereth is here?"

"He arrived three nights ago."

"You didn't tell me."

"You were graduating."

That was a perfectly reasonable answer and therefore infuriating.

"Why is he here?"

"River Transit Assessment Council. Emergency canal levy."

Damaris looked at the copper token again.

Vaun asked, "Any reason to believe the buried object concerns him?"

Meris hesitated.

Only a heartbeat.

Damaris saw it.

"What happened?"

"Nothing conclusive."

"Aunt."

Meris looked at Vaun rather than her. "He has been sleeping badly. Headaches. Yesterday he accused a chamber attendant of stealing his signet. It was found in his washroom."

Damaris's anger arrived fast.

"Which attendant?"

"Talia."

"Was she dismissed?"

"For an hour."

"Was she paid for the hour?"

Meris gave her a flat look.

"Yes."

"Was she apologized to?"

That silence was answer enough.

Damaris turned toward the house.

Meris caught her wrist.

Not hard.

"Do not go upstairs and fight with a man who may be ill."

"I was going to speak to Talia."

Meris studied her.

"That is not better for my morning."

"It may be better for hers."

Meris released her.

Rhyl had watched the exchange without intervening.

Damaris felt his attention like a hand between her shoulder blades.

Vaun said, "I want the guest room left alone until I speak with Lord Aereth. No searches. No medicine moved. No laundry collected."

Meris nodded.

"Done."

"And Talia stays available. She is a witness, not a suspect."

Damaris looked at her aunt.

Meris looked back.

"I heard her."

There was enough edge in the answer to remind Damaris that Meris did not enjoy being morally supervised by her niece in front of strangers.

Good.

A little discomfort was healthy.

Mireth lifted the sealed bundle. "Where do we open it?"

Vaun glanced toward the winter room. "Inside. Clean table. Witnesses limited."

Oren cleared his throat.

"Am I limited?"

"You are the first-contact witness. You stay until I finish with you."

He sighed as though the judiciary had personally attacked his pruning schedule.

Damaris followed the others toward the house.

Rhyl caught her hand just before the garden door.

She looked back.

His fingers were muddy at the knuckles. He had been careful not to touch her dress.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"You know I hate that answer."

"I know."

He glanced toward Meris, already inside.

Then back to Damaris.

"You told Ilyan the cipher was audited."

"Yes."

"You told us your aunt wants you close enough to understand what's happening with his family."

"Yes."

"Is there more I need to know today?"

The question was gentle.

That made it harder.

Damaris thought of Meris's exact words at graduation. Stay close. Listen. Understand enough to tell me whether Valeric instability is going to become everybody's problem.

Not seduce him.

Not deceive him into confidence.

Nothing hidden beneath the words. No command to seduce him. No instruction to manufacture a confidence he had not freely given.

Meris's actual request was uncomfortable enough without Damaris inventing a worse one for herself.

"No," she said. "Not today."

Rhyl watched her for a moment.

Then nodded.

He believed her.

The realization warmed and frightened her in equal measure.

Damaris tightened her fingers around his.

"That doesn't mean there won't be tomorrow."

"I know."

"You are infuriatingly reasonable."

"Only before breakfast."

She stepped closer.

Rain shone along the dark hair at his temple. His mouth was very near hers, and for one foolish second the entire garden narrowed to the shape of it.

Damaris kissed him.

Briefly.

Deliberately.

Not hidden, exactly. The glass door stood open. Anyone inside could have turned around.

Rhyl went still, then his free hand settled at her waist.

When she drew back, he looked pleased in a way that made her want to do it again and annoyed her for making that obvious.

"Before breakfast," she said.

"Apparently."

Mireth's voice came from inside.

"If you two are finished conducting independent research, the judge is waiting."

Damaris closed her eyes.

Rhyl laughed against her forehead.

"I will kill her."

"Probably not in front of Vaun."

"See? Reasonable. Infuriating."

They went in together.

The winter room smelled of damp wool, rosemary and old glass warmed by the house fires.

Vaun had cleared the central table. Her clerk laid fresh paper beneath the evidence bag. Mireth stood opposite her. Meris remained near the window, arms folded.

Talia had been brought in and was waiting by the door.

She was perhaps thirty, young by elven standards but older than Damaris, with dark hair pinned under a house cap and the stiff posture of someone who had spent the previous day learning exactly how conditional employment could feel.

Damaris crossed to her first.

"Talia."

"My lady."

"I'm sorry."

Talia's expression changed slightly. "You didn't dismiss me."

"No."

"Then you don't owe me the apology."

Damaris looked toward the stairs.

"Someone does."

Talia followed her gaze.

"Yes."

No gratitude. No attempt to make Damaris comfortable.

Good.

Vaun called them to the table.

The black thread came away first.

Mireth recorded each knot before cutting anything.

Inside the silk was a second wrapping: coarse household linen, clean but faintly scented with the soap Reedglass used for family rooms.

Meris noticed too.

"That is ours."

Vaun looked at her. "Certain?"

"No. Familiar."

Damaris appreciated the correction before Vaun had to make it.

The linen opened.

Inside lay a narrow strip of blue cloth, three dark hairs, two clipped fingernails, a broken brass button, a curl of black waxed thread, and a small folded packet dusted grey at the edges.

Nobody spoke.

Talia inhaled sharply.

Damaris looked at her.

"What?"

Talia pointed to the blue cloth.

"That looks like the shaving cloth from the east guest room."

Meris turned. "Aereth's?"

"The room's. It was there before he arrived."

"Missing?"

Talia swallowed. "I don't know. I haven't been allowed back in since yesterday."

Vaun looked at the brass button. "Household uniform?"

Talia leaned closer without touching it.

"Old service coat. Maybe. We changed buttons last year."

"Who would have the old ones?"

"Laundry stores. Mending room. People who took uniforms home."

Damaris thought of the house as she had described it outside: doors, corridors, keys.

That was the noble version.

Talia was describing another house layered inside it.

Laundry baskets. Mending shelves. shaving cloths. discarded buttons. Servants entering rooms because rooms needed to keep functioning.

Mireth looked at her.

They had learned the same lesson in different buildings.

The Academy had taught wards as boundaries.

Ordinary life crossed boundaries all day.

Vaun sealed the grey packet separately.

"Laboratory."

Mireth nodded. "It resembles memory salt."

"Resembles."

"Yes."

No lecture followed.

It did not need one.

Rhyl examined the black thread through the clear evidence sleeve.

"Same kind as the outer binding?"

"Looks like it," Mireth said.

Talia frowned.

"We use that."

Everyone turned toward her.

She looked suddenly frightened by the attention.

"Where?" Vaun asked.

"Mending room. Waxed repair thread. Black for work coats and travel cloaks."

"Who has access?"

Talia gave a helpless little laugh.

"Everyone who tears clothes."

Damaris looked at Meris.

Meris looked back.

There it was.

Not a locked-room mystery.

Not yet.

A household mystery.

Which was worse.

Vaun asked Talia for names of staff who had serviced the east rooms during Aereth's stay. Talia gave them carefully, including herself. She did not omit her own access to make the list prettier.

When she reached the laundry, she paused.

"Pellin."

Rhyl looked up.

"Who?"

"Pellin Doss. Laundry runner. He carried the east-floor basket the day Lord Aereth arrived."

"Where is he?"

"Not here."

Meris's face tightened. "Why not?"

"His sister is sick. He asked for two days."

"When?"

"Yesterday morning. Before the signet business."

Damaris saw the room try to decide what that meant.

Vaun prevented it by doing nothing dramatic at all.

She simply looked at Rhyl.

"Can you find him without frightening his neighborhood?"

"Yes."

"Do that. Ask where he went, what he carried, who spoke to him. Do not bring him back unless he wants to come or I send authority."

Rhyl nodded.

Damaris caught his sleeve before he turned away.

"Be careful."

He looked down at her hand.

Then at her.

"I will."

There were a dozen things she could have added.

She trusted him enough not to.

Rhyl left through the service corridor.

Damaris watched until he disappeared.

When she turned back, Meris was watching her.

Not disapproving.

Not amused.

Thinking.

Damaris knew that look too well.

"Don't," she said quietly.

Meris's eyebrows rose. "Don't what?"

"Make him useful."

For a moment neither moved.

Then Meris looked toward the corridor where Rhyl had gone.

"My dear, he already is."

Damaris felt anger flare.

Meris raised one hand before she could answer.

"That is not the same as saying I intend to use him."

"You see the distinction."

"I make my living from distinctions."

"So does Judge Vaun. I trust hers more this morning."

That landed.

Meris's face went still.

Damaris immediately regretted it.

Not because it was false.

Because she knew exactly where to put the knife now.

Adulthood again.

New skills arriving before wisdom about when to use them.

Meris looked at the evidence on the table.

"Then learn why."

Damaris blinked.

Her aunt's voice had softened.

"Learn why you trust hers more. Learn whether it is because she has earned it, because she asks less of you, or because she has not loved you long enough to disappoint you. Those are different things."

Damaris had no answer ready.

Meris turned back to Vaun.

The conversation was over.

For now.

Outside, rain moved across the green glass in silver threads.

On the table lay a copper transit token, household linen, clipped nails, hair, a shaving cloth, old uniform pieces and a packet of grey powder.

None of it yet said who had buried the bundle.

None of it said why.

But someone had reached into the ordinary life of Reedglass House and assembled pieces of it beneath Aereth's window.

Damaris looked toward the service corridor.

Somewhere beyond the canal district, Rhyl was looking for a laundry runner whose sister was ill.

Upstairs, Aereth had not yet been told what had been found beneath his room.

And in two nights the Seven were expected to stand beneath the lamps of half the political world and smile as though graduation had made their lives simpler.

Damaris suspected the dress was going to be the easiest part.

## Pellin Doss and the East Room

Pellin Doss lived above a cooper whose yard smelled of wet oak, sour beer and woodsmoke.

Rhyl found the address without asking anyone where the laundry runner had gone. The Reedglass steward had given him a street and a number, and the Red Hart had spent enough time teaching him to read cities as badly organized forests that he preferred not to announce himself to every neighbor between the canal and the door.

The building leaned toward the lane. Rain ran from one roof to the next and gathered beneath a cracked gargoyle before dropping in a steady rope beside the entrance.

Rhyl knocked.

A woman coughed inside.

Not the polite cough of someone buying time before opening a door. This came from deeper down and ended in a ragged breath.

A man's voice said something too quiet to catch.

Then the latch moved.

Pellin was younger than Rhyl had expected. Perhaps thirty, which meant old enough to have stopped being called a boy by anyone except elderly relatives and noble employers. His shirt was rolled to the elbows. A damp cloth hung over one shoulder.

He saw Rhyl's Red Hart clasp and went pale.

"If this is about the house, I asked Talia before I left."

"It is about the house. I'm not here to take you anywhere."

Pellin stared at him.

Behind him the woman coughed again.

Rhyl nodded toward the room. "Your sister?"

"Lessa."

"Is she all right?"

"No."

It was such an immediate answer that Rhyl liked him a little for it.

Pellin glanced back. "Marsh lung. The healer says it sounds worse than it is. Lessa says the healer can go drown in his own optimism."

A weak voice from inside called, "I said he could choke on it."

Pellin closed his eyes.

Rhyl waited.

"You can come in," Pellin said. "If you don't mind being insulted by a sick woman."

"I've been insulted by healthy ones."

"Less sporting."

The room was small and warm. A kettle sat near the stove. Lessa occupied the bed beneath the window with three blankets, a clay cup and the furious dignity of someone who had not intended illness to interrupt her week.

She looked Rhyl over.

"He's pretty."

Pellin made a strangled sound.

Rhyl pulled the only spare chair away from the wall.

"I'm told that occasionally."

"Sit down, pretty Red Hart. My brother has been pacing since yesterday."

"Lessa."

"You have."

Pellin sat on the edge of the washstand instead.

Rhyl kept his hands visible.

"A bundle was found in the east garden at Reedglass. Some of what was inside came through the east rooms. Judge Vaun is working out who handled what. Talia said you carried the basket when Lord Aereth arrived."

Pellin's face tightened.

"I did."

"Tell me about that day."

Pellin looked relieved by the shape of the question.

"His things came late. Two trunks, coat case, writing case. I carried the laundry basket down after supper. Shirts, underlinen, towels. There was a shaving cloth the next morning. Blue linen. Blood on one corner."

Rhyl remembered the cloth on Vaun's table.

"What happened to it?"

"Washroom basket. I took it down with the rest. Maela sorts stains before boiling."

"Did it come back upstairs?"

"Probably."

"Probably?"

Pellin shrugged. "There are forty blue cloths in that house. Maybe more. Once they're washed, I don't know which one was his unless it's marked. That one wasn't."

Useful because it was ordinary.

"Buttons?"

Pellin frowned.

"His brown council coat lost one. Another was loose. I took it to mending."

"Who repaired it?"

"Could have been Maela. Could have been Osa. Could have been me if they were busy. I can sew on a button."

"Black waxed thread?"

"For work coats, yes."

Rhyl let the silence sit.

Pellin rubbed his palms on his trousers.

"I didn't bury anything."

"I didn't ask whether you did."

"Everyone will."

"Eventually."

Lessa coughed into her cloth.

"He came straight here," she said when she could breathe again. "Yesterday morning. Complained about the stairs. Burned the porridge. Has been useless ever since."

Pellin looked wounded. "I made soup."

"You made hot water with aspirations."

Rhyl looked at Pellin. "When did you leave Reedglass?"

"Before second bell."

"Anyone see you go?"

"Gate porter. Kitchen girl, maybe. I signed out."

"Good."

Pellin's shoulders eased slightly.

Rhyl asked about the east rooms. Who came in. Who went out. Which tradesmen were expected. Which people belonged there enough that nobody noticed them.

Most of the answers were boring.

A coal boy. Two chambermaids. A tailor's apprentice. A physician's runner who left a tonic. A River Ledger courier.

Rhyl stopped.

"What did the courier bring?"

"Council packet."

"For Aereth?"

Pellin nodded.

"Did you know him?"

"No."

"Anything you remember?"

Pellin thought about it instead of rushing.

"Young. Dark hair. Good boots. Too good for a runner."

"Anything else?"

"Ring."

"What kind?"

"Silver, I think. Green stone. Big enough I noticed when he handed me the packet."

Lessa opened one eye.

"Pellin notices jewelry because he has none."

"Thank you."

"You're welcome."

Rhyl smiled despite himself.

"Did the packet go straight to Aereth?"

Pellin's expression changed.

"That's the strange part."

Rhyl waited.

"I took it upstairs. His lordship was at the desk. I said it had come from the council office. He told me he'd already read it."

"Was it open?"

"No."

Pellin looked at his own hands as if reconstructing them.

"The wax still crossed the fold. I remember because I thought maybe there were two packets and I'd brought the wrong one. He got angry when I said it was sealed. Then he took it from me, stared at it for a while and broke the wax."

"What happened after?"

"He read it."

"And?"

"Later he asked Talia why his council packet hadn't arrived."

The stove ticked.

Rain whispered against the window.

Rhyl felt the shape of the testimony settle without letting it become larger than it was.

"Did Talia answer him?"

"Said he'd been reading it all afternoon."

"How did he react?"

"He told her not to mock him."

Pellin swallowed.

"He wasn't joking."

"Anything unusual about the packet itself?"

"Not that I saw."

"The courier?"

Pellin frowned again.

"Cloves."

"Cloves?"

"He smelled of them. Strongly. Maybe oil. Maybe sweets. I don't know."

Rhyl stood.

Pellin stood too quickly.

"Am I in trouble?"

"I don't know."

The answer frightened him.

Rhyl added, "But I'm going back to tell Judge Vaun exactly what you told me, including that your sister is genuinely ill and that you appear to have come here when you said you did. She may want to speak with you."

Pellin nodded.

"I'll come."

Lessa said, "After soup."

Pellin looked at her.

"You said it was hot water."

"It's still food."

Rhyl reached the door.

"Pellin. Don't discuss the courier with anyone yet."

"Why?"

"Because right now it's your memory. I'd rather it stayed yours."

Pellin understood enough to stop asking.

Outside, the rain had eased.

Rhyl walked back toward Reedglass thinking about green stone, good boots, cloves and a sealed packet a frightened man remembered reading before it had been opened.

He did not yet know which of those things mattered.

That was why he intended to bring all of them home.

Aereth was awake when Damaris went upstairs.

He was sitting beside the window in a robe with his hair unbound, staring down at the garden where Vaun's people had covered the dead rosemary with canvas.

He looked at Damaris in the glass before turning.

"Meris says there's something under my window."

"There was."

"Was?"

"Judge Vaun removed it."

Aereth's gaze sharpened. "What was in it?"

Damaris closed the door behind her but did not lock it.

"Things from the house. Cloth. Hair. A button. Some powder they're testing."

"Mine?"

"Some may be."

"May be."

He looked back toward the garden.

"Everyone has started speaking to me as though certainty might break me."

Damaris crossed the room and sat opposite him.

"Would you prefer I invent some?"

That earned the smallest twitch at the corner of his mouth.

"No."

His hands were steady on his knees.

"Talia says I accused her of stealing my signet."

Damaris waited.

"I remember finding it in my washroom."

"She says that too."

"I remember knowing she'd moved it."

"Do you remember seeing her move it?"

Aereth's eyes went to hers.

Anger arrived, then faltered.

"No."

"Then those are different memories."

He stood and paced once to the desk.

"You sound like a judge."

"Gods forbid."

This time he did smile, briefly.

Then he looked at the dark bottle beside the washbasin.

"They'll want that."

"Probably."

"It's sleep medicine."

"I know."

"I've taken it for months."

"Then testing it may tell them nothing."

"Or everything."

"Maybe."

Aereth laughed without humor.

"There it is again."

Damaris leaned back.

"I can leave if you'd rather be lied to by someone prettier."

He looked at her properly then.

"You always were rude."

"Only to family."

"A cherished privilege."

The room quieted.

Aereth picked up the bottle, turning it once between his fingers.

"I dismissed Talia."

"Yes."

"Meris reinstated her."

"Yes."

"Did I apologize?"

Damaris said nothing.

Aereth shut his eyes.

"Right."

He set the bottle down.

"Bring her."

"Aereth—"

"Please. Before I decide I remember doing it already."

Damaris found Talia downstairs with Vaun.

Talia went pale when she heard the request, but she came.

Aereth remained standing when she entered.

"Talia."

"My lord."

"I accused you of taking my signet."

"Yes."

"I was wrong."

Talia's face did not soften for him.

Good, Damaris thought.

Aereth swallowed.

"I dismissed you. Meris reversed it. I should have apologized before she had to make me. I'm sorry."

Talia looked at him for several breaths.

"Thank you."

Not forgiveness.

Not punishment either.

Aereth nodded.

Judge Vaun appeared at the open door.

"Lord Aereth. May I examine the room and take the tincture for testing?"

He looked at the bottle.

Then at Damaris.

She kept her face still.

His choice.

"Yes," he said.

Vaun entered with her clerk.

No speech followed. No declaration that the room had transformed into evidence. The clerk set down clean cloths. Vaun sealed the bottle, noted its level, and asked Aereth when he had last taken it.

He answered.

Then corrected himself.

Then went silent.

Damaris stayed beside him.

A few minutes later footsteps sounded in the corridor.

Rhyl returned wet at the shoulders and carrying no prisoner.

Damaris knew, before he spoke, that he had found something.

Not an answer.

Something better.

A question with a name attached to it.

---

## The Packet Remembered Twice

Rhyl found them in Aereth's room.

Judge Vaun had opened the shutters despite the rain. Grey light lay across the desk, the washstand and the narrow strip of carpet beside the bed. Her clerk was kneeling beside a travelling chest with a sheet of paper balanced on one knee. Talia stood near the door. Damaris sat beside Aereth at the window.

Aereth looked worse than when Rhyl had last seen him.

Not ill, exactly. Smaller.

That was a strange thing to think about a man who had spent most of Damaris's childhood filling rooms simply by entering them.

Rhyl stopped inside the doorway and took off his wet cloak.

Damaris looked at his empty hands first.

He liked that she did not ask whether Pellin had run.

"He's with his sister," Rhyl said.

Talia's shoulders loosened.

"Lessa?"

"Marsh lung. He says the healer isn't worried. Lessa disagrees with the healer's personality."

Talia almost smiled.

"That sounds like Lessa."

Vaun looked up from the washstand. "Did Pellin agree to speak with me?"

"After soup."

"Sensible."

"His sister insisted."

"More sensible."

Rhyl glanced at Damaris.

She had shifted just enough to make room beside her on the window bench.

He crossed to her and sat.

Their knees touched.

Neither moved.

Aereth watched that happen with the faint distracted recognition of an uncle noticing a courtship several rooms away from his immediate catastrophe.

"What did he say?" Vaun asked.

Rhyl gave it to her plainly.

The basket. The blue shaving cloth. The coat button. The mending room. The courier.

At the green-stone ring, Talia frowned.

At the cloves, Damaris did.

At the sealed packet, Aereth stopped breathing for a moment.

Rhyl noticed because everyone else did.

"Say that again," Aereth said.

Rhyl looked at Vaun.

She gave the smallest nod.

"Pellin says a River Ledger courier brought a council packet for you. He carried it upstairs. He says you told him you'd already read it."

Aereth's fingers closed around the edge of the chair.

"I had."

No one answered.

Aereth looked from face to face.

"I remember reading it."

Damaris turned toward him. "What do you remember?"

"The levy tables."

His answer came immediately.

"The western dredging appropriation. Two bridge requests. Varanel's objection to the warehouse assessment."

Damaris knew the names well enough to know none of that sounded invented for effect.

"Where were you?"

"Here."

"At the desk?"

"Yes."

"Who brought it?"

Aereth opened his mouth.

Nothing came.

His eyes moved toward the desk as though someone might still be standing there.

"I don't know."

Rhyl said, "Pellin says when he brought the packet, the wax was still across the fold."

Aereth stared at him.

"No."

Talia's hand tightened around the cuff of her sleeve.

Rhyl continued carefully. "He says you told him you'd read it. He pointed out it was sealed. You took it, broke the wax and read it."

"No."

The second denial was quieter.

Damaris felt Aereth's shoulder against hers.

He had gone rigid.

"Then later," Rhyl said, "Pellin says you asked Talia why the packet hadn't arrived."

Aereth looked at Talia.

She did not look away.

"You did," she said.

He closed his eyes.

"And you told me I'd been reading it."

"Yes."

"I thought you were mocking me."

"Yes."

He breathed out slowly.

Damaris watched the rain strike the sill.

Nobody filled the silence for him.

Finally Aereth said, "I remember it twice."

Vaun folded her hands.

"Tell me."

"I remember reading it before Pellin came."

He swallowed.

"And I remember him bringing it."

"Do either of those memories feel less real?"

Aereth laughed once.

There was no humor in it.

"Wouldn't that be convenient?"

Vaun waited.

He rubbed both hands over his face.

"No."

Damaris said, "Then don't choose one yet."

Aereth looked at her.

"You make that sound easy."

"It isn't."

"Which one do you believe?"

She considered lying to him for perhaps half a heartbeat.

Not because she wanted to deceive him.

Because he was frightened and she loved him and there were kinds of mercy that arrived wearing the same coat as dishonesty.

"I believe you remember both."

His face tightened.

"That isn't what I asked."

"It's what I know."

For a moment she thought he might be angry.

Instead he leaned back and stared at the ceiling.

"Gods. You really have become a Caladren."

"Cruel."

"Precise."

"Worse."

Rhyl's hand settled beside hers on the bench.

Not over it.

Close enough that the warmth of his little finger touched hers when either of them breathed too deeply.

Vaun went to the desk.

"Where is the packet now?"

Talia answered. "Document tray. Unless he moved it."

Aereth looked offended.

Then looked ashamed of being offended.

The tray held eleven folded papers, three council circulars, two invitations, a grain memorandum and the remains of a broken green wax seal.

Vaun did not touch anything at first.

"Which?"

Aereth pointed.

His finger shook.

The packet lay beneath a river-maintenance notice.

Vaun's clerk brought clean paper and a pair of narrow tongs from his case.

Rhyl watched Aereth watching them.

The man seemed fascinated by the care being taken with an object he had apparently handled several times without remembering all of them.

"Who else has touched it?" Vaun asked.

"Me," Aereth said.

Talia said, "Me. I put it in the tray after he left it on the breakfast table."

"Pellin," Rhyl added.

Vaun looked at him.

"According to Pellin."

Rhyl nodded.

"According to Pellin."

The judge turned the packet over.

The broken wax bore the council's river-office mark.

Nothing leapt from the paper. No hidden rune flared. No curse announced itself out of professional courtesy.

Vaun's clerk began noting the folds, the wax, the cord, the handwriting.

Damaris found herself relieved by the boredom of it.

Boredom meant somebody was still doing this properly.

"The courier," she said.

Rhyl nodded. "Pellin didn't know him."

"Ledger grey?"

"Yes."

"Badge?"

"He didn't mention one."

"Did you ask?"

"No."

Damaris looked at him.

Rhyl shrugged. "I asked what he remembered."

She smiled despite herself.

"Good."

He turned his hand on the bench.

Her little finger slipped between two of his.

Aereth noticed.

Again.

This time his mouth moved faintly.

Damaris refused to be embarrassed while an uncle was apparently losing arguments with his own memory.

She lasted nearly four seconds.

Then she pulled her hand back.

Rhyl's smile was unbearable.

"Do you have a receiving book?" he asked Talia.

"For the house? Of course."

"Couriers sign?"

"Usually."

"Usually?"

"If they're delivering to the steward's office. Personal packets sometimes go through the front porter. Council and commercial traffic goes in the book because Meris likes being able to prove exactly when everyone disappointed her."

Damaris said, "That is the most accurate description of my aunt's filing system I have ever heard."

Talia looked pleased.

Vaun said, "Bring it."

Talia left.

Aereth watched the sealed tincture bottle in Vaun's case.

"How long?"

"For the medicine?"

"Yes."

"The tribunal laboratory can give me a preliminary comparison by evening if nothing complicated appears. Longer if something does."

"And the powder?"

"Same."

Aereth nodded.

He looked toward the packet.

"I have a council session tomorrow."

Damaris turned sharply.

"No."

Aereth raised an eyebrow.

"You are not my physician."

"No, I'm the relative who has just listened to you describe remembering the same packet twice."

"The levy vote matters."

"So does not voting twice because you remember doing it once."

Rhyl coughed into his fist.

Aereth glared at him.

"Sorry."

He was not sorry.

Vaun said, "You needn't decide about tomorrow today."

Aereth looked at her.

"That sounded suspiciously reasonable."

"I practice."

Talia returned carrying a thick narrow ledger bound in brown leather.

She placed it on the cleared corner of the desk and opened to a ribbon.

"Three days ago."

The page was divided into morning and afternoon deliveries.

Coal. Wine. Two letters. A parcel from a tailor. Physician's tonic.

Then the council packet.

Damaris leaned over it.

The runner had signed in a quick angular hand.

**Eren Vale.**

Rhyl read it once.

"Know him?"

Talia shook her head.

Damaris did too.

The name meant nothing to her.

That was almost refreshing.

"Ledger runner registry will," she said.

Vaun looked at her.

"Can you query it without altering the record?"

"Yes."

"Can you query it without announcing this investigation to your family?"

Damaris's answer took longer.

Rhyl felt her go still beside him.

"No."

Aereth frowned. "Why not?"

"Because my cipher access is audited."

She looked at Vaun, not Aereth.

"If I search a bonded runner credential, the query will exist. Meris may not see it immediately, but the family can see that I used my access and what registry I touched."

Rhyl said nothing.

Damaris noticed that too.

She looked at him.

"You're allowed to have an opinion."

"I do."

"And?"

"You already know the cost."

"That isn't an opinion."

"Fine. My opinion is that if you do it, you should do it because you think it's worth doing, not because everyone in this room is looking at the girl with the useful family key."

Something warm and deeply inconvenient moved through her chest.

Aereth looked between them.

"Oh, that's going badly for both of you."

Damaris stared at him.

"You are not well enough to be funny."

"Apparently I remember being funny twice."

Even Vaun smiled.

Damaris put both palms on the desk.

The name waited beneath them.

Eren Vale.

A young man in good boots who smelled of cloves.

Maybe.

A green-stone ring.

Maybe.

A sealed packet Aereth remembered reading before it was opened.

That part, at least, had two witnesses now and one frightened participant.

"I'll query it," she said.

Vaun nodded once.

"Then we preserve the result and the audit trail."

Damaris looked at Rhyl.

"You can come with me."

"All right."

"You don't have to."

"I know."

That answer was becoming a problem.

They used the small writing room downstairs because it had a Ledger cipher plate set into the desk.

Meris's house had three.

Of course it did.

Damaris unlocked the brass cover with her personal token, pressed two fingers to the glass and waited while pale script rose beneath the surface.

Rhyl stood behind her shoulder.

Not close enough to read until she shifted aside.

She did.

"Search: bonded runners. Aurelith district. Eren Vale."

The plate dimmed.

A line of text appeared.

Then another.

Damaris read them twice.

"Well?" Rhyl asked.

"He exists."

"That's almost disappointing."

"Temporarily bonded runner credential. Issued six months ago. Commercial and council carriage."

She scrolled.

Then stopped.

"Suspended."

"When?"

"This morning."

Rhyl leaned closer.

"Why?"

Damaris read the administrative note.

"Duplicate sponsor seal."

Neither spoke for a moment.

"Meaning?" Rhyl asked.

"Meaning somebody found another credential sponsored under the same authorization seal. Could be clerical duplication. Could be fraud. Could be a legitimate replacement entered badly."

She looked at him.

"It means exactly what it says until we know more."

"You have been spending too much time with Vaun."

"I hate all of you."

"No, you don't."

She looked back at the plate before he could see how much she liked hearing that.

There was a route-history tab.

Damaris hesitated.

Rhyl saw it.

"More audit?"

"Same query session."

"Your choice."

She opened it.

Seven deliveries appeared beneath the credential.

Reedglass was the most recent.

The others meant little at first glance: a physician's cooperative, a bonded warehouse, a council annex, a private townhouse, a tribunal office.

Then Damaris reached the oldest line.

She stopped.

Rhyl read over her shoulder.

Neither of them needed to say the name aloud.

**Concord Academy of Aurelith.**

Four days before Silver Night.

Rhyl's hand settled on the back of her chair.

Damaris could hear the rain again.

"That doesn't tell us what he carried," he said.

"No."

"Or who received it."

"No."

"Or whether it had anything to do with Silver Night."

She turned her head.

"You really have been spending too much time with Vaun."

"Terrible influence."

Damaris copied the registry result onto clean paper, including the query number and audit mark.

Her handwriting remained steady.

She was proud of that.

Then she closed the route history and locked the cipher plate.

Somewhere in the Caladren system, a record now existed that Damaris Caladren-Ilyra had searched a suspended courier credential on a rainy Bloomtide afternoon.

Meris would eventually know.

Perhaps before dinner.

Damaris stood.

Rhyl was close enough that she had to tilt her head to look at him.

"Still think I should have done it?"

"You thought you should."

"Coward."

"Absolutely."

She kissed him.

Not long.

Not carefully either.

His hand caught her waist because otherwise she would have backed into the cipher desk.

For one bright moment the Academy, the courier, Aereth's memory and every watching House in Elaranth became somebody else's administrative disaster.

Then Damaris pulled away.

Rhyl looked pleasantly stunned.

"That," he said, "was not evidentiary discipline."

"Say another word and I'll deny it happened."

"Twice?"

She laughed despite herself.

Then they took the copied route history upstairs.

---

## The Dead Man's Seal

By the time Damaris and Rhyl returned upstairs, the rain had softened to a fine silver mist against the green glass.

Aereth was no longer at the window.

He sat at the writing desk with both hands around a cup he had forgotten to drink from. Talia had moved the council packet to the cleared side table under Judge Vaun's eye. Mireth stood near the washstand with the sealed tincture bottle in a padded case.

Damaris put the copied registry result in front of Vaun.

The judge read it once.

Then again.

Her finger stopped on the suspension note.

"Duplicate sponsor seal."

"This morning," Damaris said.

Vaun looked at the audit number, then at her.

"Your query is preserved?"

"Yes."

That was enough.

The judge turned to Rhyl.

"Seven deliveries?"

"Reedglass and six others."

"One Academy."

"Four days before Silver Night."

Aereth closed his eyes.

No one said what that might mean.

Vaun folded the copy and gave it to her clerk.

"Request the sponsor record. Not the route history yet."

Damaris frowned. "Why not both?"

"Because I want to know how the credential came into existence before we frighten every place it visited."

She glanced at the packet.

"And because whoever used it may still believe nobody has noticed."

That made the room quieter.

Talia looked toward the door as though the young courier might be standing outside it with another sealed packet.

Rhyl noticed.

"He's not in the house."

She gave him a look.

"You don't know that."

"No."

He did not add anything.

Talia relaxed anyway.

Mireth lifted the padded case.

"I should take this to the tribunal laboratory."

Aereth looked at the tincture.

"You trust them?"

Mireth paused.

It was a fair question now.

"I trust the method more if more than one person signs it."

Vaun nodded toward her clerk.

"He goes with you. Two samples from the bottle. One retained sealed. One tested."

Mireth's expression sharpened with interest.

"And the powder?"

"Same handling."

"Can I observe?"

"Yes."

That answer pleased her more than it should have.

Damaris knew because Mireth tried not to smile.

Caelen would have noticed too.

The thought came with such certainty that Damaris nearly laughed.

Instead she watched Mireth leave with the clerk and two evidence cases, looking very young beneath a field mantle that had already begun to acquire the permanent creases of work.

Aereth stared into his untouched tea.

"What do I do?"

Vaun looked at him.

"Today?"

"Yes."

"Eat. Sleep if you can. Do not sign anything important."

"That sounds remarkably like being declared incompetent."

"No. It sounds like being frightened and tired while an investigation is occurring around you."

Aereth's mouth tightened.

Damaris expected an argument.

He surprised her.

"All right."

Talia looked at him.

So did Meris, who had entered quietly enough that nobody but Damaris seemed to notice until she spoke.

"I will send your council office a postponement."

Aereth turned.

"Reason?"

"Family illness."

"Technically true."

"The best kind."

He almost smiled.

Meris came farther into the room.

Her gaze touched Damaris, then Rhyl, then the registry copy in Vaun's clerk's hand.

She knew.

Not the contents.

The query.

Damaris had expected the knowledge to feel like a trap closing.

Instead it felt like standing in an open doorway and waiting to see who moved first.

Meris said nothing.

Damaris loved her for that.

Which was irritating.

---

The sponsor record arrived before the laboratory result.

It came by court runner rather than River Ledger courier.

Nobody commented on the choice.

Vaun opened it in the Reedglass accounting room while Damaris, Rhyl and Meris stood around the long table.

The temporary credential for Eren Vale had been sponsored six months earlier under authorization seal **LR-18-44**.

The seal belonged to a freight assessor named Joren Tal.

Damaris read the next line.

Then read it again.

"He's dead."

Meris leaned closer.

Joren Tal had died eighteen months earlier after a winter fever.

His office had closed the credential authority attached to his name nine days after his death.

Rhyl looked at Vaun.

"But Vale's credential is six months old."

"Yes."

"So someone used a dead man's authorization."

Vaun tapped the page.

"Someone used his seal number."

Rhyl nodded.

He was learning.

Damaris turned the page.

There was a destruction receipt for Tal's physical sponsor stamp.

Date: seventeen months ago.

Witnessed by two clerks.

One name meant nothing to her.

The other did.

"Sera Pell."

Meris's eyebrows rose.

"Pell?"

"No relation to the gardener that I know of."

Damaris looked at the office designation.

Lower Reed Transfer Office, stores clerk.

Vaun took the page from her.

"Alive?"

Meris answered before Damaris could.

"Almost certainly."

"Almost?"

"I don't maintain a daily census of minor freight clerks."

"A regrettable failure."

Meris looked at her.

Vaun's face did not change.

Rhyl stared very hard at the table.

Damaris failed first.

A laugh escaped her.

Meris looked betrayed.

"You are enjoying this judge far too much."

"Immensely."

Vaun handed the sponsor record to her clerk.

"We speak to Sera Pell."

Rhyl straightened.

"Now?"

"Before the office closes."

"Do we tell her why?"

"We ask about Joren Tal's stamp."

"And if she lies?"

"Then she lies."

Rhyl waited.

Vaun looked at him.

"What?"

"I thought there would be more."

"There usually is after someone lies."

He accepted that.

Damaris reached for her coat.

Meris said, "No."

She stopped.

"Again?"

"Again."

"Why?"

Meris pointed at the registry copy.

"Because you have already put our family name into the audit trail today. Let the judge interview a freight clerk without arriving with a Caladren niece at her shoulder."

Damaris hated that the argument was good.

Rhyl saw it on her face.

"I'll go."

She looked at him.

"I know."

He came close enough that Meris's attention sharpened by a degree.

Damaris noticed.

Rhyl probably did too.

He still touched two fingers to the inside of Damaris's wrist.

Briefly.

Warm skin beneath her cuff.

"I'll come back."

The words were ordinary.

Her body treated them as something else entirely.

"You had better."

Meris waited until Rhyl and Vaun had gone.

Then she looked at Damaris.

"Oh, dear."

Damaris closed her eyes.

"Do not."

"I said two words."

"They were malicious words."

"They were experienced words."

Damaris turned toward the window.

Meris came beside her.

For several breaths they watched the rain on the canal.

"You queried the runner registry," Meris said.

There it was.

"Yes."

"And found something."

"Yes."

Meris waited.

Damaris felt the shape of the choice.

Her aunt had not ordered her to report. Had not asked what she had found. Had not reached for the family authority she could have used.

That restraint made the question harder, not easier.

"A credential problem," Damaris said.

Meris looked at her.

"How large?"

"Large enough that Vaun is following it."

"Silver Night?"

Damaris did not answer immediately.

Meris understood anyway.

"Possibly," she said.

"Possibly."

Her aunt nodded.

No demand followed.

Damaris looked at her.

"That's all?"

"Would you prefer interrogation?"

"No."

"Then stop looking disappointed."

Damaris laughed despite herself.

Meris's expression softened.

"I gave you access because I believed you could decide when to use it."

"And because you wanted to know what I would search."

"Both things can be true."

There it was.

Love and surveillance occupying the same sentence without cancelling each other.

Damaris looked back at the canal.

"I hate that answer."

"You are becoming an adult. You will hate many accurate answers."

Damaris leaned her shoulder against Meris's.

Her aunt accepted the contact without comment.

For a while they stood that way.

---

The laboratory result arrived near dusk.

Mireth brought it herself.

Her hair had escaped its pins in three places. There was a faint grey streak across one cuff where somebody had apparently introduced her to laboratory dust by force.

Damaris opened the door before the footman could.

"Well?"

Mireth handed the report to Vaun, who had returned ten minutes earlier with Rhyl and no Sera Pell.

The clerk had left work early to collect a child from a temple school.

They had an address.

They also had enough discipline not to turn collecting a child into an ambush.

Vaun read the laboratory report.

Aereth stood at the far end of the room.

He had changed clothes.

That small act of normality made Damaris's chest ache.

"The tincture," Vaun said, "contains the ingredients on its label in the expected proportions. No unexpected sedative. No mnemonic compound detected in the preliminary screen."

Aereth sat down.

Not dramatically.

His knees simply seemed to decide before the rest of him.

Damaris crossed the room.

He held up a hand.

She stopped.

"So it isn't the medicine."

Mireth answered carefully.

"The laboratory did not find an unexpected compound in that bottle."

Aereth gave her a tired look.

"You have been spending time with the judge too."

"Yes."

"Terrible."

"Apparently."

His mouth moved faintly.

Vaun turned to the second page.

The room changed before she spoke.

Damaris saw it in the judge's face.

"The grey powder contains refined mnemonic salt."

No one breathed for a moment.

Mireth looked at the floor.

Rhyl looked at Damaris.

Aereth looked at nobody.

Damaris remembered Ilyan beneath the oak.

Necessary.

The west tower.

Witness materials.

Memory salt.

She forced herself not to run ahead of what they had.

Vaun continued.

"It is compounded with ash stabilizer suitable for damp storage."

Rhyl said, "River warehouse?"

"Possibly. Cellar. Canal store. Apothecary chest. Any damp place where someone wanted the material to keep."

He nodded.

Aereth stared at his hands.

"Can that make someone remember something that didn't happen?"

Mireth looked at Vaun.

The judge answered.

"Mnemonic salt can be used in workings involving memory. The presence of it in the buried object does not tell me what working was attempted, whether it succeeded, or whether it caused what is happening to you."

Aereth's jaw tightened.

Damaris expected him to demand certainty.

He did not.

"But now there is something."

Vaun nodded.

"Yes."

That was enough to frighten him properly.

Damaris sat beside him.

This time he did not raise a hand.

Rhyl stood across the room with rain still darkening the shoulders of his coat.

She wanted him beside her too.

There was no sensible arrangement of furniture that solved that problem.

Mireth noticed.

Of course she did.

Her mouth twitched.

Damaris gave her a murderous look.

Mireth became fascinated by the laboratory report.

Vaun spread the sponsor record beside it.

A dead assessor's seal number.

A suspended courier credential.

Seven deliveries.

One to the Academy four days before Silver Night.

Mnemonic salt beneath Aereth's window.

A council packet he remembered opening twice.

Nothing in the room joined those facts for them.

They simply sat on the table.

Damaris looked at them until the urge to make them into one clean story became almost physical.

Then she remembered Aereth accusing Talia because a missing signet had demanded an explanation.

She let the story remain unfinished.

Vaun looked at the fading light.

"Tomorrow we speak to Sera Pell."

Rhyl nodded.

"And the other six deliveries?"

"Preserve them tonight. Quietly."

Damaris looked at the Academy line.

"All six?"

"All six."

"Without contacting the recipients?"

"For now."

The judge gathered the papers.

"I want the records before I want frightened people improving their memories for us."

Aereth leaned back in his chair.

"And me?"

Vaun looked at him.

"You stay here tonight."

"As a prisoner?"

"As a man whose family has excellent wine and enough spare bedrooms to survive one evening without governing the river."

Aereth considered.

"That is offensively persuasive."

"Good."

Meris called from the doorway, "The red is better than the white."

Aereth looked at her.

"Traitor."

"Family."

He laughed.

Quietly.

It was the first time Damaris had heard him laugh all day without anger inside it.

Outside, the rain finally stopped.

The canal held the last grey light between the houses.

Somewhere in Lower Reed, a freight clerk was collecting a child from school, unaware that a dead man's seal had placed her name on Judge Vaun's desk.

And somewhere else in Aurelith, six old delivery records were about to become very difficult to lose.

## What the Fire Ate

Sera Pell lived above a cooper's shop where Lower Reed narrowed toward the old lock stairs.

The morning smelled of wet oak, river mud, and yesterday's rain trapped in brick. Rhyl had been expecting an office. Judge Vaun had apparently expected breakfast.

She stopped at a street cart and bought two hot buns without interrupting her reading of the copied destruction receipt.

Rhyl watched her pay.

"Is that part of judicial procedure?"

"Yes."

"Which part?"

"Breakfast."

He accepted one of the buns.

It contained onion, sharp cheese, and enough pepper to make his eyes water pleasantly.

The cooper below Sera's rooms was already working. Each strike of his mallet traveled through the building with a dull wooden certainty. A child sat on the front step arranging barrel hoops into a pattern only she understood.

She looked up when Vaun and Rhyl approached.

"Mam said if you're from the school I have my slate."

Vaun crouched just enough to meet her eyes.

"We're not from the school."

The child considered this.

"Debt?"

"No."

"Temple?"

"No."

"Then she's upstairs."

Rhyl glanced at Vaun.

Vaun glanced at him.

Neither commented on the taxonomy.

Sera Pell opened the door before they knocked a second time.

She was perhaps fifty, broad through the shoulders, with dark hair braided hastily and a pencil tucked through it. She wore an office skirt under an old kitchen apron. One cuff had been buttoned and the other forgotten.

Her gaze went first to Vaun's tribunal pin, then to Rhyl's Red Hart clasp.

"Has something happened at the transfer office?"

"Not this morning," Vaun said. "May we come in?"

Sera looked behind them toward the street.

"Is this about my daughter?"

"No."

That answer changed her face more than any reassurance could have.

She stepped aside.

The rooms were small and meticulously used. A school satchel hung beside a freight clerk's coat. Three jars of preserves occupied the windowsill. Someone had repaired the back of a chair with packing cord and considerable confidence.

The child came in after them, carrying one barrel hoop.

"Nessa," Sera said.

"I'm quiet."

"You are visibly carrying part of Master Oren's stock."

Nessa looked at the hoop.

"He has lots."

"Downstairs."

The child sighed with the patience of someone burdened by unreasonable adults and departed.

Vaun waited until the door closed.

Then she put the copied receipt on the table.

Sera did not touch it.

She read the date first.

Then the name.

Her expression went still.

"Joren."

"You remember this?" Vaun asked.

"Of course I remember it."

No elaboration followed.

Vaun let the silence stand.

Sera pulled out a chair and sat.

"He'd been dead nearly a month. His widow kept finding office things in the house. Seals, old tariff cards, keys. Joren took work home even when he wasn't supposed to."

Rhyl leaned against the wall rather than taking the chair opposite her.

"The stamp was among them?"

"Two stamps. His ordinary assessment stamp and the sponsor stamp. The assessment stamp was cancelled and archived. Sponsor authority dies with the officer, so that one was destroyed."

"You saw it destroyed?"

Sera looked at him.

"I held the tongs."

Vaun's eyes lifted from the receipt.

"Tell me."

Sera rubbed her thumb against the edge of the table.

"Stores furnace. End of Forgeday. I remember because Nessa had fever and I wanted to go home. Pellis Arden was duty clerk. He checked the number. I checked the number. We struck the face with a cold chisel first. Four cuts. Then the stamp went into the furnace with three cancelled customs dies and a bridge inspector's plate."

"And you watched it melt?"

Sera frowned.

"No."

Rhyl felt the room narrow.

Vaun did not move.

"Why not?"

"Because that's not how stores disposal worked. We loaded the destruction tray, recorded the pieces, and gave the tray to the furnaceman. He signed the batch sheet after the melt."

"Who?"

Sera looked back at the receipt.

"I don't remember."

She said it without drama.

Then her face changed.

"Wait."

She stood and went to a narrow cupboard beside the stove. Bills, school papers, folded notices and household accounts came out in a disciplined stack.

"What are you looking for?" Rhyl asked.

"A complaint."

"About the furnace?"

"About my pay."

Vaun's mouth almost moved.

Sera found the paper.

"The office docked us half a shift that month because the stores furnace was out for repairs and we were sent home early twice."

Rhyl stopped chewing the last of his bun.

Vaun held out her hand.

Sera gave her the notice.

The date was two days after the destruction receipt.

"Out for repairs when?" Vaun asked.

"I don't know. The flue cracked sometime that week."

"Could it have been out on the day you put Tal's stamp on the destruction tray?"

Sera's face tightened.

"It could."

"Would that stop the receipt being signed?"

"It should have."

"Would it?"

Sera looked angry now, but not at Vaun.

"Apparently not."

Rhyl looked at the destruction receipt again.

A dead man's stamp had not climbed out of molten brass. It did not need to.

Someone only needed a tray nobody watched all the way to the furnace.

Vaun folded the pay notice beside the receipt.

"Pellis Arden. Where is he now?"

"North Quay customs. Unless he finally retired."

"And the furnaceman?"

"The batch sheet will have him."

"Where are the old sheets kept?"

"Municipal stores archive."

Sera stared at the papers between them.

"What was done with Joren's seal?"

Vaun regarded her for a moment.

"We don't know."

Sera looked relieved by the answer.

Rhyl understood why.

A lie would have meant Vaun had come to trap her. Certainty would have meant something terrible was already settled. Not knowing was frightening, but it left Sera outside the story rather than pinned inside it.

"Do I need a lawyer?" she asked.

"You may have one if you want one," Vaun said. "At present I need the destruction batch sheet and Pellis Arden."

Sera nodded.

Then she looked toward the door through which Nessa had vanished.

"Don't come to her school."

"I won't."

That was all.

---

At Reedglass, Damaris discovered that waiting was an occupation invented by cruel people.

She had three ledgers open, one letter half written, and no idea what any of them said.

Across the table, Meris was doing actual work.

This was offensive.

"You have read that freight summary four times," Meris said.

Damaris looked down.

"Have I?"

"You turned back to the first page after the third."

"Perhaps I admire the prose."

"Then your condition is worse than I feared."

Damaris threw a folded scrap of blotting paper at her.

Meris caught it without looking.

Aereth was asleep upstairs. Talia had bullied him into eating eggs, bread and stewed apples before allowing him to retreat. Mireth had gone to Green Covenant duty with the laboratory report copied under seal. The six preserved delivery records had arrived before breakfast and now sat locked in Vaun's evidence chest.

Damaris had been told not to open them without the judge.

She had obeyed.

She resented everyone for making obedience possible.

Meris finally closed her own ledger.

"You could go home."

"No."

"You could bathe."

Damaris looked at her sleeve.

There was still a faint mark where Rhyl's wet coat had brushed her yesterday.

"No."

Meris followed her gaze.

"Ah."

"I will have you thrown into the canal."

"Not in those shoes."

Damaris looked at her aunt.

Meris smiled with the serenity of a woman who had survived other people's young love before Damaris was born.

"How long?" she asked.

"How long what?"

"Since you decided he was no longer merely handsome."

Damaris opened her mouth.

Closed it.

The answer was embarrassingly difficult because there had never been a clean boundary. Rhyl had been Rhyl at the Academy: sun-browned when everyone else was pale from examinations, perpetually smelling faintly of cedar and cold air, easy to tease because he pretended not to notice teasing until the exact moment he chose to return it.

Then there had been his hand in hers after graduation.

His mouth.

The infuriating steadiness with which he let her make decisions he could easily have made for her.

"I don't know," she said.

Meris accepted that.

No lecture followed.

Damaris glanced at her.

"That's all?"

"You seem disappointed again."

"I am suspicious of restraint in this family."

"Sensibly."

The front door opened downstairs.

Damaris was out of her chair before she heard Rhyl's voice.

Meris looked down at her ledger and very carefully did not smile.

Damaris hated her.

Rhyl came into the accounting room with Vaun behind him and rainless morning air still clinging to his coat.

"Well?" Damaris asked.

He looked at Meris.

Then back at her.

"The stamp was put on a destruction tray. Sera saw it damaged first. She didn't see it melted."

Damaris's pulse quickened.

Vaun laid the pay notice beside the old receipt.

"The stores furnace was under repair that week. We are obtaining the destruction batch sheet and locating the duty clerk."

Meris rose.

"So the stamp may have survived."

"May have."

Damaris looked at Rhyl.

He was already looking at her.

She wanted to touch him.

Meris was standing three feet away.

That had stopped being a meaningful deterrent sometime yesterday.

Damaris crossed the space between them and put her hand flat against his chest.

Rhyl's eyebrows lifted.

"You came back."

"I said I would."

"Yes."

She kissed him.

It was not the first time and therefore should have been less catastrophic.

It was not.

His hand settled at her waist, warm and careful. The kiss lasted just long enough for Damaris to become aware of the exact number of people in the room and stop caring about any of them.

When she stepped back, Meris had become deeply interested in the destruction receipt.

Vaun had not looked up from her notes.

Damaris suspected both women of criminal levels of self-control.

Rhyl's thumb brushed once against her side before his hand fell away.

"Good morning," he said.

"It is now."

Meris made a small sound into her fist.

Damaris pointed at her without turning.

"Canal."

"Shoes," Meris reminded her.

---

The destruction batch sheet arrived shortly after midday.

Pellis Arden had signed as duty clerk.

The furnaceman's line was blank.

Not crossed through. Not marked absent. Blank.

Vaun laid the sheet beside the receipt.

"That should have failed audit," Damaris said.

Meris leaned over her shoulder.

"It did."

She pointed to a tiny notation in the lower corner.

**Corrected under Stores Variance 14-B.**

Damaris felt the hairs rise along her arms.

"Where is fourteen-B?"

Vaun's clerk was already moving.

The variance file came from the municipal archive an hour later.

It was thin.

Too thin.

Three destruction batches had been regularized after the furnace outage. Two had attached remelt certificates from the following week.

Joren Tal's batch did not.

Instead there was a single sentence in Pellis Arden's hand:

**Cancelled dies rendered unusable by prior mechanical defacement; remelt waived under stores discretion.**

Rhyl read it twice.

"Four chisel cuts counted as destroyed."

"For the record," Vaun said.

Damaris looked at the old receipt.

"Which means the stamp never had to reach the furnace."

Vaun nodded once.

That was as far as she went.

Pellis Arden had not retired.

He had been promoted.

North Quay Customs now listed him as deputy stores supervisor, with authority over bonded seals, confiscated dies, temporary credential blanks, and disposal certification.

Meris read the office description over Damaris's shoulder.

"Convenient."

"Maybe," Damaris said.

Her aunt looked at her.

Damaris met the look.

"I am learning."

"Painfully."

"From excellent teachers."

Vaun closed the file.

"We speak to Arden before we touch the six deliveries."

Rhyl nodded.

Damaris did not.

"Why?"

Vaun looked at her.

"Because if the seal was diverted through stores, the courier credential may be one use of a larger weakness. If we begin knocking on seven doors first, anyone who knows that weakness exists has time to close it."

Damaris thought of the Academy line.

Four days before Silver Night.

It sat in her mind like a hook.

She wanted to pull.

That was exactly why she did not.

"All right."

Vaun's attention lingered on her for half a second.

Approval from the judge was so small a thing that Damaris was irritated by how much she wanted it.

---

The Seven met that evening in the room above the tea shop where Lyrion had somehow convinced the proprietor that seven newly graduated nobles, order initiates and professional nuisances required privacy more than paying customers required the good upstairs table.

No one believed his explanation.

Everyone benefited from it.

Caelen arrived first with Mireth, carrying a garment box he had apparently been ordered not to crush. Thessa and Lyrion came together, arguing about whether a diplomatic reception could reasonably require three separate forms of footwear. Ilyan arrived last, pale from another day in the archive and carrying the certified oath extract under his coat.

Damaris watched them settle.

For a moment they looked almost like students again.

Then Caelen put his guard gloves beside the tea tray. Mireth had laboratory dust under one thumbnail. Thessa's Warden insignia was pinned correctly for once. Ilyan checked the door before sitting.

Not students.

Not anymore.

Rhyl's knee touched Damaris's beneath the table.

He left it there.

Lyrion noticed within seconds and had the decency to say nothing for almost a full minute.

"I am proud of you," he said at last.

Damaris kicked him under the table.

He looked wounded.

"Violence against diplomacy."

"An ancient tradition," Thessa said.

Caelen opened the garment box.

Inside lay a dark formal coat with silver-green embroidery at the collar.

"My cousin says if I arrive at Seven Lamps in field uniform my mother will interpret it as a declaration of war."

Mireth touched the embroidery.

"Your mother has excellent strategic instincts."

"You encouraged her."

"I like you alive."

The exchange required no explanation. Caelen smiled at her, and Mireth's fingers remained on the collar longer than cloth required.

Damaris looked away before they caught her watching.

Ilyan unfolded the certified extract.

"What happened at Reedglass?"

The room settled.

Damaris told them.

Not everything Vaun had sealed. Not Meris's private words. Not Aereth's fear in ways that would turn it into entertainment.

The buried powder had tested as mnemonic salt. Aereth's tincture had not shown an unexpected mnemonic compound. The runner credential used a dead freight assessor's sponsor number. The destruction record for the physical stamp had a hole large enough to walk through. Seven deliveries existed. One had gone to the Academy four days before Silver Night.

Ilyan went very still at that.

Mireth watched him.

"We don't know what was delivered."

"I know."

"We don't know it had anything to do with Silver Night."

"I know."

He sounded angry.

Not at her.

At the need to say it.

Damaris let him have the anger.

Thessa leaned forward.

"What do we do?"

"Nothing with the Academy delivery yet," Rhyl said. "Vaun is tracing the seal through stores."

"Nothing?" Caelen asked.

"We preserve what we have," Damaris said. "And we go to the reception tomorrow night."

Lyrion stared at her.

"You say that as though the second thing follows from the first."

"It does."

"I am delighted to hear the theory."

Damaris looked around the table.

"Seven Lamps will have half the people who can make records disappear and the other half who will notice if they do. We were already invited. If we cancel now, we announce that something frightened us."

Thessa's mouth curved.

"So we dress beautifully and pretend not to be hunting anyone."

"We are not hunting anyone," Ilyan said.

Thessa looked at him.

"Fine. We dress beautifully and pretend not to be terrified by administrative irregularities."

"Much better," Lyrion said.

Ilyan tried not to smile.

Failed.

The room eased.

Only a little.

Caelen poured tea.

"Do we tell our institutions?"

That was harder.

Mireth looked at the steam rising from her cup.

"The laboratory result is already tribunal record. My order will know the material was tested. They don't necessarily know why it matters to us."

Thessa said, "Avera knows we're looking at Silver Night generally. Not this."

Lyrion tapped one finger against his saucer.

"Silver Bough knows I am socially incapable of minding my own business. That is less useful than it sounds."

Rhyl looked at Damaris.

She knew what he was asking.

Meris.

"My family knows I queried the runner registry," she said. "My aunt knows there is a serious credential problem. She does not know the route details from me."

Ilyan nodded.

No accusation.

That mattered.

"And Valeric?" Caelen asked him.

Ilyan's face closed.

"Nothing."

The answer remained his.

No one pushed.

Outside, evening traffic rattled over the canal bridge. Someone below laughed too loudly. Cups clicked against saucers.

Ordinary sounds.

Damaris rested her hand under the table.

Rhyl found it without looking.

Tomorrow they would stand beneath the lamps in formal clothes while Houses assessed marriages, appointments, loyalties and weaknesses.

Somewhere before that, Judge Vaun intended to ask a promoted customs supervisor what had happened to a dead man's seal.

And six preserved delivery records waited in a locked chest.

The Seven had spent their last night at the Academy believing adulthood began at graduation.

Damaris was beginning to suspect adulthood was mostly discovering how many doors had already been open around you while you were still being told not to enter.

## The Man Who Kept the Keys

North Quay Customs had been designed by someone who believed a public office became honest if every corridor was narrow enough to make theft inconvenient.

The result was a building full of locked cabinets, iron grilles, damp wool, sealing wax and clerks forever turning sideways to let one another pass.

Rhyl disliked it immediately.

Judge Vaun seemed almost comfortable.

They arrived shortly after the midday bell, before the office had finished digesting lunch. A porter with mustard on his cuff took Vaun's tribunal card, looked at Rhyl's Red Hart clasp, and decided without asking that neither of them had come about tariffs.

"Deputy Arden is in stores inspection."

"We'll wait," Vaun said.

The porter hesitated. "That can take a while."

"Then we'll become older here."

Rhyl looked at her.

"Is patience also judicial procedure?"

"Occasionally."

They waited beside a barred counter while customs clerks moved boxes, manifests and little brass cylinders from one locked place to another. Rhyl watched hands. He had learned at the Academy that people lied with their faces and told the truth with their feet. The Red Hart had taught him that hands were worse.

The young clerk at the confiscation desk checked his key ring every time a supervisor passed. The woman sorting temporary credentials never touched the blank forms without counting them first. A grey-haired porter carried a broken seal press openly because nobody cared about a tool that could no longer make a clean impression.

Ordinary habits. Ordinary weaknesses.

Pellis Arden appeared twenty minutes later with ink on two fingers and irritation already prepared.

He was a compact man in his late seventies, neat except for a waistcoat buttoned one hole wrong. His eyes went to Vaun first.

"Judge."

Then to Rhyl.

"Hart."

"Rhyl."

Arden did not apologize.

"My office."

It was barely large enough for three chairs. Two walls were shelving. The third held a framed customs charter whose glass had cracked in one corner and never been replaced.

Vaun sat.

Rhyl remained standing until Arden looked annoyed by it, then sat too.

Vaun placed a copy of Joren Tal's destruction receipt on the desk.

Arden read it.

His expression did not change.

Then she placed the variance beneath it.

That did.

Not much. His eyes stopped moving.

"You wrote the variance."

"Apparently."

"Do you remember it?"

Arden leaned back.

"I remember the furnace failure. I remember three days of everyone behaving as though civilization had ended because stores could not melt scrap brass on schedule. I do not remember every die in the tray."

"Joren Tal's sponsor stamp."

"No."

Rhyl watched his hands.

Still on the desk. Not gripping anything.

Vaun said, "Sera Pell remembers four chisel cuts across the face."

"That would have been normal."

"And the remelt was waived."

"Also normal when a die was mechanically spoiled beyond usable impression."

"Was it?"

Arden's mouth tightened.

"At the time? Yes. Now? No. Procedures changed after the South Toll counterfeit case."

Vaun did not chase the name. "What happened to spoiled dies when remelt was waived?"

"Scrap cage. Then bulk sale by weight."

Rhyl felt the answer arrive before the implication did.

"Sold?"

Arden looked at him.

"Brass has value. We did not bury it ceremonially."

"Who could remove something before the sale?"

"Anyone with the cage key."

"Who had the cage key?"

Arden's irritation returned, but thinner.

"Duty clerk. Stores supervisor. Senior porter when countersigned."

Vaun folded her hands.

"You were duty clerk."

"Yes."

"Who was stores supervisor?"

"Mera Holt. Dead six years."

"Senior porter?"

"It changed by shift."

"Records?"

"Probably."

"Probably?"

Arden looked at the cracked charter glass rather than at her.

"The old key register was kept in the cage ledger."

"Where is it?"

He was quiet for long enough that Rhyl stopped watching his hands.

"It should be here."

Vaun waited.

Arden stood.

"Come with me."

---

The old scrap cage had become a stationery room.

That seemed to offend Vaun more than anything else so far.

The iron door remained. The bars remained. Inside, shelves now held string, ink, blank manifests, sealing wax and enough paper to prosecute a small kingdom.

Arden unlocked a cabinet in the back.

Three ledgers lay inside.

He took out the first, opened it, then the second.

The third was newer than the period they needed.

Arden stared at the empty space beneath it.

"No."

It was the first thing he had said that sounded frightened.

Vaun stepped closer but did not touch the shelf.

"Which volume?"

"Two-eighty through two-seventy-nine. Key issue, scrap weight, sale lots, disposal exceptions."

"Missing since when?"

"I don't know."

Rhyl looked around the little room.

"Would someone know it was missing?"

Arden rubbed both hands over his face.

"We stopped using the cage ledger after the office reorganization. The old books were boxed. When I became deputy I had them brought back because nobody could answer questions about inherited seal stock."

"When?"

"Nine years ago."

"You saw this volume then?"

"Yes."

"Since?"

Arden looked at him.

"I have used it."

"How recently?"

"Last winter. Maybe early Thawcall."

Vaun said, "For what?"

"A disputed bridge inspector's die."

"Who knew you used it?"

Arden gave a humorless laugh.

"Everyone who heard me complain about climbing three flights to find it."

Rhyl almost smiled.

Almost.

Vaun examined the cabinet lock without touching it.

"Forced?"

"No."

"Keys?"

Arden's gaze moved to the ring at his belt.

"Mine. Supervisor's. Archive master."

"And copies?"

"Not officially."

There it was again. The small gap between a system and the people who lived inside it.

Vaun turned to Rhyl.

"Door."

He understood.

He stepped into the corridor and stopped the first clerk approaching with an armful of paper.

"Stationery can wait."

The clerk looked at the room, at Vaun, at Arden's face, and decided it could wait indefinitely.

Arden said, "Judge, if you seal this room you'll stop half the quay by morning."

"Then we work quickly."

"That isn't an answer."

"It is the answer you have."

He did not like it.

He did not argue again.

---

They found the ledger before sunset.

Not in the cabinet.

In the archive return room downstairs, shelved under bridge assessments because someone had written **BRIDGE DIE — HOLLEN DISPUTE** across a temporary wrapper and a junior clerk had filed the whole volume by the wrapper rather than by the book inside it.

Rhyl laughed when they found it.

He could not help himself.

Arden sat down on a crate.

Vaun closed her eyes for one second.

"Do not," she said.

"I didn't say anything."

"Your face did."

The missing ledger had not been stolen.

That mattered.

So did what was inside it.

Joren Tal's destruction batch appeared exactly where it should. Four mechanically defaced dies. Remelt waived. Scrap cage transfer recorded the next morning.

The sale lot was entered six days later.

Weight expected: eleven pounds, four ounces.

Weight sold: ten pounds, nine ounces.

Rhyl leaned over the page.

"Eleven ounces short."

Arden shook his head.

"That could be scale error. Dirt. Wax. A misweighed plate."

"Could a sponsor stamp weigh eleven ounces?"

"Some."

Vaun looked at him.

"Could Tal's?"

Arden swallowed.

"I don't know."

The ledger had three key issues between transfer and sale.

Pellis Arden.

Mera Holt.

And a senior porter named **Cevan Rusk**.

No one said anything for a while.

Then Vaun pointed to the sale line.

"Who certified the weight?"

Arden read the initials.

His shoulders lowered.

"Rusk."

"Where is he now?"

"River Gate bonded warehouse. Night superintendent."

Rhyl looked at Vaun.

She was already copying the entry.

Arden said, "You think he took it."

Vaun did not look up.

"No."

"Then why—"

"Because he had a key and certified a short lot. Those are questions."

Arden's jaw worked.

"I had a key."

"Yes."

"Mera had a key."

"Yes."

"And she's dead."

Vaun finally looked at him.

"Which makes her harder to interview."

Rhyl coughed into his hand.

Arden stared at the judge.

Then, despite himself, laughed once.

The tension in the room changed shape.

Not gone.

Usable.

---

Damaris spent the afternoon being fitted for a reception gown while thinking about counterfeit credentials.

There were, she decided, cruelties too refined for criminal law.

The dressmaker had come to Reedglass because Meris refused to let Damaris vanish into the city when Vaun might return. Three assistants occupied the morning room with pins, chalk, silk and opinions.

Damaris stood on a low platform in stockinged feet while pale river-blue fabric was draped across her hips.

"Don't breathe," said the eldest assistant.

"I require that for several ongoing obligations."

"Not for ten seconds."

Meris sat by the window drinking tea.

"Try to cooperate."

"You brought them here."

"Because last time you chose formal clothes without supervision you wore mourning silver to a harvest arbitration."

"We won."

"The barley delegation thought someone had died."

"Their tariff proposal did."

The dressmaker made a sound suspiciously like a swallowed laugh.

Damaris lifted her arms while the bodice was pinned.

She had slept badly. Aereth had slept better. Talia reported no new missing time that morning, which Damaris had carefully not transformed into hope.

Hope was greedy. It liked to eat one quiet morning and call itself a cure.

A knock came at the door.

Meris looked up.

Damaris did not.

"If that is Rhyl, let him in."

The youngest assistant dropped a pin.

Meris's eyebrows rose.

"You are half dressed."

"I am covered from collarbone to ankle."

"In pins."

"He has seen worse wilderness injuries."

"That was not my concern."

Damaris turned her head.

Meris's expression was composed.

The dressmaker's was not.

"Oh," Damaris said.

Meris sipped her tea.

"Age does eventually improve pattern recognition."

It was not Rhyl.

It was Ilyan.

Damaris's disappointment was immediate enough that the dressmaker finally laughed aloud.

Ilyan stopped in the doorway.

"Should I come back?"

"No," Damaris said. "Unless you have become scandalized by shoulders."

"I survived the Academy baths."

"Then enter."

He did, carefully, as though the carpet might contain legal traps.

He carried a folded paper.

Damaris noticed that before his face.

"What happened?"

Ilyan handed it to Meris rather than trying to reach around three assistants and six yards of silk.

"The Hall of Veils answered my request about the restricted signatory."

The room changed.

The dressmaker looked from one face to another.

Meris said, "Ladies, give us five minutes."

Nobody argued.

The door closed behind the assistants.

Damaris stepped down from the platform with half the skirt pinned and took the paper.

The response was short.

The personal signatory on Revision Seventeen remained restricted under internal seal.

But the restriction authority had been renewed twice since Silver Night.

The most recent renewal was not Valeric.

It was **Dmitrigical Review**.

Damaris read the line again.

"Does this tell us who signed the revision?"

"No."

"Does it tell us Dmitrigical Review wrote it?"

"No."

"Then what does it tell us?"

Ilyan looked exhausted.

"That someone in Dmitrigical Review still cared enough about the identity of the signatory to renew the seal after the massacre."

Meris put down her tea.

"Or cared about the category of record."

Ilyan nodded.

"Yes."

Damaris looked at him.

He was getting better at that.

Not certainty.

Room for another explanation.

It frightened her more than certainty would have.

"Who can renew it?"

"A review chair, tribunal liaison, or delegated seal officer. The renewal record itself is not restricted. I requested it."

"When?"

"Tomorrow morning."

The reception was tomorrow night.

Damaris looked down at the unfinished river-blue skirt around her legs.

"Good."

Ilyan's eyes moved to the dress.

"Is that for Seven Lamps?"

"Apparently I am forbidden to attend naked."

Meris said, "By several overlapping bodies of law."

"Oppressive government."

Ilyan smiled.

A real one.

It disappeared quickly, but Damaris saw it.

That mattered too.

---

Rhyl returned near dusk.

This time Damaris did not run downstairs.

She made it as far as the landing before deciding the distinction was meaningless.

He looked up and stopped.

The gown was finished enough to be dangerous.

River-blue silk followed her body without the enormous court skirts favored by women who wished to occupy both a ballroom and neighboring territory. Silver thread ran in narrow lines at the waist and cuffs. Her hair was still loose because the dressmaker had threatened to return tomorrow with combs.

Rhyl stared.

Damaris rested one hand on the banister.

"You appear to have forgotten language."

"Temporarily."

"Promising."

He came up two steps.

Then stopped again, because Meris was visible through the open morning-room door.

Meris looked at him.

"If you make her late tomorrow, I will have you hunted."

Rhyl considered this.

"By whom?"

"Your family."

He laughed.

Damaris descended the remaining steps and kissed his cheek.

"What did you find?"

His attention returned reluctantly to work.

"The ledger wasn't stolen. It was misfiled."

"Of course it was."

"The scrap lot was eleven ounces short. Three people had keys. Arden, his supervisor, and a porter named Cevan Rusk. Rusk certified the sale weight."

"Where is he?"

"River Gate bonded warehouse."

"Tonight?"

Rhyl shook his head.

"Vaun wants the original scale inspection and sale docket first. If the scales were running light, eleven ounces means nothing."

Damaris leaned against him for one quiet moment.

He put an arm around her.

No urgency. No performance.

Just warmth.

"I hate sensible people," she murmured.

"You keep collecting them."

"A character defect."

His mouth touched her hair.

Meris, from the other room, said, "The door remains open."

Damaris did not move.

"I know."

"Good."

That was all.

The older woman went back to her accounts.

Rhyl's arm tightened slightly.

"Your aunt is terrifying."

"You noticed."

"I have excellent field instincts."

Damaris smiled against his coat.

Tomorrow they would go beneath the Seven Lamps in silk and formal coats and pretend the city was not narrowing around a dead man's seal, a sealed name, a missing eleven ounces of brass and a delivery made to their Academy four days before almost everyone they knew died.

Tonight, for a little while, Rhyl was simply holding her.

She let that be enough.

## Eleven Ounces

The scale had been condemned for honesty.

Rhyl learned this shortly after breakfast, standing in a municipal weights office that smelled of brass, lamp oil and yesterday's onions while Judge Vaun read a calibration sheet twice.

The inspector across the counter was an elderly woman with three silver rings through one ear and the patient expression of someone who had spent most of her life explaining fractions to merchants.

"It ran heavy," she said.

Vaun looked up. "How heavy?"

"At ten pounds? Between an ounce and an ounce and a half, depending on temperature."

Rhyl leaned against the counter. "So a lot recorded at ten pounds nine—"

"May actually have been lighter."

"Not heavier."

"Correct."

Vaun slid the old sale docket beside the calibration sheet.

The inspector read the date and frowned.

"This was before repair."

"Yes."

"Then your eleven ounces do not go away."

Rhyl had not expected them to.

He had nevertheless hoped bureaucracy might, for once, solve a problem by being bad at arithmetic.

Vaun tapped the inspector's signature at the bottom of the calibration record.

"You performed this inspection?"

"I did."

"Do you remember the scale?"

"I remember swearing at it."

That seemed sufficient identification.

The inspector disappeared into the back and returned with a narrow bound register. Its pages contained test weights, temperatures, repairs and the names of people who had objected to the laws of mass when those laws became financially inconvenient.

The customs scale had failed three inspections in two months. It had never once read light.

Vaun copied the relevant entries.

Rhyl looked through the office window at carts grinding over the wet street.

"So the lot was at least eleven ounces short."

"At least," Vaun said.

The inspector corrected him without looking up.

"Probably twelve."

Rhyl sighed.

"Of course it was."

---

Cevan Rusk worked nights and slept badly during the day.

They found him in a room above a cooper's shop near River Gate, wearing trousers, one sock and the expression of a man considering whether judicial authority justified murder before noon.

He was broad through the shoulders, greying at the temples, and old enough to have stopped pretending that being awakened pleasantly was a moral obligation.

Vaun showed him her tribunal card.

Rusk stared at it.

"Can I put on the other sock?"

"Please."

He did.

His room was clean. A narrow bed, washstand, two chairs, a shelf of cheap adventure novels and a clay pot containing a plant that had died despite apparently heroic intervention.

Rhyl remained near the door.

Rusk sat on the bed and rubbed his face.

"This about North Quay?"

Vaun's eyes sharpened slightly.

"Why would you think that?"

"Pellis sent a boy last night asking whether I still had my old stores notebooks."

"Do you?"

"One. Maybe two."

He reached beneath the bed and pulled out a wooden box.

Vaun stopped him before he opened it.

"Leave it there."

Rusk looked at her, then at Rhyl.

"You people make ordinary objects exhausting."

"Occupational hazard," Rhyl said.

Vaun asked about Joren Tal's disposal lot.

Rusk remembered neither Tal nor the stamp. He remembered the furnace failure, the scrap cage and the sale because the buyer had complained about mixed brass.

"He wanted clean casting stock. We sold scrap. He behaved as if this were treachery."

"You certified the weight."

"If my initials are there."

"They are."

"Then I did."

"The lot was short."

Rusk's irritation faded.

"How short?"

"At least eleven ounces."

He stared at her.

"No."

Not defensive. Immediate.

Vaun waited.

Rusk stood, crossed to the washstand, then seemed to remember he had nowhere useful to go and came back.

"We weighed cage lots twice. Intake and sale. If they disagreed by more than two ounces, you opened the lot and counted pieces."

"The ledger shows no recount."

"Then the ledger is wrong."

"Or the procedure wasn't followed."

Rusk looked offended in a way Rhyl recognized from craftsmen accused of poor work.

"I followed it."

"Do you remember doing so?"

Silence.

Rusk's certainty weakened.

"No."

Vaun did not fill the space for him.

He looked toward the box beneath the bed.

"My notebook."

This time Vaun let him retrieve it after noting where it had been kept.

The book was pocket-sized, warped from old damp, filled with shifts, overtime, wagon numbers and terse complaints about other people's competence.

Rusk turned pages slowly.

"What date?"

Vaun told him.

His finger stopped.

There were four lines.

**Scrap 6. Holt says sell.**

**Scale quarrel again.**

**Boy from river office collected one spoiled sponsor die for pattern comparison — chit in cage envelope.**

And beneath it:

**Reweigh after removal.**

Nobody spoke.

Rusk read it again.

"There."

Vaun said, "What river office?"

"I don't know."

"Who was the boy?"

"I don't know."

"Did you see him?"

Rusk closed his eyes.

"Maybe. It was eighteen years ago."

Rhyl looked at the dead plant because looking at Rusk felt unfair.

Vaun asked, "Pattern comparison for what?"

"We used damaged dies to compare suspected impressions. Customs, toll offices, River Ledger, bridge inspectors. If somebody had a questionable seal, they'd sometimes borrow a spoiled die rather than move an active one."

"Was that permitted?"

Rusk made a face.

"With a chit."

"Where would the chit be?"

"Cage envelope. Then monthly attachments went to stores accounts."

Vaun's pencil paused.

"Not the cage ledger."

"No."

Rhyl pushed away from the door.

"So the missing weight may have been recorded somewhere else the whole time."

Rusk looked at him.

"If the attachment survived."

There was the problem.

There was always another room full of paper.

---

The Hall of Veils gave Ilyan the renewal record at eleven.

No argument. No dramatic refusal. No masked archivist emerging from shadow.

A clerk checked his request number, fetched the folio and asked him not to drip rain on it.

Ilyan almost wished for opposition. Opposition was easier to understand than cooperation from an institution his family might have bent years before he knew enough to notice.

The renewal had been entered six months after Silver Night and renewed again the following year.

The authorizing office was Dmitrigical Review.

The delegated seal officer was named.

**Teren Valeric.**

Ilyan read it once.

Then again.

His uncle.

The man who had congratulated him for understanding necessity.

For a few seconds the archive disappeared around the edges.

Not literally. Shelves remained shelves. The clerk continued scratching notes at the desk. Rain ticked against the high windows.

Ilyan put both hands flat on the table.

He knew what the record said.

He knew what it did not say.

His uncle had renewed the restriction on the identity of Revision Seventeen's signatory after Silver Night.

That was enough for the morning.

He requested a certified copy.

The clerk glanced at the name, then at Ilyan.

If she recognized the relationship, she was polite enough not to show it.

"Twenty minutes."

"Thank you."

He sat down to wait.

His hands had begun to shake.

He tucked them beneath the table.

---

Damaris found him there.

She had come because he was late.

Not very late. Twelve minutes. Ilyan had spent six years being early to everything, so twelve minutes was practically a distress flare.

She sat opposite him without asking.

He slid the uncertified reading copy across the table.

Her eyes reached the name.

She stopped.

"Ah."

It was exactly the right thing to say.

No recital of what his uncle had done. No reminder of the oak. No careful explanation of why this hurt.

Ilyan looked toward the rain.

"I thought I was prepared to see another Valeric name."

Damaris rested her forearms on the table.

"You weren't."

"No."

"All right."

He laughed once, quietly and without humor.

"That's your advice?"

"At the moment."

The clerk brought the certified copy.

Ilyan thanked her. Damaris waited until they were outside beneath the stone arcade before touching him.

One hand between his shoulder blades.

Nothing more.

"Do the others know you're here?" he asked.

"Rhyl knows I was coming to find you. Mireth thinks I'm collecting ribbons."

"Are you?"

"Eventually. Apparently my hair requires engineering."

That got another laugh from him, a better one.

They walked toward Reedglass beneath Damaris's umbrella.

Halfway there, Ilyan said, "I want to confront him."

"Today?"

"Yes."

"Will he answer?"

Ilyan considered it.

"Probably not."

"Will he know what you found?"

"Yes."

She let that sit between them.

"Then decide which thing you want more."

He hated the question because it was useful.

By the next corner, he had his answer.

"The record first."

Damaris nodded.

They kept walking.

---

The stores accounts attachment survived.

Vaun sent word to Reedglass shortly before the afternoon bell.

Rhyl arrived twenty minutes later with rain in his hair and a copy tucked beneath his coat.

Damaris met him in the entrance hall.

She had ribbons pinned through half her hair and one silver comb sticking out at an angle that suggested violence had occurred.

Rhyl looked at it.

"Should I ask?"

"No."

He kissed her forehead carefully, avoiding the architecture.

"Good."

Ilyan was in the morning room with Meris. Mireth and Caelen had arrived from their own preparations; Thessa and Lyrion were expected before supper.

Rhyl put the copied attachment on the table.

The old chit was brief.

A spoiled sponsor die had been released from North Quay stores on temporary comparison loan.

The receiving office was not merely "river office."

It was **River Ledger Credential Review**.

The borrower had signed with an employee number rather than a name.

Damaris leaned over the copy.

"I can resolve that."

Meris, from the window, said nothing.

Damaris looked at her aunt anyway.

Meris raised one eyebrow.

"Your cipher. Your audit trail."

There was no lecture attached to it.

Damaris drew the little River Ledger tablet from her writing case.

The employee number returned a personnel record almost immediately.

The screen filled with an old appointment line.

**Mara Venn — temporary credential examiner.**

Talia's surname.

Damaris went very still.

Rhyl read over her shoulder.

Nobody said Talia's name.

They did not need to.

Below the appointment was an end date.

Mara Venn had left River Ledger service seventeen years ago.

One year after borrowing Joren Tal's damaged sponsor die.

Damaris closed the tablet.

"We speak to Talia before anyone else."

Meris nodded.

Ilyan folded his uncle's certified renewal record once, very precisely.

Outside, rain moved across the Reedglass windows in silver threads.

Tomorrow night the Hall of Seven Lamps would be full of people smiling beneath flowers.

For the moment, they had two names.

One belonged to Ilyan's uncle.

The other belonged to Talia's family.

Neither was yet an answer.

---

## Mara Venn

Talia was mending a cuff when Damaris found her.

Not because the household lacked seamstresses. Talia had simply reached the age at which watching someone else repair a thing she could repair herself felt inefficient.

She sat near the east-room window with Aereth's dark house coat across her lap and a little wooden box of thread beside her. Rain silvered the glass behind her. Aereth slept in the adjoining room, properly this time, after a night without another vanished hour.

Damaris stopped in the doorway.

Talia looked up.

Her needle paused.

"What?"

There was no useful gentle route into it.

"Do you know a Mara Venn?"

The needle slipped from Talia's fingers.

It landed on the coat without making a sound.

Damaris closed the door behind her.

Talia stared at the black wool in her lap.

"My mother."

Damaris had prepared herself for cousin, aunt, elder sister, no relation at all.

Not that.

She crossed the room and sat opposite her.

"Is she alive?"

"Yes."

"Where?"

"Southbank. Near the old ferry stairs."

Talia picked up the needle but did not resume sewing.

"Why are you asking me about my mother?"

Damaris took out the copied stores chit.

She did not hand it over immediately.

"Eighteen years ago she worked for River Ledger Credential Review."

"I know."

"She borrowed a damaged sponsor die from North Quay stores. Joren Tal's."

Talia's face changed.

Not recognition.

Calculation.

"The dead assessor?"

Damaris nodded.

"The same seal number that appears on Eren Vale's credential."

Talia looked toward Aereth's closed door.

"Does Judge Vaun know?"

"She knows the record. I asked her to give me enough time to speak to you first."

"Why?"

"Because you're here. Because you've been here through all of this. Because I didn't want a tribunal officer arriving to ask whether your mother stole a seal before you knew why her name had appeared."

Talia's mouth tightened.

"Did she?"

"I don't know."

That answer seemed to hurt less than a reassurance would have.

Talia set the coat aside.

"She hated that job."

"Why?"

"She said everyone wanted credentials issued quickly until something went wrong, and then suddenly every clerk was expected to remember which thumb touched which piece of brass six years earlier."

Damaris almost smiled.

"That sounds plausible."

"She left when I was small."

"You remember that?"

"I remember her coming home angry. I remember the blue coat. I remember being told not to touch the wax in her work case."

Damaris's attention sharpened.

"Work case?"

Talia noticed.

"Don't."

"I'm not doing anything."

"Your face is."

Damaris leaned back.

Fair.

Talia rubbed both hands over her skirt.

"She brought things home sometimes. Papers. Impression sheets. Never active seals, as far as I know. She worked late and I was a child."

"Did she keep anything from the job?"

"A box."

The room became very quiet.

"What kind of box?"

"Tin. Green paint. She keeps old employment papers in it. Letters, pay chits, certificates."

"Have you seen it recently?"

"Last winter."

"Where?"

"Her house."

Damaris breathed out slowly.

Talia's eyes narrowed.

"You're going there."

"Vaun will want to."

"I'm coming."

"Talia—"

"No."

It was not loud.

Damaris stopped.

Talia picked the coat up again, folded it once and set it carefully on the chair beside her.

"If you are going to my mother's house because something she did eighteen years ago may be tied to what happened here, I am coming."

Damaris considered telling her it might be easier if she did not.

It probably would be.

That was not the same as having the right to decide for her.

"All right."

Talia looked faintly surprised.

"That easily?"

"I am trying a new and difficult practice called not ordering competent adults around when I become frightened."

"How is it going?"

"Poorly."

Talia laughed despite herself.

The sound reached the adjoining room.

Aereth stirred but did not wake.

For a moment both women looked toward the door.

Then Talia said, "Give me ten minutes."

---

Mara Venn lived above a stationer's shop that sold paper by the sheet and ink in little square bottles.

The old ferry stairs were visible at the end of the lane, descending into brown river water beneath a line of dripping willows.

Vaun arrived separately. Rhyl came with her. Damaris had expected that and was still absurdly pleased when she saw him.

He kissed her cheek beneath the awning.

Talia pretended not to notice.

Vaun did not bother pretending.

"Mrs. Venn knows we're coming?"

Talia nodded.

"I told her a judge wanted to ask about River Ledger."

"How did she react?"

"She said she had retired specifically so nobody would ever say that sentence to her again."

Rhyl looked at Damaris.

"I like her already."

"You like difficult women."

"Evidence suggests that."

Talia looked between them.

"You two are exhausting."

Damaris smiled.

"Apparently that's contagious."

Mara opened the upstairs door before they knocked.

She was smaller than Talia, with the same dark eyes and none of her daughter's instinct for hiding irritation. Her hair was iron grey and cut bluntly at the jaw. She looked first at Talia, then at Vaun's tribunal clasp, then at Damaris.

"Caladren."

"Ilyra branch."

"Worse. Come in."

The sitting room was warm and smelled of tea, paper and beeswax. Books filled one wall. A half-finished puzzle occupied the dining table.

Mara pointed Vaun toward a chair.

"If this is about something I certified eighteen years ago, I reserve the right to complain about your filing practices."

"It is about something you borrowed."

That stopped her.

Vaun placed the copied chit on the table.

Mara read it standing.

Her eyes moved once across the employee number.

"Yes."

Talia stared at her.

Mara sat down.

"I remember this."

Vaun said, "Tell me."

Mara looked at her daughter first.

"We had a run of counterfeit bonded-runner credentials. Not good ones. Good enough. The impressions had damage marks that matched retired or spoiled dies."

Damaris felt Rhyl become still beside her.

"Someone was copying damaged seals?"

"We thought someone was using the damage as camouflage. An active sponsor die makes a clean impression. A copied die is never perfect. But if you copy one already chipped, scratched or cancelled, defects stop looking suspicious."

Vaun said, "Tal's die?"

"One of several I borrowed for comparison."

"Did you return it?"

Mara frowned.

"Yes."

"You are certain?"

"I signed the return."

"Do you have a copy?"

"Possibly."

Talia said, "The green box?"

Mara looked at her.

"You remember that?"

"You told me not to touch it approximately every third day of my childhood."

"You touched everything."

"I was six."

"You were industrious."

Mara stood and went into the bedroom.

Talia closed her eyes.

"This is going better than expected."

Rhyl murmured, "What were you expecting?"

"Fire."

Mara returned carrying a green-painted tin box.

She put it on the table and opened it.

Inside were employment certificates, old pay statements, two faded ribbons, a child's drawing Talia immediately tried to hide, and a packet tied with string.

Mara untied it.

The return chit was there.

**J. Tal sponsor die — returned North Quay Stores. Received: M. Holt.**

Mera Holt.

The dead supervisor who had held one of the scrap-cage keys.

Vaun copied the chit.

"Why did you keep this?"

Mara's expression hardened.

"Because six months later someone accused our office of losing a borrowed customs die. Not Tal's. Another one. It turned up in the wrong drawer after three days, but by then I'd learned that a clerk without paper is simply the nearest available culprit."

Damaris thought of Pellin. Of Talia. Of every servant and clerk who had become suspicious merely by being close enough to touch something.

"The counterfeit runner credentials," she said. "What happened to that investigation?"

Mara looked at her.

"Closed."

"Solved?"

"No. Closed."

Vaun's pencil stopped.

"By whom?"

"Credential Review sent it upward because the counterfeit impressions crossed offices. River Ledger, customs, two bridge authorities. We were told a joint review had assumed jurisdiction."

"Which joint review?"

Mara's gaze moved toward Talia again.

"I don't remember the full title. Something to do with order liaison."

Damaris and Rhyl looked at each other.

Vaun asked, "Dmitrigical Review?"

Mara's face went blank with effort.

"Maybe."

"Maybe is not yes."

"Then maybe. It was eighteen years ago."

Good.

No convenient recovered certainty.

Vaun asked, "Do you have the closure notice?"

Mara searched the packet.

She did not.

She had a note to herself instead.

**Case transferred. Do not pursue duplicate-damage set without authorization. Ask Sel about missing comparison sheet.**

"Who's Sel?" Rhyl asked.

Mara stared at the handwriting.

"Selwyn Ar. No. Arven. Selwyn Arven. He was senior examiner."

"Alive?"

"I don't know."

"What missing comparison sheet?"

"I don't remember."

Talia touched her mother's wrist.

"Don't guess."

Mara looked at her daughter.

Something softened.

"No. I don't remember."

Vaun closed her notebook.

"That's enough for today."

Mara looked surprised.

"It is?"

"Yes."

"You don't want the box?"

"I want copies of the relevant records. You may keep your daughter's drawing."

Talia made a wounded sound.

Mara smiled for the first time.

"Absolutely not. Tribunal evidence now."

"Mother."

Rhyl looked at the ceiling.

Damaris bit the inside of her cheek.

Even Vaun failed to remain entirely expressionless.

---

They left Mara's house with three copied records and one new name.

Talia stayed behind for tea.

Damaris was glad.

The rain had stopped. Water ran along the gutters toward the river, carrying petals, straw and the city's smaller failures downhill.

Rhyl walked close enough that their sleeves touched.

"You were right to tell her first," he said.

"Talia?"

"Yes."

Damaris glanced back at the upper window. Mother and daughter moved behind the glass, indistinct through the rain.

"I nearly didn't."

"But you did."

She took his hand.

Ahead of them, Vaun was already reading the copied return chit again.

Mera Holt had received Tal's damaged die back into North Quay custody.

That did not explain how the later scrap sale lost weight.

It did not explain Eren Vale.

It did not explain the Academy delivery.

But Mara Venn was no longer a blank space in an old personnel register. She had borrowed the die for a legitimate counterfeit investigation, documented its return, and been ordered away from a broader duplicate-damage case after it crossed several offices.

The shape was becoming larger without becoming clearer.

Damaris disliked that.

She disliked something else more.

"Ilyan."

Rhyl's thumb moved across her knuckles.

"Still at Reedglass."

"Good."

"He wants to speak to Teren."

"I know."

"Will he wait?"

Damaris looked at him.

"Until tonight, probably."

The Hall of Seven Lamps reception waited at the end of the day.

Teren Valeric might be there.

So might half the institutional world that had spent the last eighteen years filing papers over one another until nobody could tell which layer had been meant to hide something and which had merely been Tuesday.

Damaris tightened her grip on Rhyl's hand.

"Then we'd better get dressed."

---

## Seven Lamps

By the time Damaris decided the silver pins were trying to kill her, Meris had already dismissed two arrangements and was considering a third.

"Hold still."

"I am holding still."

"You are threatening the mirror."

"The mirror started it."

Meris's mouth twitched.

The dressing room at Reedglass had been turned inside out. Silk lay across the bed in folds of green and smoke-grey. A maid stood behind Damaris with three pins between her lips and the careful expression of someone employed by a family old enough to consider stabbing an heiress in the scalp a routine professional hazard.

Rain had finally stopped. The windows stood open to wet leaves and the evening bells.

Damaris looked at herself again.

The gown was not the one she would have chosen.

That was why Meris had chosen it.

It was dark green until she moved, when the woven silver beneath the surface caught the light and made the skirt look briefly wet. The neckline left her shoulders bare. A narrow fall of embroidered leaves ran down one side rather than centering itself politely.

It made her look older.

Not older in years.

More expensive.

More deliberate.

Like someone whose mistakes would have consequences for other people.

She disliked how accurate that was becoming.

Meris came behind her and adjusted one of the pins.

"You look lovely."

"That sounds suspiciously maternal."

"I can be maternal."

"You can imitate it."

Meris met her eyes in the mirror.

"Careful."

Damaris smiled.

The maid found sudden fascination in the pin box.

Meris finished the adjustment and stepped back.

On the little table beside her lay the copied return chit from Mara Venn's green box.

Mera Holt had received Tal's damaged die.

That was where the paper trail stopped.

For now.

Damaris had spent the last hour dressing while trying not to think about a dead customs supervisor, a counterfeit credential case closed eighteen years ago, and the possibility that Teren Valeric would be drinking wine beneath seven ceremonial lamps before the night was out.

It was not working.

"Don't ask him tonight," Meris said.

Damaris turned.

"Ask whom what?"

"Ilyan. His uncle."

Damaris looked at her.

Meris shrugged.

"You have all been walking around Reedglass with the particular expressions young adults wear when they possess information they believe their elders cannot possibly detect."

"That is an offensively broad category."

"It has served me well."

"You don't know what we found."

"No."

Meris said it without irritation.

That was worse.

She crossed to the dressing table and picked up one of Damaris's earrings.

"But I know Ilyan has spent the afternoon looking as though he intends to challenge a relative to single combat using administrative law."

Damaris laughed before she could stop herself.

"That is unfair."

"To administrative law?"

"To Ilyan."

"Mm."

Meris handed her the earring.

Damaris put it on.

"Would you tell him not to speak to Teren?"

"I would tell him to decide what he wants before he opens his mouth."

"Answers."

"Everyone wants answers. Does he want his uncle frightened? Angry? Comfortable? Talking? Lying? Does he want to learn something, or does he want the pleasure of letting Teren know that he knows something?"

Damaris's fingers stopped at the second earring.

Meris watched her in the mirror.

"Those are different evenings."

There it was.

The reason Damaris could resent her aunt and still listen when she spoke.

"Did you learn that from intelligence work?"

"Marriage."

Damaris turned around.

Meris's face remained perfectly composed.

"You're joking."

"Frequently."

"That was not an answer."

"Exactly."

The maid coughed.

Meris took pity on her and moved toward the door.

"Rhyl is downstairs."

Damaris's stomach performed a humiliating little movement.

Meris saw that too.

Of course she did.

"Oh, don't look so alarmed. You're an adult. He's attractive. The condition is survivable."

"Aunt."

"Usually."

Damaris threw a glove at her.

Meris caught it and left with the glove.

---

Rhyl had been taught from childhood that staring was rude.

His education failed him completely.

Damaris came down the Reedglass stairs with one hand on the rail and paused when she saw him.

That helped nothing.

The green made her skin look warm. Silver flashed when she moved. Her hair had been pinned up loosely enough that several dark curls had already escaped around her neck.

Rhyl forgot whatever he had intended to say.

Damaris reached the bottom.

"Well?"

He considered lying.

"No."

Her eyebrows rose.

"I haven't asked anything yet."

"I know. I am declining in advance."

"That bad?"

"Quite the opposite."

She smiled.

That was worse too.

Rhyl offered his arm.

She took it and then, because apparently neither of them had any remaining interest in behaving subtly, leaned close enough to kiss him just below the ear.

He closed his eyes.

"That was cruel."

"You survived."

"Barely."

Behind them, Meris said, "The carriage is waiting."

Rhyl opened his eyes.

Meris stood in the doorway wearing dark blue and the expression of a woman who had watched generations of younger people discover desire and had long ago ceased pretending it was mysterious.

Damaris looked entirely unrepentant.

They went out.

---

The Hall of Seven Lamps had been built by people who believed architecture should make visitors reconsider their place in the universe.

Seven bronze lamps hung beneath the dome, each suspended on chains so fine they seemed to float. Their flames were ordinary tonight. No sacred working had been called. That made them almost more beautiful.

Below them, half the city had dressed for war and called it a reception.

House colors moved through the hall in silk, velvet, polished metal and embroidery. Order insignia flashed on collars. Magistrates stood beside bankers. Old soldiers drank with scholars they had spent thirty years insulting in print. The musicians played from a gallery screened by carved branches.

Damaris felt the room notice her.

Not everyone.

Enough.

She had attended receptions before.

She had never attended one after Silver Night.

That changed the arithmetic.

The Seven were no longer merely academy students with inconvenient surnames.

They were survivors.

They were graduates.

They were available.

For employment.

For patronage.

For marriage.

For blame.

Rhyl's hand rested lightly against the back of hers.

"Still breathing?"

"Against my better judgment."

"Good."

Mireth found them first.

She wore pale gold with no jewelry except a narrow chain at her throat. Caelen stood beside her in formal black, looking as though somebody had forced a sword to attend a poetry reading.

Damaris kissed Mireth's cheek.

"You look beautiful."

"So do you."

Caelen looked at Rhyl.

Rhyl looked at Caelen.

Neither man said anything.

Mireth sighed.

"They've been doing that for ten minutes."

"We are communicating," Caelen said.

"About what?"

"Nothing that concerns you."

"It concerns me if you begin dueling."

Rhyl said, "We have agreed not to."

Caelen nodded.

Mireth looked at Damaris.

"See?"

Damaris did.

Thessa and Lyrion arrived together a few minutes later, Thessa in deep red and Lyrion in the dark formal coat of his family. She had one hand tucked through his arm and the expression of a woman prepared to enjoy herself until someone made the mistake of giving her a reason not to.

Ilyan came last.

He looked immaculate.

He also looked furious.

Damaris intercepted him before he could scan the room a second time.

"Don't."

"I haven't done anything."

"Your face has."

Rhyl made a quiet choking sound.

Damaris looked at him.

"What?"

"Nothing."

Ilyan's attention moved past her shoulder.

Damaris followed it.

Teren Valeric stood near the north colonnade speaking with a woman from the Hall of Veils.

He was older than Ilyan by enough to make the resemblance unsettling rather than immediate. Same line of the jaw. Same pale eyes. Teren's hair had gone silver at the temples.

He laughed at something the woman said.

Ilyan went very still.

Damaris lowered her voice.

"What do you want?"

He looked at her.

She did not explain.

He knew what she meant.

After a moment he said, "I want to know why."

"Then don't tell him what you have."

"I know."

"You don't look like you know."

"I look like I want to hit him."

"That too."

A server passed with wine.

Ilyan took one.

Did not drink it.

"Tomorrow morning," he said.

Damaris nodded.

The unrestricted renewal record would be waiting.

Tonight he had only the fact of Teren's delegated seal on the restriction renewal.

Enough to hurt.

Not enough to spend carelessly.

Thessa appeared at Ilyan's other shoulder.

"Are we glaring at relatives?"

"Apparently."

"Good. I was worried this would be dull."

Lyrion touched the small of her back.

She leaned into him automatically.

The gesture was so familiar Damaris almost missed it.

Almost.

Across the hall, someone else did not.

A woman in violet watched them for half a second too long before turning away.

Damaris filed that somewhere in the increasingly overcrowded cabinet inside her head.

Then the musicians changed pieces.

The first formal dance began.

Rhyl offered his hand.

"Before somebody recruits you."

"Into what?"

"Marriage, espionage, municipal drainage. This room contains options."

She put her hand in his.

"Drainage sounds stable."

"Excellent pension."

They joined the floor.

---

Damaris had forgotten that dancing with someone she wanted was different.

The steps were the same.

That was almost insulting.

Rhyl's hand rested at her waist. Her palm fit against his. They turned beneath the lamps while the hall moved around them in measured circles.

"Meris knows we're sleeping badly," Rhyl said.

Damaris nearly stepped on him.

"What?"

"Not together."

"Rhyl."

"That was not what I meant."

"You chose an astonishing way to clarify it."

He was laughing now.

She wanted to kiss him.

This was becoming a recurring administrative problem.

"What does she know?"

"That we're tired."

"Everyone is tired."

"She asked whether the investigation was keeping us awake."

"And?"

"I said yes."

"Truthful."

"I am experimenting."

She squeezed his hand.

They turned.

Over his shoulder she saw Teren again.

He had stopped speaking to the Hall woman.

Now he was watching Ilyan.

Not staring.

Watching.

Ilyan had joined Caelen near one of the side tables and was making an admirable effort to look interested in a discussion about river tariffs.

Teren's gaze lingered.

Then moved away.

Damaris felt Rhyl's hand shift slightly at her waist.

He had noticed her attention.

"Teren?"

"Yes."

"He's watching Ilyan."

"I saw."

"Does Ilyan know?"

"Probably."

"Don't tell him."

Damaris looked at Rhyl.

"Why?"

"Because he's currently succeeding."

She glanced back.

Ilyan was still speaking to Caelen.

His shoulders were no longer quite so rigid.

"Fair."

The dance carried them past Meris.

Her aunt was speaking with an elderly magistrate.

Meris's eyes flicked to Damaris, then Rhyl's hand, then back to the magistrate without losing a word.

Damaris felt heat rise into her face.

Rhyl saw that too.

"Your aunt?"

"Unfortunately."

"Should I remove my hand?"

She pulled him closer.

"No."

His expression changed.

Only slightly.

Enough.

The next turn brought them close to the edge of the floor.

A man Damaris did not know bowed to them as they passed.

"Lady Damaris."

She returned the courtesy.

Rhyl's fingers tightened once.

"Who was that?"

"No idea."

"He knew you."

"Half the room knows me."

"I dislike half the room."

"Jealous?"

"No."

She waited.

He sighed.

"Possibly."

Damaris smiled so broadly that a passing dowager noticed and smiled too.

Rhyl looked offended.

"You are enjoying this."

"Immensely."

"Monster."

"Yes."

The music ended.

They bowed.

Neither moved away immediately.

Then the room changed.

Not dramatically.

No doors slammed. No herald shouted.

A court runner entered through the west arch and spoke to Judge Vaun.

Damaris saw Vaun's face from across the hall.

That was enough.

She touched Rhyl's wrist.

He turned.

Vaun read the folded note once.

Then looked directly at them.

---

They found her in a small side gallery beneath a painted panel of summer orchards.

Ilyan came with them.

Mireth remained in the hall with the others. There was no reason to turn seven visible survivors leaving together into an announcement.

Vaun handed the note to Damaris.

North Quay records office.

One paragraph.

The old counterfeit case had been found.

Not the whole case.

Its transfer register.

Damaris read the receiving line twice.

**Joint Office for Order and Credential Integrity.**

Below it, in a different hand:

**Dmitrigical Review liaison acknowledged.**

Ilyan swore softly.

Rhyl read over Damaris's shoulder.

"That still isn't authorship."

"No," Vaun said.

Her tone suggested approval that he had said it before she needed to.

Damaris looked at Ilyan.

He was pale.

"Your uncle?"

"Not named."

"Good."

He gave her a sharp look.

She held it.

"Good because you still have room to learn something."

His anger lasted perhaps three seconds.

Then he nodded.

Vaun pointed to the second line of the note.

Damaris had missed it.

**Senior examiner of record: Selwyn Arven. Current residence located.**

"Alive?" Rhyl asked.

"Apparently."

"Where?"

"Old Orchard district."

Ilyan looked toward the hall.

"Teren is twenty yards away."

Vaun folded the note.

"Selwyn Arven has been eighteen years away."

Ilyan almost laughed.

Almost.

"Point taken."

Damaris handed the note back.

"Tomorrow?"

"Tomorrow," Vaun said.

The reception continued beyond the gallery.

Music. Conversation. Glass against glass.

A hundred people who knew nothing about a damaged die, a buried packet, a counterfeit runner or the old office that had swallowed an investigation and left a liaison acknowledgement behind.

Or perhaps some of them knew.

That was the problem with rooms like this.

Damaris looked at Ilyan.

"Can you go back in there?"

He took a breath.

"Yes."

"Can you speak to Teren without asking?"

Another breath.

"Yes."

Rhyl said, "Convincingly?"

Ilyan looked at him.

"No."

That made all four of them laugh.

Quietly.

It helped.

---

Teren approached Ilyan before Ilyan could decide whether avoiding him would look worse.

Damaris was three conversations away.

Close enough to see.

Too far to hear everything.

That was probably safer.

Teren touched Ilyan's shoulder.

Ilyan did not flinch.

Good.

They spoke.

Teren smiled once.

Ilyan did not.

Then Teren's expression changed.

Not fear.

Concern.

He leaned closer.

Ilyan answered.

Damaris watched his hands.

Open.

Still.

No accusation.

No triumph.

Whatever he said, Teren's shoulders eased.

Then his uncle embraced him.

Briefly.

Ilyan stood rigid for half a heartbeat.

Then returned it.

Damaris looked away.

Some things deserved not to become evidence merely because she happened to see them.

Rhyl appeared beside her with two glasses.

He handed her one.

"Well?"

"He didn't explode."

"Promising."

"Low standard."

"We've had a week."

She drank.

The wine was floral and cold.

Across the hall, Thessa had somehow acquired an elderly knight who was telling her a story with enormous gestures. Lyrion watched with affectionate resignation. Mireth and Caelen had disappeared from Damaris's immediate sight, which in this room probably meant they had found a quieter corner rather than been assassinated.

Probably.

Rhyl followed her gaze.

"Do you ever stop counting everyone?"

"No."

"Good."

She looked at him.

"Good?"

"I like knowing someone is counting me."

That struck somewhere softer than she expected.

Damaris set down her wine.

"Come here."

"We are already—"

She kissed him.

Not long.

Long enough.

When she stepped back, Rhyl looked at the seven lamps overhead.

"I have reconsidered my earlier position."

"Which one?"

"This room may be tolerable."

"Because of the architecture?"

"Entirely."

A woman behind them laughed.

Damaris did not care.

For the first time since Silver Night, she realized she was enjoying herself without having to justify it.

The realization hurt.

Then it didn't.

Not completely.

She let that be enough.

---

Near midnight, the Seven found one another beneath the east colonnade.

No formal meeting.

No synchronized briefing.

Mireth had stolen two honey cakes from a tray and was sharing one with Caelen. Thessa had removed her shoes. Lyrion carried them because apparently some forms of devotion were less poetic than others.

Ilyan looked tired.

Better.

Damaris touched his sleeve.

"Tomorrow."

He nodded.

"Tomorrow."

No one asked what that meant.

They all had their own versions of tomorrow waiting.

Mireth looked around the group.

"Are we leaving?"

Thessa said, "I am."

"Because you're tired?"

"Because if one more elderly knight tells me I remind him of a woman he nearly married in 241, I am going to become historically significant."

Caelen said, "Reasonable."

Lyrion handed Thessa her shoes.

She stared at them.

"No."

He sighed and kept carrying them.

They walked out together.

Not in formation.

Not as investigators.

Seven friends leaving a reception too late, overdressed and exhausted.

The city beyond the Hall smelled of wet stone and flowering trees.

Carriages waited under the lamps.

Damaris paused before climbing into hers.

Across the steps, Ilyan looked back once toward the Hall.

Teren remained inside.

Tomorrow they would find Selwyn Arven.

Tomorrow Ilyan would decide what to do with his uncle.

Tomorrow Damaris would ask Talia what, if anything, Mara remembered after sleeping on eighteen-year-old records.

Tomorrow the Academy delivery would still be there.

None of it needed to be solved tonight.

Rhyl offered her his hand.

She took it.

---

## The Old Examiner

Damaris woke with one silver hairpin still lodged beneath her pillow and Rhyl's kiss lingering far more agreeably in memory than the headache behind her eyes.

The house had already begun without her.

Somewhere below, crockery knocked softly against a tray. A cart went past in the lane. Rainwater dripped from the eaves in the slow, irregular rhythm left behind after a wet night. She lay still for several breaths, warm beneath the coverlet, and remembered the Hall of Seven Lamps in pieces rather than sequence: Rhyl staring at her on the stairs, Thessa barefoot under the east colonnade, Ilyan standing rigid in his uncle's arms, Meris noticing absolutely everything and graciously pretending to notice only most of it.

Then she remembered the note.

Selwyn Arven.

Old Orchard.

She opened her eyes.

The silver pin stabbed her palm when she reached beneath the pillow.

"Appropriate," she muttered.

By breakfast she had changed into a plain dark walking dress, pinned her hair without assistance, and discovered that Meris had arranged the morning post beside the marmalade.

There were six invitations.

Damaris stared at them.

Meris buttered toast.

"No."

"I haven't said anything."

"You have your invitation face."

"I have an invitation face?"

"You do now."

Three envelopes bore House seals. One came from a River Ledger councillor she knew well enough to distrust socially and trust professionally. One came from an elderly widow who hosted musical evenings and collected young people with the same enthusiasm other people collected porcelain. The last was addressed in a hand Damaris did not recognize.

Meris nudged that one toward her.

"Open it."

Damaris did.

Lady Maeryn Caladren-Voss requested the pleasure of Lady Damaris Caladren-Ilyra's company at luncheon on Ledgerday. The invitation included Rhyl by name.

Damaris read it twice.

Meris bit into her toast.

"There. Consequences."

"Of dancing?"

"Of being seen enjoying dancing."

"We danced once."

"You kissed him in a room containing approximately everyone."

"Not everyone."

"An important distinction."

Damaris folded the invitation.

"Is this a marriage ambush?"

"Probably not."

"That is not reassuring."

"If Maeryn intended a marriage ambush, she would invite only you and arrange for Rhyl to arrive accidentally. Including him means she wants both of you aware that she has noticed."

"Why?"

"Ask her at luncheon."

Damaris gave her aunt a long look.

Meris drank tea.

There were mornings when affection for one's family required discipline.

"Vaun is sending a carriage at nine," Meris said.

Damaris's irritation vanished.

"Selwyn?"

"Yes."

"Rhyl?"

"Already downstairs."

That improved the morning considerably.

Meris saw that too.

Damaris refused to reward her by reacting.

---

Old Orchard had once been exactly what its name promised.

The city had swallowed most of the trees two centuries ago, but several ancient pear and apple orchards survived behind stone walls too inconveniently placed for builders to remove cheaply. Houses leaned into lanes that curved around roots older than their foundations. Moss climbed garden steps. Flowering branches reached over walls and dropped white petals into gutters.

Selwyn Arven lived in a narrow brick house beside one of the surviving orchards.

He answered the door himself.

He was old enough that Damaris first noticed the careful way he stood and only afterward the sharpness of his eyes. His white hair had been cut close. One hand rested on a polished cane. The other held the door.

Judge Vaun introduced herself.

Selwyn looked at her tribunal clasp, then at Damaris and Rhyl.

"You found Mara."

Nobody had said Mara's name.

Vaun's expression did not change.

"Why do you think so?"

"Because if you'd found the case first, you would have brought paper. If you'd found me first, you wouldn't have brought young people. Mara always remembered the young people involved in things."

Rhyl glanced at Damaris.

Selwyn noticed.

"Come in. My knees are old and your investigation is unlikely to improve them."

His sitting room smelled of apples and dust. A fire burned despite the mild morning. Shelves covered two walls, but most of the books were gardening manuals, histories and cheap adventure novels rather than professional texts.

Vaun waited until they were seated.

"Do you remember a counterfeit bonded-runner investigation involving damaged sponsor dies?"

Selwyn looked toward the fire.

"Yes."

No performance of surprise.

No miraculous recitation either.

He rubbed his thumb along the head of his cane.

"Not all of it. Enough to have disliked seeing the same trick again in the papers three years later."

Damaris leaned forward.

"Again?"

Vaun glanced at her.

Damaris sat back.

Selwyn smiled faintly.

"Mara's daughter has that habit too."

"You know Talia?"

"I knew a six-year-old who stole sealing wax and pressed flowers into it. I assume she has changed."

"Somewhat."

"Pity."

Vaun brought him back gently.

"The investigation."

Selwyn nodded.

"Credential Review started seeing impressions that were wrong in ways designed to look old. Not crude counterfeits. Someone understood how examiners think. A clean copy of a clean die is suspicious because copied lines lose character. A copy of a damaged die arrives already carrying an explanation for its imperfections."

"Mara borrowed Joren Tal's die."

"Yes."

"Why that one?"

"Because it had four cancellation cuts and a chipped lower border. Useful comparison piece. We borrowed several damaged dies from different offices."

"Did Tal's match a counterfeit?"

Selwyn frowned.

"Not exactly."

Damaris felt Rhyl's attention sharpen beside her.

Selwyn noticed that too.

"There was a sheet," he said. "Three impressions. One genuine cancelled die. One known counterfeit. One questioned credential. We were comparing defect inheritance."

"What happened to it?" Vaun asked.

"Missing when the case came back from joint review."

There it was.

Not a revelation.

A missing piece with a place where it had once been.

"Came back?" Rhyl asked.

Selwyn looked at him.

"The case file came back. The inquiry did not."

Vaun said, "Explain."

"We were told the cross-office portion had been assumed by the Joint Office for Order and Credential Integrity. We sent our working file. Several weeks later our local material was returned with instructions to close the credential-review matter as superseded."

"By whom?"

"The Joint Office."

"Signed?"

"Probably. I don't remember the name."

"Dmitrigical Review?"

Selwyn's eyes moved to Vaun.

"There was a liaison."

"Do you remember who?"

"No."

"Teren Valeric?"

Damaris watched his face.

Nothing useful happened.

Selwyn shook his head.

"Name means nothing to me."

Good.

It should have been disappointing.

Instead Damaris felt relief.

She was becoming suspicious of answers that arrived too neatly.

Vaun asked, "Why was the comparison sheet missing?"

"We asked."

"And?"

"We were told retained exhibits were outside our jurisdiction."

"Was that normal?"

Selwyn considered.

"Possible. Not normal."

"Difference?"

"Joint offices retain things. They also return things. There were rules. The trouble with joint work is that everybody knows a rule belonging to somebody else."

Rhyl's mouth moved.

Selwyn pointed his cane at him.

"You may laugh. I spent forty-two years being defeated by forms designed by people who died before I was born."

Rhyl surrendered and smiled.

Selwyn smiled back.

Damaris said, "Did you believe the case was actually solved?"

"No."

"Did Mara?"

"Absolutely not. Mara believed closure notices were insults written on expensive paper."

That sounded like Mara.

"What did you believe?"

Selwyn's amusement faded.

"That someone had found a way to move credentials through the spaces between offices. River Ledger saw runners. Customs saw seals. Bridge authorities saw passage rights. We each inspected our own little piece. Whoever made the counterfeits understood that nobody owned the whole journey."

Vaun's pencil moved.

"Did you identify a beneficiary?"

"No."

"A maker?"

"No."

"A distribution route?"

"Fragments."

"Do you still have anything?"

Selwyn looked toward the shelves.

For the first time he hesitated.

Then he stood.

Slowly.

"I kept something I should have destroyed."

Vaun did not move to help him.

Neither did Damaris.

He had not asked.

Selwyn crossed to a writing desk and unlocked the bottom drawer.

He removed a thin envelope.

"Not an exhibit," he said. "My notes. Personal notes. Which is why I was permitted to be foolish enough to keep them."

He gave the envelope to Vaun.

Inside were six small pages covered in compact handwriting.

Vaun read silently.

Damaris waited.

Rhyl waited.

Selwyn sat again.

Finally Vaun passed the first page to Damaris.

It was a list of questioned credentials.

Not names.

Routes.

A bridge crossing. A customs clearance. Two bonded deliveries. A warehouse admission. A temporary archive pass.

Beside three entries Selwyn had written the same notation.

**Damage copied after cancellation.**

Beside another:

**Issuer valid. Sponsor authority doubtful.**

And at the bottom:

**Ask Holt whether scrap custody permits comparison removal after return.**

Damaris read that line again.

"You asked Mera Holt?"

Selwyn closed his eyes.

"Yes."

"What did she say?"

"That it shouldn't."

"Shouldn't?"

"A returned comparison die was supposed to go back into its original disposal lot or be logged into retained exemplars. It was not supposed to wander."

"Did Tal's wander?"

"I don't know."

"Why write her name?"

"Because Mara's return receipt said Holt received it, and the sale weights later made no sense."

Damaris's skin prickled.

"You saw the weight discrepancy?"

"Eventually."

"Eleven ounces?"

Selwyn stared at her.

That was recognition.

"Yes."

Vaun stopped writing.

"How did you know the amount?"

"Because I requested the stores ledger after the case was closed."

"Was that authorized?"

"No."

Selwyn looked entirely unashamed.

"I was sixty-eight and angry."

Rhyl coughed into his hand.

Damaris did not look at him.

Vaun said, "What did Holt tell you?"

"Nothing useful. She was frightened."

The room changed.

Damaris kept still.

"Of you?" Vaun asked.

"No. Of the question."

"What did she say?"

Selwyn stared at the fire.

"She told me the lot had been reconciled. I said eleven ounces did not reconcile itself. She told me I was no longer assigned to the case."

"Anything else?"

A long pause.

"She asked whether Mara had kept copies."

Damaris felt the old house around them settle with a small wooden crack.

"What did you tell her?"

"That Mara kept copies of everything."

"Was that true?"

"Mostly."

"What happened then?"

"Nothing."

Selwyn's voice had gone quiet.

"Holt died the following winter. Fever. Ordinary fever, as far as I ever knew. The Joint Office dissolved some years later. I retired. Mara left Credential Review. The world found newer problems."

Vaun asked, "Why didn't you pursue it?"

Selwyn looked at her.

"With what authority?"

There was no self-pity in it.

That made the answer heavier.

"I had a closed file, a frightened stores supervisor, missing comparison paper, and a suspicion that somebody understood administrative seams better than we did. I could have made noise. Noise is not the same thing as evidence."

Vaun nodded once.

Damaris thought of all the times in the last days she had wanted suspicion to become proof simply because waiting was unbearable.

Selwyn looked at her.

"You disagree?"

"No."

"You looked as though you wanted to."

"I often do."

"Good. It keeps judges employed."

Vaun ignored that.

"May I take these notes?"

Selwyn looked at the envelope.

"Copy them here. Originals stay with me unless you issue process."

Vaun's eyebrows rose slightly.

"You remember procedure."

"I remember being young enough to annoy judges."

"You still manage."

"Practice."

For the first time, Vaun smiled.

---

They spent another hour copying Selwyn's notes.

No hidden confession emerged.

No list of conspirators waited on the final page.

There were only fragments: offices, credential numbers too old to mean anything without archive retrieval, defect comparisons, questions never answered, and the name of one clerk who had transferred away before the case closed.

It was enough.

More importantly, it was bounded.

When they left, Selwyn stood in his doorway beneath a branch heavy with white blossom.

"Lady Damaris."

She turned.

"Yes?"

"Tell Mara I was right about keeping notes."

"I suspect she'll be unbearable."

"She already was."

The door closed.

Rhyl waited until they reached the lane.

"I like him."

"You like everyone who insults bureaucracy."

"Not everyone. Some people lack technique."

Vaun handed Damaris the copied page.

"Holt is still the next custody question."

Damaris nodded.

"But she's dead."

"Records aren't."

"And the missing comparison sheet?"

"Separate question."

Rhyl looked at the line naming Holt.

"Selwyn saw the same eleven ounces eighteen years ago."

"Yes," Vaun said.

"And Holt was frightened."

"According to Selwyn's memory."

Rhyl nodded.

He did not need the rest explained.

They had learned enough from Vaun.

Damaris folded the copy.

A carriage waited at the end of the lane.

Talia sat inside.

Damaris stopped.

Talia opened the door before anyone reached it.

"Mother remembered something."

Vaun's gaze sharpened.

Talia held up a folded scrap of paper.

"Not a revelation. Before you make that face."

Damaris looked at Rhyl.

"I have a face too?"

"Several."

Talia continued before Damaris could retaliate.

"She remembered why she kept the return chit. Holt came to see her after the case closed. At home."

Vaun stepped closer.

"When?"

"Mara isn't certain. Weeks later. Maybe two months. She wrote the date down this morning after checking an old household account because she'd bought tea for the visit."

That was exactly the sort of ridiculous mundane anchor Damaris had begun to trust.

"What did Holt want?"

Talia looked at the folded paper.

"To know whether Mother still had her comparison copies."

Damaris and Rhyl looked at one another.

Selwyn had just said the same thing.

Independent memories.

Different witnesses.

Vaun held out her hand.

Talia gave her the note.

"Mother says Holt looked terrified. She wouldn't say why. She only told Mother that if anyone asked about Tal's die, Mother should show the return receipt before she said anything else."

Damaris felt cold despite the mild air.

"Did Mara ask what happened to it?"

"Of course."

"And?"

"Holt said, 'I put it where it was supposed to go.'"

Talia swallowed.

"Then she said, 'That stopped being the same thing as knowing where it was.'"

Nobody spoke for several seconds.

Vaun read the note again.

"Mara will give a statement?"

"Yes."

"Good."

Talia looked at Damaris.

There were shadows under her eyes.

"Is my mother in trouble?"

Damaris could have answered quickly.

She didn't.

"She's part of the evidence now. That isn't the same thing."

Talia studied her face.

Then nodded.

Not relieved.

But steadier.

Damaris reached into the carriage and squeezed her hand.

Talia squeezed back.

"Aereth asked for me this morning," she said.

Damaris waited.

"I went."

"How was he?"

"Embarrassed."

"That sounds promising."

Talia's mouth twitched.

"He asked whether I was leaving Reedglass."

"Are you?"

"I don't know."

There it was too.

An apology had not repaired everything.

It had merely made repair possible.

Damaris nodded.

"You don't have to decide today."

"I know."

Talia looked down at their joined hands.

"I'm learning that."

---

They returned to Reedglass after noon.

Ilyan was waiting in the library.

Not pacing.

That was new.

The certified unrestricted renewal record lay on the table before him.

Damaris stopped just inside the door.

"You have it."

"Yes."

Rhyl shut the door behind them.

Ilyan tapped the page.

"Teren renewed the restriction. We knew that. The unrestricted copy adds the requesting office."

Damaris sat.

"Which?"

"Joint Office for Order and Credential Integrity."

Silence.

Not because the name was new.

Because it had now arrived through another door.

Vaun placed Selwyn's copied notes beside Ilyan's record.

Two documents.

Different archives.

Different purposes.

The same dead office.

Rhyl leaned against the table.

"Still not authorship."

Ilyan looked at him.

"I know."

No anger this time.

Just exhaustion.

Damaris said, "Did you speak to Teren after we left?"

Ilyan looked at the window.

"About my mother."

Damaris waited.

"He asked whether I was sleeping. I told him badly. He said my mother used to sleep badly before examinations and then pretend she hadn't."

His mouth tightened.

"I had forgotten that."

Nobody turned it into evidence.

Nobody asked what Teren had meant.

Ilyan rested both hands on the table.

"I still want to ask him."

"Then ask him when you know what question you're asking," Vaun said.

Ilyan nodded.

Damaris looked at the two documents again.

The old Joint Office had touched the counterfeit credential investigation eighteen years ago.

Years later, after Silver Night, the same office's old authority still appeared in the request chain protecting Revision Seventeen's signatory.

That was a pattern.

A pattern was not yet an answer.

But it was finally something large enough that they could stop pretending each thread belonged to a different room.

Rhyl's hand settled against the back of Damaris's chair.

Warm.

Familiar already.

She leaned into it without thinking.

Ilyan noticed.

For once, he smiled.

"Maeryn invited you both to luncheon."

Damaris stared at him.

"How do you know that?"

"Meris told me."

"Traitor."

"She seemed delighted."

Rhyl said, "Should I be frightened?"

"Yes," Damaris and Ilyan said together.

That made Rhyl laugh.

Outside, the orchard trees around Reedglass moved in a soft afternoon wind.

Tomorrow there would be Holt's records.

There would be Mara's statement.

There would be an old joint office to reconstruct from the bureaucratic bones it had left behind.

And somewhere beyond all of that, an Academy delivery still waited unopened in the history they were trying to understand.

Damaris reached up and caught Rhyl's hand.

For the moment, that was enough.

---

## Where It Was Supposed to Go

Mara Venn arrived at Reedglass carrying the green tin box under one arm and a cake under the other.

Damaris stared at the cake.

Mara stared back.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"You have the face again."

Talia, behind her mother, closed her eyes.

"Please don't encourage her."

"I brought documents for a tribunal statement," Mara said, stepping into the morning room, "and almond cake because your cook sent my daughter home with broth twice this week and I was raised properly."

Judge Vaun looked up from the table.

"Put the documents here."

"The cake?"

"Anywhere else."

Mara nodded approvingly. "A serious woman."

Talia took the cake away before the law could contaminate it.

Aereth was already seated near the window. He had dressed himself that morning and looked offended by how much satisfaction everyone had taken in the accomplishment. The hollows beneath his eyes remained, but the strange exhausted looseness Damaris had seen after the missing hour was gone.

He and Talia had spoken again before breakfast.

Damaris knew because she had passed them in the corridor, standing several feet apart and speaking quietly.

She did not know what they had said.

Talia had not told her.

That was allowed.

Mara sat opposite Vaun and unfolded her hands.
