# Chapter Twenty-Four — Where the Question Changed

The memorandum was six pages long, which offended Ilyan before he had read a word of it.

Six pages was long enough to hide a murder if one had the right clerk and sufficient confidence in headings.

Examiner Sael took the packet from his hand.

“You are already angry at the paper.”

“I am preparing.”

“For what?”

“Disappointment.”

“That is not preparation. That is temperament.”

Sael set the memorandum back on the reading table and moved the lamp two inches farther from its edge. Morning rain pressed softly against the high windows of the Hall of Seven Lamps. The room smelled of wet wool, old paper, and the bitter tea Sael preferred because, Ilyan suspected, pleasure made him professionally uneasy.

Judge Vaun had not yet arrived. Neither had the Commonwealth clerk assigned to supervise the bounded review. That left Ilyan with six sealed pages he was not permitted to open and exactly enough time to imagine all the things they might contain.

He put both hands flat on the table.

Sael watched him.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“You have the expression.”

“What expression?”

“The one that makes archivists move sharp objects out of reach.”

Ilyan looked toward the letter tucked inside his coat.

Thessa’s latest had arrived before dawn.

> Condemned another culvert.
>
> Not romantically.
>
> Sorn says I have become drunk on authority. I pointed out that I am not authorized to condemn culverts, merely to recommend condemnation in language sufficiently embarrassing that somebody else does it.
>
> We ride north tomorrow. The road is supposed to be worse.
>
> I am delighted.
>
> I miss your face.
>
> — T.

Below that, in smaller writing:

> Especially the one you make when a document refuses to confess.

He had not shown Sael the letter.

He did not need to.

“Do not say it,” Ilyan said.

“I was going to ask whether you wanted tea.”

“No, you were not.”

“No.”

The door opened.

Judge Vaun entered with a Commonwealth records officer and Damaris, who carried a ledger case beneath one arm and looked as though she had already argued with three people before breakfast and won twice.

“Good,” she said when she saw Ilyan. “You have not opened it illegally.”

“I considered it.”

“I know.”

Vaun removed her gloves.

“Sit.”

They did.

The records officer was an elderly woman named Oressa Pell, not noble so far as Ilyan knew, with close-cut white hair and a pair of spectacles hanging from a green cord. She checked the seals, checked the authorization Vaun placed beside them, checked Ilyan’s name against the permitted-reader list, then checked all three again.

Nobody hurried her.

Finally she broke the outer seal.

“The memorandum may be read in full,” Pell said. “Two attached schedules remain restricted. You may see their headings, dates, and aggregate categories. You may not see names, individual kinship mappings, or identifiable appointment projections. The membership register for the temporary advisory authority is separately released in office-and-role form. Personal names remain restricted where the member was serving by delegated office rather than individual appointment.”

Ilyan nodded.

Pell looked at him over her spectacles.

“Do you understand?”

“Yes.”

“You answered too quickly.”

He took a breath.

“We can read the commissioning memorandum, the aggregate schedule categories, and the advisory authority’s office-level membership. We cannot use those releases to reconstruct named students, private marriage prospects, or protected family mappings.”

“Good.”

Damaris smiled at the table.

Pell unfolded the first page.

The title was less dramatic than Ilyan had hoped.

**Temporary Advisory Review on Regulated Chair Continuity and Medium-Horizon Concentration Exposure.**

Damaris leaned toward him.

“Try not to faint.”

“I am devastated.”

The authority had been constituted forty-one days before Silver Night, more than two weeks before the Joint Office docket opened. Its mandate came from an existing Dmitrigical Review provision used when several regulated chairs were expected to change hands within a short period and ordinary annual review was considered too narrow.

That was not unprecedented.

Pell supplied the comparison without being asked. Similar advisory reviews had been constituted seven times in the previous sixty years. Most had concerned shortages: too few qualified candidates, too many elderly chairholders likely to retire together, a regional training collapse, or one institution producing nearly all candidates in a specialty.

This one had begun that way too.

Three chairs were potentially turning over. Two were almost certain within the next year. A third depended on the health and intentions of an elderly holder. Several adjacent advisory and licensing offices were expected to change personnel during the following decade.

Ilyan read the first two pages without speaking.

The prose was dry enough to preserve grain.

Then he reached the sentence that had caused the temporary advisory authority to request a longer horizon.

He read it once.

Damaris read it over his shoulder.

Neither made a joke.

The authority had concluded that a five-year appointment forecast was insufficient because the cohort then approaching graduation would remain within its first decade of professional placement during the expected turnover of a second group of regulated offices.

Ten years was not mystical.

It was not a secret Valeric number.

It was the overlap between two appointment cycles.

Ilyan sat back.

“There.”

Vaun waited.

“That answers why ten.”

“Yes.”

He disliked the answer because it was sensible.

Damaris turned the page.

“Not all of it.”

She pointed.

The authority had begun with professional concentration. Its first concern was that too many future chairholders might come through the same training institution or technical school, creating a narrow pipeline vulnerable to doctrinal capture, patronage, or simple institutional groupthink.

Again, not sinister.

Again, the sort of thing a competent government ought occasionally to notice.

The next paragraph widened the concern.

The advisory authority had asked whether professional concentration might combine with **kinship concentration**.

Not because kinship itself was unlawful. Elaranth had too many Houses, cadet branches, marriages, cousins, legitimized lines, acknowledged companions, donor arrangements, and succession compacts for public office to function if relatives were treated as contamination.

The concern was cumulative.

One chairholder was not a dynasty.

Two cousins in different offices might be coincidence.

Three related officeholders could still be entirely legitimate.

But a cluster of regulated chairs, chartered offices, inheritance rights, and probable appointment eligibility concentrated inside a durable kinship network could become politically difficult to dislodge even if every individual appointment had been lawful.

Damaris tapped the margin.

“That is the phrase.”

Ilyan had seen it.

**Durable kinship network.**

Not House.

Not branch.

Not family.

Network.

The memorandum did not identify one in the released text.

It described a model.

The authority wanted to know whether the next decade could produce a self-reinforcing concentration of regulated magical office through overlapping inheritance, cadet affiliation, marriage, appointment eligibility, and long professional tenure.

Ilyan felt the old coldness return.

“Still policy,” he said.

Vaun nodded.

“Still policy.”

“Ugly policy.”

“Potentially.”

Damaris shook her head.

“Not even necessarily ugly yet.”

He looked at her.

“If five banks were about to be controlled by cousins, I would want somebody to notice before they all started lending to one another.”

“That is not the same.”

“No. It is an analogy. Those are permitted.”

Sael hid a smile behind his cup.

Damaris continued. “The question is what they intended to do with the answer.”

That was the problem.

The memorandum’s stated remedies were ordinary: stagger appointments, widen candidate recruitment, require recusals, strengthen conflict disclosures, commission training outside the dominant pipeline, or place temporary limits on simultaneous service by closely connected officeholders.

No disqualification by secret decree.

No compelled marriage.

No engineered scandal.

No students dying under a tower.

Ilyan thought of Corren admitting that unlawful interference had been contemplated elsewhere: disqualification, reassignment, scandal. He kept the thought where it belonged. Nearby evidence was not the same evidence.

Pell turned to the first attached schedule.

Only the headings were visible.

**A. Regulated-office turnover projection.**

**B. Candidate-pipeline concentration by training source.**

**C. Kinship-overlap bands by declared House sphere and public succession eligibility.**

**D. Cross-institutional appointment exposure.**

The second schedule had been prepared later.

Its heading made Damaris stop moving.

**Supplemental questions required to distinguish temporary concentration from durable concentration.**

Below it were four released category labels.

Expected external availability.

Public hereditary or chartered-office eligibility.

Declared senior-or-cadet House sphere.

Potential future kinship realignment where lawfully knowable.

Ilyan looked at Vaun.

“Marriage.”

“Potential future kinship realignment,” Vaun said.

“That is marriage wearing spectacles.”

“Sometimes adoption, legitimization, household compact, or succession transfer.”

“Marriage with friends.”

Damaris snorted.

Pell did not.

The schedule itself had not ordered the Joint Office to ask the Academy for probable marriage alignment. That mattered.

It said only that durable-concentration analysis would be improved by information about **future kinship realignment where lawfully knowable from existing declarations or public instruments**.

Damaris read the phrase again.

“Existing declarations or public instruments.”

“Yes,” Vaun said.

“The Academy request went further.”

“Yes.”

Ilyan leaned forward.

The memorandum had created the category.

It had not created the exact question.

Somewhere between the temporary advisory authority and the liaison-desk supplementation, *lawfully knowable future kinship realignment* had become *probable marriage alignment outside declared House sphere*.

That was a change.

Not yet a crime.

But a change.

“Who drafted the supplementation?” he asked.

Pell opened the office-level membership register.

The temporary advisory authority had six seats.

One representative of the Dmitrigical Review secretariat.

One regulated-chair continuity clerk.

One Crown appointments observer without voting authority.

One Commonwealth conflict-law adviser.

One independent magical-licensing statistician.

One rotating technical assessor drawn from the affected regulated institutions.

Ilyan read the list twice.

“No Valeric seat.”

“No House seat of any kind,” Pell said.

“Who held the technical-assessor seat?”

“Restricted by name. The office class is released.”

She pointed.

For the first half of the review, the rotating assessor had been delegated from a mixed civic-magical licensing institution. For the second half, beginning twenty-four days before Silver Night, the seat had rotated to a Dmitrigical chair-secretariat associated with one of the offices under review.

Ilyan felt Damaris become still beside him.

“Associated how?” he asked.

Vaun answered before Pell had to.

“Administratively. Do not turn association into ownership.”

“I know.”

“Good.”

The chair-secretariat was one in which Valeric interests had representation.

That much they already knew from the Joint Office docket.

Representation did not establish control.

The memorandum’s approval line showed four concurring votes and one abstention. The Crown observer did not vote. The office-level release did not identify which member abstained.

Then Damaris found the transmission note.

The ten-year supplementation sent to the Joint Office liaison desk had not been copied verbatim from the advisory memorandum.

It had been prepared as a shorter implementation note by the Dmitrigical Review secretariat after the authority approved the broader analytical categories.

That note was itself available in bounded form.

Pell placed it beside the memorandum.

It was one page.

Ilyan read it slowly.

The first three additions matched the advisory authority’s approved categories closely enough: future availability, public succession eligibility, House-branch distinction.

The fourth did not appear in the first implementation note at all.

There was no marriage question.

Damaris looked up.

“So that came later.”

“Yes,” Pell said.

The later liaison supplementation had been issued after the first Academy projection returned.

Its internal justification was also released.

**Initial aggregate insufficient to distinguish concentration that disperses through ordinary adult affiliation from concentration likely to persist across House spheres. Request bounded prospective kinship-alignment indicator if available.**

Ilyan stared at the sentence.

There it was.

Not a name.

Not a murderer.

A mind.

Or at least the nearest thing a bureaucracy provided to one.

Somebody had looked at the first aggregate projection and decided that the cohort’s political significance depended on whether its members were likely to marry outward or remain inside an already connected network.

Damaris spoke first.

“That is no longer just chair eligibility.”

“No,” Vaun said.

“It is coalition durability.”

Sael set down his cup.

“Potential coalition durability.”

Damaris gave him an irritated look.

“Yes. Potential.”

Ilyan barely heard them.

He was thinking of the Academy students before Silver Night.

They had worried about examinations, inductions, lovers, family dinners, uniforms, which order would take whom, whether a parent would approve of a courtship, whether somebody’s terrible poem would survive another revision.

Elsewhere, adults had looked at the same cohort and seen a ten-year concentration model.

That alone was not monstrous. Governments planned. Houses planned. Families planned. Damaris could probably produce three ledgers proving that grain merchants planned farther ahead than most lovers.

But the social shield around students had depended partly on adults agreeing not to make them pieces too early.

This memorandum had not broken that shield.

The later question had pressed against it.

“Who authored the later supplementation?” Ilyan asked.

Pell turned the page.

The answer was not a person.

**Dmitrigical Review liaison desk, pursuant to supplemental analytical request from the temporary advisory authority’s continuity subreview.**

Ilyan closed his eyes briefly.

“Of course there is a subreview.”

“There is always a subreview,” Damaris said.

“Why?”

“Because if six people can make a problem, three people can make it smaller and more irritating.”

Vaun ignored them.

The continuity subreview had consisted of three office roles: the regulated-chair continuity clerk, the independent licensing statistician, and the rotating technical assessor.

That narrowed the supported commissioning mind.

It did not identify an individual.

More importantly, the chronology did.

The subreview had been formed before the technical-assessor seat rotated to the Dmitrigical chair-secretariat. Its first two meetings concerned training-source concentration and retirement timing. The request for the ten-year horizon had been recommended before the rotation.

The prospective kinship question came after.

Four days after the assessor seat changed.

Damaris traced the dates with one fingernail.

“That does not mean the new assessor proposed it.”

“No,” Vaun said.

“But it tells us when the subreview changed its question.”

“Yes.”

Ilyan looked at the abstention line again.

“Can we know who requested the kinship supplement inside the subreview?”

Pell’s mouth tightened.

“There is a minute reference.”

“Released?”

“Partially.”

She produced one final slip.

The minutes did not record conversational attribution. They recorded motions.

The motion to seek a prospective kinship-alignment indicator had been introduced by the **rotating technical-assessor seat** and seconded by the **regulated-chair continuity clerk**.

The independent licensing statistician had requested that the question be limited to public or voluntarily declared information and had objected to predictive inference from private data.

The motion passed two to one.

Nobody spoke for several seconds.

Rain ticked against the windows.

Ilyan read the three lines again.

The first supported commissioning mind behind the marriage-alignment question was not House Valeric.

It was not even a named person.

It was an office seat: the rotating technical assessor, at that moment delegated from a Dmitrigical chair-secretariat in which Valeric interests had representation.

That was narrower than yesterday.

It was also maddeningly short of the thing his anger wanted.

“Can we identify the delegate?”

Vaun said, “Probably.”

Pell said, “Lawfully, yes. Not from this release.”

Ilyan looked between them.

“Why not?”

“Because the person was serving as a technical delegate inside a protected deliberative review. The name is not permanently sealed. It requires a relevance showing.”

“We have one.”

“Perhaps.”

Vaun’s voice was calm.

“We now have enough to request it. We did not have enough yesterday.”

That was how the investigation kept moving: not by smashing doors, but by earning the right to ask for the next key.

Ilyan hated that this had begun to satisfy him.

Damaris was still studying the minutes.

“What did the statistician say exactly?”

Pell read the released paraphrase.

“Prospective private affiliation is too uncertain for responsible concentration analysis unless grounded in an existing declaration, contract, public betrothal, succession instrument, or other voluntary disclosure.”

Damaris nodded slowly.

“The Academy agreed with that.”

“In effect,” Vaun said.

“And refused the predictive question.”

“Yes.”

“So the system had brakes.”

“Some of it did.”

Ilyan thought of 264-B/17’s independent reservation. Another brake. Another institution built by people who did not trust the other half completely.

It would have been easier if the past had divided itself cleanly into conspirators and heroes.

Instead it was full of people building safeguards beside dangerous machinery, clerks narrowing bad questions, officers accepting some unlawful acts and resisting others, institutions cooperating because they had to and distrusting one another because they had learned to.

“Request the delegate,” he said.

Vaun looked at him.

“And the chair-secretariat’s instruction to the delegate, if one exists. Not private deliberation generally. Just appointment authority, tasking, and whether the kinship motion came from the delegate’s own analysis or an instruction carried into the subreview.”

Sael’s eyebrows rose.

Ilyan noticed.

“What?”

“That was almost narrow.”

“I can make it worse.”

“No need.”

Vaun wrote the request.

---

Thessa’s day began with a wagon axle in mud.

By noon she had mud to the knee, rain under her collar, and a professional opinion about a district engineer she had never met.

Captain Sorn stood on the road shoulder while two teamsters levered the wagon high enough to wedge stone beneath the wheel. The road had been resurfaced the previous autumn. According to the district return, it had also been crowned properly, ditched on both sides, and certified for heavy freight through ordinary Bloomtide rain.

According to the road, the district return was fiction.

Thessa crouched beside the rut.

Water seeped through the gravel beneath her glove.

“Base layer is gone.”

Sorn grunted.

“Or never existed.”

Thessa scraped deeper.

Below the fresh gravel lay clay.

No crushed stone.

No drainage bed.

She sat back on her heels.

“Oh, that is rude.”

Sorn laughed.

The nearest village roadwarden, a broad woman named Kessa with rain streaming off the end of her nose, did not laugh.

“We told them.”

Thessa looked up.

“When?”

“Last Casktide. Again at Frostgate. Again when the thaw opened it.”

“Who is them?”

Kessa named the district works office, not an individual.

Good.

Thessa was learning that local anger arrived prepackaged with names and certainty. Her job was not to borrow either without checking.

“Do you have copies?”

Kessa stared at her.

“Of the notices?”

“Yes.”

“In the shrine chest.”

Sorn looked toward the village roofs through the rain.

“Why the shrine?”

“Because the works clerk lost the first one.”

Thessa stood.

“Selvara protects travelers and paperwork now?”

Kessa shrugged.

“Selvara keeps what the district office doesn’t.”

An hour later Thessa sat beneath the awning of a roadside shrine with three damp copies of old notices spread across her knees.

The roadwarden had reported missing base stone before the resurfacing contract was closed.

The contractor had certified the base as complete anyway.

That did not prove theft. It could be incompetent inspection, substituted material, a drainage failure that removed stone later, or records copied from the wrong section.

Thessa wrote all four possibilities.

Then she recommended immediate heavy-load restriction, physical core sampling, and audit of the works contract before another grain wagon broke an axle.

Sorn read the draft over her shoulder.

“You left out fraud.”

“I left out what I cannot show.”

“Good.”

Thessa glanced sideways.

“You sound like someone I know.”

“Poor bastard.”

She smiled despite herself.

That evening, in a wayhouse smelling of onion broth and wet leather, she wrote to Ilyan.

> Today I discovered that a road can lie more convincingly than most people.
>
> The surface says repaired. The clay underneath says somebody either failed magnificently or signed for work that was not done. I have recommended we cut it open and ask.
>
> You would like it here. Everything is evidence if you are willing to get mud in your boots.
>
> I am beginning to suspect that is also true at the Hall, except your mud has filing numbers.
>
> I miss you.
>
> I also still love my work.
>
> These remain annoyingly compatible.

She hesitated, then added:

> I dreamt about you last night and woke up furious at a blanket.
>
> I will not elaborate because you have not paid your ribbon-storage bill.

She sealed the letter smiling.

---

The request for the technical delegate’s identity did not return that day.

It did not return the next.

Ilyan went to work anyway.

That, too, had become part of adulthood: important questions continuing to exist while one ate, slept, copied forms, attended instruction, answered lesser questions, and waited for somebody in another building to find the correct box.

On the second evening, Mireth found him in the Hall courtyard reading Thessa’s road letter beneath the covered walk.

“You are smiling again.”

“So I have been told.”

“Disturbing.”

She leaned against the stone rail beside him. Caelen was at a Crown reception. Damaris and Rhyl were somewhere together and had become sufficiently predictable that nobody bothered pretending otherwise. Lyrion had sent apologies from a diplomatic dinner with a sketch of himself being strangled by a neckcloth.

Mireth looked out at the rain-dark garden.

“What did the memorandum say?”

Ilyan gave her the short version.

Ten years was the overlap of appointment cycles. The advisory authority had begun with a legitimate concern about professional concentration. It widened that concern to durable kinship concentration. The first implementation note did not ask about marriage. After the initial projection returned, the continuity subreview decided it could not tell whether the concentration would disperse through ordinary adult affiliations or persist across House spheres. The rotating technical assessor proposed asking for prospective kinship alignment. The assessor seat had just rotated to a Dmitrigical chair-secretariat with Valeric representation.

Mireth listened without interrupting.

“And the name?”

“Requested.”

“Not known.”

“Not yet.”

She nodded.

He looked at her.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“You are doing the thing.”

“What thing?”

“Being pleased with me for not declaring the delegate my uncle.”

“I was not.”

“You were.”

“I was thinking that six months ago you would have.”

“That is worse.”

Mireth smiled.

Then her expression changed.

“The question itself bothers me.”

“Marriage?”

“No. The assumption underneath it.”

He waited.

“That we would become a stable political block because we were connected.”

“The memorandum does not say the Seven.”

“I know.”

She looked toward the dark garden.

“But somebody was trying to measure whether a young cohort’s affiliations would disperse or persist. That is how Houses think about marriages after graduation. Not before.”

There it was.

Not evidence of murder.

Not even necessarily evidence of wrongdoing.

A cultural trespass.

The students had been approaching the threshold where adult society could begin counting their marriages, careers, offices, alliances, and children openly. Somebody had started early.

“The Academy refused,” Ilyan said.

“Yes.”

“The statistician objected.”

“Yes.”

“The advisory memorandum limited itself to public or voluntarily declared information.”

“Yes.”

Mireth turned to him.

“That is why it bothers me rather than proving anything.”

He understood.

A system could be functioning and still reveal what someone wanted.

The question had reached toward protected students and been stopped.

The reaching mattered.

The stopping mattered too.

Both belonged in the truth.

---

The delegate’s identity arrived the following morning under seal.

Judge Vaun read it first.

Her face did not change.

Ilyan had learned that this meant nothing.

She passed the release across the desk.

The name was not his father’s.

It was not his uncle’s.

It was not Corren’s.

For one disorienting heartbeat, relief arrived before he could decide whether he deserved it.

The technical delegate was **Master Auditor Pellisar Veyran**, serving under temporary appointment from the chair-secretariat.

Ilyan knew the name only faintly: an institutional auditor, older than his parents, not a Valeric, not a Great House principal, not someone who had occupied any important place in his childhood.

Damaris, reading beside him, frowned.

“I know that name.”

“From where?”

“Give me a moment.”

She did not invent the answer to fill the silence.

Vaun handed Ilyan the second release.

Pellisar’s tasking order was short.

He had been instructed to assess whether the projected candidate pool created **durable concentration exposure beyond ordinary conflict-screening remedies** and to recommend additional questions if necessary.

The tasking came from the chair-secretariat as an office.

No personal instruction from a Valeric actor appeared in the released order.

Then came the detail that changed the next question.

Pellisar had attached a note to his kinship-alignment motion.

Not a name.

Not a family.

A reason.

**Current aggregate understates persistence because one projected kinship cluster shows both unusually high successor depth and unusually low expected dispersal under existing declared affiliations.**

Ilyan read it again.

Successor depth.

The phrase was clinical.

Its meaning was not.

A family line with more future adults than expected.

More heirs, more cadets, more eligible descendants, more people who could marry, inherit, hold office, and still leave cousins behind to do the same.

The demographic motive was no longer merely an abstract possibility supplied by later investigation.

Someone inside the pre-Silver-Night review had seen an unusually deep successor cluster and considered its likely persistence politically important.

Still no family name.

Still no rival.

Still no numbers.

Still no bridge to murder.

But the concern existed before the massacre.

Damaris finally snapped her fingers once.

“Pellisar Veyran.”

Vaun looked at her.

“He audited succession exposure for the River councils twelve years ago. My aunt hated him.”

“Why?” Ilyan asked.

“He was good.”

“That seems a poor reason.”

“You have never met my aunt during an audit.”

Damaris leaned over the tasking order.

“If it is the same man, he is not a demographic theorist. He is an institutional concentration auditor.”

Vaun nodded.

“That is consistent with his service record.”

“So he may have identified the cluster because that was exactly what he was sent to find.”

“Yes.”

Ilyan looked at the note.

“And somebody sent him.”

Vaun’s eyes lifted to his.

“The chair-secretariat sent him to assess concentration. That is established. Do not make the next sentence larger than the record.”

He breathed.

The old anger wanted ancestry. It wanted his father’s hand in every margin, his uncle behind every euphemism, House Valeric staining every piece of paper merely by existing nearby.

The record did not give him that.

What it gave him was harder.

A legitimate review had found a real political condition: a projected kinship cluster with unusually deep succession capacity and low expected dispersal.

A competent auditor had decided ordinary conflict screening might not be enough to understand it.

The bureaucracy had then asked a protected school a question it should not have asked so broadly.

The school refused.

None of that explained why students died.

But now they knew what some adults had been afraid of before they did.

Not simply who might hold the next chair.

Who might still be holding influence ten years later, after marriages, appointments, inheritances, and the multiplication of a family line had made the concentration harder to unwind.

Ilyan folded the release.

“What can we ask next?”

Vaun did not answer immediately.

Damaris did.

“Whether the cluster was actually exceptional.”

Ilyan looked at her.

“Not who.”

“No. Not yet. Compare the shape.”

She pulled a blank sheet toward herself.

“Successor depth against ordinary Great House and cadet baselines. Expected dispersal against normal marriage and service patterns. Office eligibility against other cohorts. If Pellisar’s concern was reasonable, the numbers should show that without telling us the family.”

“And if it was not?”

“Then we ask why he made it look reasonable.”

Vaun nodded once.

That was enough.

The next question had earned itself.

Outside, the rain had stopped.

Water still clung to the courtyard leaves, bright along every edge. Somewhere beyond the Hall, Thessa was cutting into a road to see whether the stone promised on paper existed beneath the gravel.

Ilyan looked down at Pellisar Veyran’s neat institutional phrase.

**Unusually high successor depth.**

He thought of thirty-six dead students.

Then he put the dead where they belonged—not inside the sentence, not yet—and began asking what an ordinary future looked like.
