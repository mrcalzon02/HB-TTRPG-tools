# Chapter Six M — The Duplicate Slip

The River Ledger did not lose documents often.

It misplaced them with exquisite precision.

Damaris explained the distinction while leading Ilyan and Judge Vaun through a corridor whose windows looked down over the river quays. Morning light lay white on the water. Barges moved beneath the bridges in patient lines, their cargo awnings striped with house marks, guild marks, customs chalk and the less official symbols dockworkers used when they wished to communicate that a particular captain was an ass.

"Lost means nobody knows where it is," Damaris said. "Misplaced means three departments know exactly where it is and disagree about which one has the authority to admit it."

"That sounds worse," Ilyan said.

"It is much more respectable."

Maeryn had obtained the historical Contingency packet before breakfast.

She had also refused to let Damaris carry it out of audit custody.

That had annoyed Damaris for almost seven minutes, which Ilyan considered a remarkably short recovery time for being told no by one's own family.

The audit room waiting for them was narrow, cool and aggressively plain. No portraits. No ornamental screens. No upholstered chairs capable of concealing papers beneath cushions. The River Ledger apparently believed that comfort encouraged accounting irregularities.

Lady Maeryn Caladren-Voss sat at the end of the table with a sealed grey packet before her and a cup of tea at her right hand.

Jalen occupied the chair beside her.

He looked rested, which Ilyan found personally offensive.

"Judge," Maeryn said.

"Lady Maeryn."

Her eyes moved to Ilyan.

"You look worse."

"Thank you."

"It was not criticism."

Damaris sat opposite her aunt.

"Was it there?"

Maeryn put one finger on the packet.

"The month survived intact. Contingency's year-end reconciliation survived intact. The duplicate authority series survived intact."

Damaris's shoulders loosened.

"And C-17?"

"Is inside."

Nobody spoke for a moment.

Maeryn looked at Vaun.

"Your order permits inspection and certified copying of the duplicate authority slip and its attached accounting references. It does not permit removal of the original."

"Agreed."

"Good. I dislike arguing with judges before tea."

"After tea?"

"Much easier."

Damaris hid a smile in her hand.

Maeryn broke the archive seal.

The packet contained the usual dull machinery by which institutions remembered spending money: messenger fees, carriage reimbursements, sealing wax, clerical overtime, protected-room charges, two meals purchased at an hour sufficiently late that somebody had apparently considered hunger an administrative emergency.

C-17 was halfway through.

It was a narrow duplicate slip attached to a messenger disbursement voucher.

The messenger's name meant nothing to Ilyan.

The purpose line did.

**Protected evidentiary substitution delivery — VEIL-3 external review.**

The issuing office was Contingency.

The administrative authorization field bore a clerk's mark.

The personal countersignature beneath it was not Corren's.

Ilyan knew that before Jalen leaned closer.

He had seen enough of his father's writing over the past week to recognize the absence of it.

The hand was narrower. More vertical. The final stroke of the surname initial turned inward instead of cutting away.

Damaris did not ask him what he thought.

Jalen took out his comparison lens.

"Do we have a known sample?"

Maeryn slid another folder across the table.

"Three."

Ilyan looked at her.

"You already suspected someone."

"No. I already knew which offices had authority to countersign Contingency evidentiary movement at that date. That produced four possible officeholders. One was absent from the capital. One had delegated authority but no surviving hand sample in this archive. Two have ordinary financial signatures here. I brought what the Ledger possesses."

That was, Ilyan thought, the difference between investigation and guessing performed in expensive clothing.

Jalen worked without commentary.

He compared line pressure, initial construction, the shortened office flourish and the habit of crossing the final vertical through the preceding letter.

Then he sat back.

"Highly consistent with **Deputy Secretary Neral Voss**."

Damaris went very still.

Ilyan looked at her.

The surname was not Caladren-Voss.

Not necessarily.

Elaranthi surnames had an unfortunate habit of repeating themselves across three centuries of marriages, cadet lines, legitimizations and people who had simply been born with insufficient concern for future archivists.

Maeryn answered the question before anyone asked it.

"No close relation to my branch. Voss was his birth family. Minor river gentry. His daughter married into Caladren service later."

Damaris nodded once.

Jalen examined the second and third samples.

"I would want another examiner before putting it in a finding."

"Already requested," Vaun said.

Damaris glanced at her.

"You do enjoy arriving with tomorrow already arranged."

"Tomorrow is unreliable."

The duplicate slip had an attachment reference.

Not the substitute packet itself.

A justification code.

**C-17/J2 — evidentiary continuity correction following bearer failure.**

Below it, in a cramped accounting hand, someone had written:

**Charge to protected review implementation, Revision 17.**

Ilyan felt his jaw tighten.

There it was again.

Revision Seventeen had not merely swallowed Haleth's later inquiry. Its implementation account had paid for the messenger who brought him the cleaned substitute packet four days after Silver Night.

That still did not prove Revision Seventeen had been created for that purpose.

But the same administrative structure was no longer merely nearby.

It had paid the bill.

Maeryn turned the slip over.

There was writing on the back.

One sentence.

**Original continuity packet unrecoverable; substitute prepared from retained office copies to permit independent closure.**

Vaun read it twice.

"Prepared by whom?"

No name.

Only another reference.

**Preparation certificate: C-17/P.**

Damaris closed her eyes.

"Please tell me that survived."

Maeryn looked almost sympathetic.

"Not in the expense packet."

"Of course not."

"But the disbursement index gives its destination."

She turned the ledger toward them.

C-17/P had been returned after payment reconciliation to the **Contingency Secretariat implementation file**.

That file had later transferred into Revision Seventeen protected review.

Ilyan laughed once.

It was not amusement.

"Everything goes there."

Vaun looked at him.

"Several things go there."

He rubbed both hands over his face.

"I know."

Damaris nudged his boot under the table.

Not comfort exactly.

Contact.

He let his foot remain against hers for a moment.

Maeryn watched the exchange and said nothing.

Older adults in Elaranth had apparently developed the useful skill of noticing everything and choosing which fraction deserved speech.

Jalen returned to the accounting notation.

"There's something else."

He pointed to the charge allocation.

The messenger fee had been paid from Revision Seventeen implementation.

The packet-preparation labor had not.

That charge had been transferred from another cost center.

**Academy casualty reconciliation — emergency clerical support.**

Damaris frowned.

"Why would casualty reconciliation pay for rebuilding a credential packet?"

"It might not have known it was," Jalen said. "Cost centers are not descriptions of moral purpose. Someone could have assigned clerical hours there because the staff were already processing Silver Night records."

"Or because the casualty list was one of the things being replaced."

"Also possible."

Vaun tapped the code.

"Can you identify the transfer authority?"

Maeryn nodded toward a second ledger.

"That is why I asked for the month rather than the slip."

Damaris smiled despite herself.

"I forgive you for not letting me carry the packet."

"I was devastated by your displeasure."

They found the transfer six pages later.

It had been authorized the morning after Silver Night.

Not by Neral Voss.

By **Corren Valeric**.

Ilyan did not move.

His father's signature sat beneath a mundane allocation of clerical hours: twelve staff-days from Academy casualty reconciliation made available to Contingency evidentiary reconstruction and closure support.

The words were ordinary.

That was almost worse.

Corren had not signed the later messenger slip.

He had not personally countersigned the cleaned packet's delivery to Haleth.

But the morning after Silver Night, he had authorized the staff pool from which the reconstruction labor was paid.

"He knew they were rebuilding it," Ilyan said.

Vaun did not answer immediately.

"He knew he was assigning casualty-reconciliation clerical capacity to evidentiary reconstruction and closure support."

Ilyan looked at her.

"That's what I said."

"No."

He hated her a little for that.

Which was one of the reasons he trusted her.

Vaun continued, "We do not yet know what packet he believed they were reconstructing, what he knew had been omitted, or whether he saw the finished substitute."

Ilyan looked back at the signature.

Corren had already been guilty of enough.

There was no need to make him guilty of things the paper had not proved.

That discipline had become exhausting.

It was also the only thing separating investigation from inheritance.

Damaris's boot touched his again.

This time he pressed back.

---

The second handwriting examiner arrived before noon.

She agreed with Jalen.

Neral Voss had almost certainly countersigned C-17.

The finding went into Vaun's file with the usual careful language and none of the satisfaction that fiction assigned to identifying a name.

Neral had been dead for nine years.

His office papers had been distributed between the Tribunal administrative archive and a family deposit in a minor river-town record house.

Neither collection could be opened that afternoon.

There was, however, one living person who had worked directly beneath him.

Oren Savel.

Ilyan stared at the name when Maeryn found it in the personnel index.

Savel again.

The request-entry clerk who had remembered Pel Ardis.

The clerk who had remembered that the technical question came before the bearer names.

The clerk who had preserved notes because Ardis had become uneasy after Silver Night.

"Did he work for Neral?" Ilyan asked.

"Temporarily," Maeryn said. "Four months. Contingency borrowed clerical staff from three offices during graduation season."

Vaun looked at Ilyan.

"We speak to him again."

"Today?"

"If he agrees."

Thessa would have said something rude about miracles.

Ilyan missed her suddenly.

Not because he needed help with the evidence.

Because the morning had become full of his father again, and Thessa had developed the irritating habit of making rooms containing his father easier to breathe in even when Corren himself was only ink.

He sent her a note before they left the Ledger.

**Found the duplicate. Father authorized the clerical transfer. Not the delivery. Savel may know the countersigner. I am all right. Mostly.**

He stared at the last two words.

Then left them.

Her reply reached him before they crossed the second bridge.

**Mostly is allowed. Eat something.**

Beneath it:

**Also, if you get yourself murdered before supper I will be furious.**

He folded the note carefully and put it inside his coat.

Vaun pretended not to notice.

---

Oren Savel agreed to see them at fourth bell.

His rooms smelled of paper, old wood and the fennel broth simmering on a small kitchen stove. He had apparently decided that retirement meant no longer separating workspaces from soup.

Ilyan approved.

Savel read the certified copy of C-17 in silence.

When he reached Neral's countersignature, his mouth tightened.

"Yes."

Vaun waited.

Savel looked at her.

"You want more than yes."

"Usually."

He set the paper down.

"Neral signed those. Not all of them. Anything that moved outside Contingency after the incident went across his desk. He was furious for about a week."

"At what?"

"Everything."

"More useful than that."

Savel sighed.

"At Ardis. At the casualty office. At the Academy. At whoever had decided an emergency credential procedure needed three institutions and five kinds of seal. At Corren Valeric for changing instructions after the alarm. At Teren for asking questions. At Haleth for refusing closure. Pick a direction. Neral had enough anger for it."

Ilyan leaned forward.

"Changing what instructions?"

Savel looked at him.

Recognition passed through his face.

Not surprise at the surname.

Calculation about whether the answer belonged to the young man sitting before him.

Then he looked at Vaun.

"Can I answer that?"

"If you remember it from your own work."

"I do."

He rubbed his thumb along the edge of the table.

"The morning after the alarm, Corren changed the casualty-reconciliation priority."

"From what to what?"

"From body confirmation first to credential-and-bearer reconciliation first for the west tower."

Ilyan felt the room narrow.

"Why?"

"He said there was a bearer missing who might not be dead."

Mara.

Nobody said her name.

They did not need to.

"Did he identify the bearer?" Vaun asked.

"Not to me. He gave Neral a protected designator and told him not to close the west-tower continuity chain on casualty presumption. Neral argued. Corren won."

That did not fit neatly with the later attempt to close Mara as presumed dead.

Which meant either Corren changed position, someone else overrode him, or different parts of Contingency were working at cross-purposes.

Ilyan felt something almost like relief.

Not because the contradiction made his father better.

Because contradictions were real.

Real institutions contained people who disagreed, duplicated work, concealed things from one another, followed obsolete instructions and occasionally spent an entire morning defeating their own previous afternoon.

A conspiracy that behaved like a single mind was usually a story told after the fact.

"Did Neral prepare the substitute packet?" Vaun asked.

Savel shook his head.

"No."

"Who did?"

"I don't know. Neral ordered it prepared."

"On whose instruction?"

Savel's gaze moved to the certified slip.

"That was the argument."

"Which argument?"

"Haleth wouldn't close. Neral wanted something delivered that would let him examine the credential question without the missing bearer. Teren said the failed fragments mattered. Neral said they were unavailable. Teren said then the packet was incomplete."

Ilyan stared at him.

"Teren knew about the substitute packet?"

"He knew they were trying to reconstruct one. I don't know if he saw what they sent."

Another distinction.

Another irritatingly necessary distinction.

Savel continued.

"Corren came in halfway through."

"And?"

"He told Neral not to use the word closure."

Vaun's eyebrows rose slightly.

"Why?"

"Because, he said, there was nothing to close until the bearer was found or confirmed dead."

Silence.

Ilyan heard the broth simmering in the next room.

His father had helped build the disaster.

His father had authorized Mara's independent role.

His father had supplied Teren with Mara's authentication phrase later.

His father had also, the morning after Silver Night, resisted closing her continuity chain on a false casualty presumption.

And his clerical allocation had nevertheless enabled the reconstruction of the packet somebody later tried to use for precisely that closure.

People were unbearable.

Dead fathers especially.

"Then why did Neral send it?" Ilyan asked.

Savel looked tired.

"Because Neral thought Haleth was being impossible. Because the office was drowning. Because somebody above him wanted the matter reduced to something administratively containable. Because he believed the successful credentials were enough to answer the narrow technical question. I don't know which reason mattered most."

"Who was above him?"

"The Contingency Secretary."

"Name?"

Savel hesitated.

Then gave it.

**Secretary Avarn Dethril.**

The name was not Valeric.

Not Dmitrigical.

Not one Ilyan knew.

Vaun wrote it down.

"Living?"

"Last I heard."

"Where?"

"Retired north."

"That is not a place."

Savel almost smiled.

"It becomes one when you are old enough."

Vaun did not smile back, but the corner of her mouth considered treason.

"What did Dethril want?"

"I never heard him say."

"What did you observe?"

Savel thought.

"He wanted the emergency system defended as lawful. He wanted the Academy casualty process separated from the credential review. He wanted Mara treated as a casualty problem rather than a witness-continuity problem."

There it was.

Not motive.

Behavior.

"Did Corren agree?" Ilyan asked.

"Not about Mara."

The answer came quickly.

"About the rest?"

"Sometimes."

Ilyan looked down at his hands.

He had spent years wanting his father to become a shape he could understand.

Monster would have been easiest.

Martyr would have been worse.

The records offered neither.

Corren was becoming what most dangerous people eventually became under sufficient examination: a man who had made choices, some monstrous, some protective, some compromised, some perhaps merely bureaucratic, and who had been surrounded by other people making choices of their own.

It did not reduce the dead.

It did make hatred more work.

Savel rose to rescue his broth before it became evidence of another institutional failure.

When he returned, he brought bowls.

Vaun accepted one.

Ilyan blinked at her.

"What?" she said.

"Nothing."

They ate.

The soup was good.

That annoyed him too.

---

Thessa found him after dusk beneath the old oak near the Academy wall.

He had not asked her to come there.

He had only told her where he was going.

Apparently that distinction had become meaningless between them.

Bloomtide evening had softened the grounds. The grass smelled green and damp. Lanterns were beginning to appear along the paths, one by one, and somewhere in the residence court a group of new graduates were laughing with the heedless volume of people who had not yet learned how quickly adulthood could turn laughter into evidence, leverage or memory.

Thessa sat beside him without speaking.

Their shoulders touched.

Ilyan gave her the short version.

Not every reference number.

Not every signature.

Neral. The reconstructed packet. Corren's clerical transfer. Savel's memory that Corren had refused to close Mara as dead. Secretary Dethril's preference for separating the casualty problem from the credential problem.

When he finished, Thessa said, "That is inconvenient."

"Yes."

"You wanted him simpler."

"Yes."

"He's still guilty."

"Yes."

"And he still did something that may have kept Mara from being administratively buried."

Ilyan looked at her.

"You make it sound easy."

"No. I make it sound like two things."

He laughed despite himself.

She leaned her head against his shoulder.

For a while they watched the lanterns.

Then Ilyan said, "I kept your note."

"Which one?"

"The one threatening me over supper."

"That wasn't a threat."

"It said you'd be furious if I got murdered."

"A statement of future emotional condition."

"Very legal."

"I've been around you too much."

He turned his head.

Her hair brushed his cheek.

The previous evening she had kissed him there and left him with the startling discovery that he wanted repetition.

Now she was close enough that he could feel the warmth of her face without touching it.

He could have said something clever.

He did not.

He leaned the small remaining distance and kissed her.

Not dramatically.

Not with the desperate competence of lovers in songs who had apparently been born knowing where every hand belonged.

It was brief, uncertain and very soft.

Thessa went still.

Ilyan pulled back half an inch.

"Was that—"

She kissed him again before he could finish.

That answered enough.

Her hand came up to the side of his neck. His found her waist almost by accident. The second kiss lasted longer, still careful, carrying years of friendship into a new grammar neither of them had practiced together.

When they parted, Thessa rested her forehead against his.

"You took your time."

He stared at her.

"Yesterday I didn't know I wanted to do that."

"Yesterday you knew enough to look disappointed when I only kissed your cheek."

"I did not."

"Your face is the least private part of you."

He groaned.

She laughed against his mouth and kissed him a third time.

The old oak did not care.

The Academy windows did not darken in shock.

Somewhere on the path an elderly groundskeeper saw them, altered course by three polite steps and continued carrying a watering can with the serene indifference of someone who had spent a century watching graduates discover one another under trees.

When Thessa finally leaned back, her hand remained in his.

"What happens tomorrow?"

It was not a question about them.

Not only about them.

Ilyan looked toward the Hall lights beyond the wall.

"We find Dethril."

"And the preparation certificate."

"And the original casualty correction."

"And then?"

"Then we see which one lies less."

Thessa squeezed his fingers.

"Good."

They stayed beneath the oak until the lanterns were fully lit.

Tomorrow had acquired another appointment.
