# Chapter Six G — The Notes He Left Her

Lena Ardis worked in a school archive that smelled of chalk, old wool and rain-damp children.

Ilyan had expected something grander.

Not because school archivists required marble, but because Pel Ardis had been dead for fourteen years and had somehow grown larger in Ilyan's mind during the walk north. Dead people did that when they left behind unanswered questions. They became corridors instead of people.

The northern ward school was resolutely uninterested in cooperating with that transformation.

A boy of perhaps nine ran past them carrying a wooden model of the moon with one half painted silver and the other half still wet. Two girls were arguing beside a rack of cloaks about whose turn it was to feed something called Mistress Pebble. Somewhere beyond the archive door, a teacher was attempting to persuade twenty children that multiplication was not a personal insult.

Judge Vaun stopped beneath a painted sign that read ARCHIVE — STAFF ONLY.

Thessa looked at Ilyan.

"Better than Restricted Testimony."

"The lettering?"

"The threats."

Vaun knocked.

A woman called, "If you're bringing another box of winter compositions, put them outside."

Vaun opened the door.

Lena Ardis was kneeling on the floor between two low shelves with a bundle of tied exercise books in her arms. She looked to be in her middle fifties, dark-haired except for a broad silver streak above her left temple, with ink on three fingers and spectacles pushed up into her hair.

She looked at Vaun's judicial collar.

Then Serrat's black one.

Then Ilyan.

The exercise books went very still in her arms.

"This isn't about the children."

"No," Vaun said.

Lena stood.

"My father?"

Nobody had said his name.

Vaun closed the door behind them.

"Oren Savel told us he gave you some of Examiner Ardis's old papers."

Lena's face changed in a way Ilyan recognized immediately.

Not surprise.

Calculation.

"He gave me a box."

"Do you still have it?"

"Some of it."

Vaun waited.

Lena set the exercise books on a desk.

"You're going to need a better question."

Thessa glanced at Ilyan.

He almost smiled.

Vaun did not.

"Did the box contain Savel's desk notes concerning Emergency Substitution Review?"

"Yes."

"Do you still have those?"

"Yes."

"Have you altered, recopied or separated them?"

"Separated, yes. Altered, no."

"Why separated?"

"Because my father was a procedural examiner for forty-one years and believed throwing away paper was a character defect. The box contained tax notices, two recipes, a list of boots he meant to have repaired, notes from three unrelated hearings, letters from my mother and enough obsolete forms to build a small house. I sorted it."

Serrat said, "By what method?"

Lena looked at him over the top of her spectacles.

"Archivally."

Thessa coughed into her hand.

Ilyan looked at the floor.

Vaun's mouth moved by perhaps half a degree.

"Can you show us the substitution-review material?"

Lena did not answer immediately.

Instead she crossed the room to a narrow cabinet and unlocked it with a key from a chain beneath her collar.

Inside were six document boxes.

She touched the third.

Then stopped.

"Before I give you anything, I want to know whether my father is being accused of something."

There it was.

Not obstruction.

A daughter.

Vaun answered her as one.

"No accusation has been made against him. We have evidence that he participated in a technical review before Silver Night and later became concerned about how substituted credentials performed during a west-tower review. We are trying to establish what he learned and what he did with it."

Lena's fingers remained on the box.

"Silver Night."

"Yes."

Her eyes moved to Ilyan.

He had become accustomed to that moment. Recognition did not always arrive at his face. Sometimes it arrived at his surname before anyone spoke it.

"Valeric?"

"Ilyan Valeric."

She studied him.

"My father disliked your uncle."

Ilyan's stomach tightened.

"Teren?"

"He disliked his filing habits."

Thessa made a small sound that might have been a laugh dying of exhaustion.

Lena continued, "He liked him personally. Said he was one of the few custody men who would tell an examiner to get his tea off restricted paper and mean it."

That matched Savel almost word for word.

Ilyan hated the relief that moved through him.

He let it exist anyway.

"Did he ever say Teren knew what the work was for?"

Vaun looked at him.

Not a rebuke.

A reminder.

Lena shook her head.

"Not to me."

Ilyan stopped there.

She lifted the box.

"There's a reading table in the next room. No ink. Pencil only. Gloves if Serrat insists, though my father would rise from his grave to complain about people losing their grip on paper."

"Clean hands are sufficient," Serrat said.

"He would have liked you."

"Unlikely."

"Then he definitely would have liked you."

---

The papers were not a revelation.

That was the first important thing about them.

They were work.

Pel Ardis had written in a compact slant, heavily abbreviated and apparently convinced that vowels were a luxury supplied to other departments. Savel's hand was broader and more legible. Several pages were nothing more than dates, room numbers, request codes and complaints about missing attachments.

Ilyan felt his shoulders loosen as the first half hour passed.

Not because the papers were harmless.

Because they were ordinary enough to be believable.

Nobody had written *we are preparing to falsify credentials for a massacre* in a margin.

Ardis had written things like:

**Need defect continuity when parent object absent. Veyr says CR-88 proves secondary carry possible. Ask whether lawful reproduction can be distinguished from counterfeit reproduction after sealing.**

And:

**Contingency wants field-verifiable result, no destructive opening. Problem: examiner at receiving end cannot know production chain. Only object.**

And, underlined twice:

**If validation tests only the inherited flaw, then a copied flaw becomes validation.**

Ilyan read that sentence three times.

Thessa leaned over his shoulder without touching the page.

"That's the problem."

"Yes."

Serrat was reading a different sheet.

Vaun stood beside him.

Lena sat at the far end of the table with her hands wrapped around a cup of tea she had forgotten to drink.

Ilyan turned the page.

A date.

Nine days before Revision Seventeen was filed.

Ardis had written:

**Pushed back on broad language. Emergency substitution should require secondary witness check where parent authorization absent. Secretariat says delay defeats emergency purpose. Asked Veyr whether physical defect inheritance alone sufficient. He said technically sufficient to establish method consistency, not provenance. Important distinction.**

Ilyan looked across the table.

"Serrat."

The examiner came over.

Ilyan pointed.

Serrat read it once.

Then again.

"Veyr warned them."

Vaun said, "Ardis recorded that Veyr warned them."

"Right."

Ilyan rubbed his thumb against the side of his forefinger.

Method consistency was not provenance.

The same distinction Jalen had made when examining Eren Vale's credential.

Not because Jalen knew this note existed.

Because competent people looking at the same technical problem could reach the same limit.

Vaun asked Lena, "Did your father ever discuss this language with you?"

"Not before Silver Night."

"After?"

Lena looked at the page.

"He said he'd helped build a door that could not tell who walked through it."

Nobody spoke.

She frowned.

"Those were his words. I was young enough to think he meant an actual door."

Ilyan thought of the west service gate.

For one irrational second he wanted the metaphor to mean that gate too.

It did not.

A credential system was a door in the sense Ardis meant. Nothing more was earned.

"When did he say that?" Vaun asked.

"After the funerals. Maybe a month. He was sleeping badly."

"Did he name Silver Night?"

"Yes."

"Did he say his work caused it?"

"No."

Lena's voice sharpened.

"He said he didn't know. That was what frightened him."

Ilyan understood that better than he wanted to.

He turned another page.

Savel's desk notes began where Ardis's became less orderly.

There were copies of request times. A list of people present at two sessions. Tavian Veyr. Pel Ardis. Teren Valeric. Oren Savel.

Teren's name had no annotation beside it except **custody / keys**.

On the next sheet, Ardis had drawn a line between three dates.

Technical request.

Revision filing.

West-tower credential issuance.

The last date sat four days before Silver Night.

Beside it:

**Need actual issuance register. Were these ours?**

Ilyan's pulse climbed.

"What west-tower credential issuance?"

Serrat was already reading.

"We don't know."

Vaun leaned closer.

The next note answered only part of it.

**Post-event: substituted credentials recovered from west-tower access review show defect consistency beyond expected variation. Need originals compared to bench examples. Asked Contingency for issuance register. No response.**

Then, two lines lower:

**Savel says expense duplicates may preserve chair authorization. River audit after retention period.**

Damaris.

Again.

Ilyan looked toward the window as if professional instinct might finally summon her.

This time there was a knock at the archive door.

Lena rose.

A school clerk entered carrying a folded judicial message.

"For Judge Vaun. River Ledger runner."

Thessa looked at Ilyan.

"Don't."

"I didn't say anything."

"Your face did."

Vaun opened the message.

Read it.

Then handed it to Ilyan.

Damaris's handwriting filled the lower half beneath an archivist's formal notation.

**Found the monthly packet. Contingency Secretariat, Bloomtide 264. Duplicate countersignature survives. Personal hand, not office stamp. Jalen is comparing against known administrative specimens. Do not come running; the archive closes nothing merely because you are impatient. — D.**

Below that, squeezed into the margin:

**Rhyl says this is me being kind. I dispute the characterization.**

Ilyan laughed once.

It escaped him before he could stop it.

Lena looked offended on behalf of the dead.

"Sorry."

"Good news?"

"Potentially useful bureaucracy."

She considered that.

"My father would have approved."

---

Damaris had found the packet because Maeryn refused to let her search for it.

"You are an observer," Maeryn said, without looking up from the bridge reconciliation draft. "Not an archive warrant with hair."

Damaris stood beside her aunt's desk holding Vaun's preservation request.

"I have authorization."

"The archive has authorization. You have an interest. Those are different things."

"You enjoy this."

"Immensely."

Damaris sat.

The River Ledger audit archive occupied two underground levels beneath the eastern counting hall, where temperature and humidity were kept stable enough that the clerks complained more reliably than the paper. Vaun's request had gone to the proper custodian. Damaris had been permitted to identify the likely accounting series because she knew what Savel had described and because her current committee work gave her legitimate familiarity with the archive's transport-expense classifications.

Then she had been told to wait.

So she waited.

Badly.

Rhyl arrived twenty minutes later carrying two cups of spiced milk and a paper packet of seed cakes.

Maeryn looked at him.

"You aren't an archive warrant either."

"I brought food."

"That improves your standing considerably."

He handed Damaris a cup.

She took it and caught his wrist before he could pull away.

"You're late."

"You didn't invite me."

"A technicality."

He bent and kissed her forehead.

Maeryn continued reading as though young adults had not been kissing in offices since the invention of offices.

Damaris tugged Rhyl down into the chair beside hers.

"They found Ardis's daughter."

"Alive?"

"The daughter, yes. Ardis remains inconveniently dead."

"Rude of him."

"Extremely."

Rhyl opened the seed cakes.

Damaris took one.

"Ilyan is going to climb through the archive wall if this takes another hour."

"No, he won't."

"Metaphorically."

"Thessa will sit on him."

Damaris pictured it.

"Probably."

Maeryn turned a page.

"If you two intend to become unbearable, do it quietly."

Damaris smiled into her cup.

A clerk arrived with a grey packet sealed in fresh preservation tape.

The smile vanished.

The packet contained routine misery: catering receipts, carriage reimbursements, copying fees, emergency room rentals, messenger charges, seal wax, two disputed lodging claims and an expense for six replacement lockplates that nobody had bothered to explain.

Then the countersignature slip.

The approving office was the Dmitrigical Tribunal Contingency Secretariat.

The procedure code matched Emergency Substitution Review.

The amount was small enough to be insulting.

Three technical sessions. Two archive transfers. One credential bench fee.

At the bottom, beside the authorization line, sat a personal countersignature.

Not a full signature.

A compact administrative mark used for recurring approvals.

Damaris did not know it.

Maeryn did.

Her aunt's face did not change much.

That was how Damaris knew it mattered.

"Who?"

Maeryn looked at her.

"I am not answering from memory."

Damaris felt irritation flare.

Then she recognized the discipline underneath it.

"You recognize it."

"I think I recognize it."

"From where?"

"Old grain-contingency releases. Possibly."

"Valeric?"

Maeryn's gaze sharpened.

"Do not fish with surnames."

Rhyl, beside Damaris, said nothing.

His knee remained against hers.

Maeryn called for Jalen.

The forensic accountant arrived with his coat half buttoned and a plum in one hand.

"If this is another bridge-hour problem, I resign from the family."

"Compare this mark," Maeryn said.

He stopped chewing.

Damaris watched him work.

Not dramatically.

He requested three old administrative specimen books. Then two more. He compared stroke order, pressure, abbreviation habit and the tiny backward hook beneath the terminal line. He rejected the first apparent match. He rejected the second.

After forty minutes he placed two sheets side by side.

"Same hand," he said.

Damaris looked down.

The known specimen came from a grain-contingency release nineteen years earlier.

The name printed beneath it was not Teren Valeric.

It was **Corren Valeric**.

Ilyan's father.

For a moment Damaris heard nothing except the archive ventilation moving air through the room.

Rhyl's hand closed around hers under the table.

Maeryn did not touch her.

She did something more useful.

"Confidence?"

Jalen looked again.

"High. Not absolute. I want the original specimen book and a second examiner before anyone says the word proven."

Damaris swallowed.

"Send that. Exactly that."

She wrote the message to Vaun.

Then stopped before the final line.

Ilyan would read his father's name in a room full of other people if she sent it now.

He deserved the fact.

He also deserved not to receive it as a clever note beneath a bureaucratic joke.

Damaris tore the sheet in half.

Rhyl watched her.

"What?"

"The packet survives. The mark survives. Jalen is comparing it. That's enough for the runner."

Maeryn looked at her for a long moment.

Then nodded once.

Damaris sent the shorter message.

The name could wait until Vaun had the second comparison.

Not because she intended to protect Ilyan from truth.

Because truth did not become more truthful when delivered carelessly.

---

The second comparison arrived near dusk.

By then the Seven had begun gathering at the canal eating house Lyrion had found, though nobody had yet managed to call the evening ordinary.

Mireth and Caelen occupied one end of the table and were pretending not to be touching beneath it. They were failing so thoroughly that the pretense had become ceremonial. Lyrion had acquired a bottle of pale summer wine. Thessa sat beside Ilyan. Rhyl and Damaris arrived last.

Damaris took off her cloak.

Ilyan saw her face.

The room changed.

Not for everyone.

For him.

He stood.

"What happened?"

Vaun entered behind them.

That answered enough.

The judge carried a sealed River Ledger copy sleeve.

Nobody reached for the wine.

Vaun sat.

"The Contingency expense packet survived."

Ilyan remained standing.

"The authorization?"

"A duplicate personal countersignature survived with it. River Ledger performed a handwriting comparison against known administrative specimens. A second examiner repeated it."

He knew before she said the name.

Damaris could see that.

Maybe because his father had already occupied too much of the shape around the evidence. Maybe because hope was not the same thing as expectation.

Vaun said, "Both examiners assess the mark as highly consistent with Corren Valeric's administrative countersignature."

Ilyan sat down.

Nobody spoke.

Thessa's hand moved across the table.

Stopped before touching him.

Ilyan looked at it.

Then put his hand over hers.

Damaris watched his face.

No collapse.

No revelation of a father newly transformed into a monster.

Ilyan already knew his father had helped engineer Silver Night.

This was different.

Smaller.

Worse in its own way.

A line connecting a known crime to a particular procedural choice before the crime occurred.

"What exactly did he authorize?" Ilyan asked.

Vaun slid the certified copy toward him.

"The expense packet covers the Emergency Substitution Review sessions, archive transfers and credential bench work. The countersignature authorizes expenditure and procedure under Contingency authority. It does not tell us whether Corren originated the request, dictated Revision Seventeen, selected the west-tower credentials, or knew how the resulting method would be used."

Ilyan read the page.

"But he signed for the work."

"Yes."

His thumb rested beside the copied mark.

Damaris sat opposite him.

"I almost sent you the name by runner."

He looked up.

"Why didn't you?"

"Because Jalen had one comparison and wanted another. And because I didn't want you learning this from a joke in my handwriting."

For several seconds he said nothing.

Then: "Thank you."

Rhyl's shoulder touched hers.

Lyrion poured wine.

Not as celebration.

Because there were seven cups and something had to happen next.

Mireth asked, "What did Ardis leave?"

Ilyan told them.

The notes. Veyr's warning that method consistency was not provenance. Ardis pushing for a secondary witness check. The Secretariat rejecting delay. The post-Silver-Night concern that west-tower substituted credentials had behaved too well. The request for an issuance register that had apparently gone unanswered.

Caelen's expression hardened at that.

"West tower."

"Yes."

"Our west tower?"

"We don't know yet."

Caelen nodded.

He did not make the next leap for him.

Thessa said, "Ardis wrote 'west-tower access review.' We need the issuance register before we decide which west tower or which access event."

"Yes," Ilyan said.

Damaris looked at Vaun.

"And the die?"

"Tribunal receiving book tomorrow morning."

"Teren?"

Ilyan looked down at his father's countersignature.

"After the receiving book."

Nobody challenged him.

He was no longer delaying because he feared the conversation.

He was sequencing evidence.

That mattered.

Outside, canal lamps came on one by one in the Bloomtide dusk. Their reflections stretched in the black water and broke whenever a boat passed.

The food arrived.

For a while nobody touched it.

Then Thessa nudged Ilyan's plate toward him.

"Eat."

He looked at her.

"You are becoming repetitive."

"You are becoming reliably feedable."

A laugh moved around the table, small and tired.

Ilyan picked up his fork.

His father's mark remained beneath Vaun's seal.

Pel Ardis's notes remained in a school archive among children's compositions and obsolete forms.

Tal's die remained, perhaps, behind a tribunal vault door.

And somewhere in an issuance register that nobody at the table had yet seen was the answer to a narrower question than the one they had begun with:

Who had been given substituted credentials before Silver Night, and where had those credentials opened a door?
