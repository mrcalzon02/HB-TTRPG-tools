# Chapter Ten — What the Oath Keeps

Teren slept badly and lied about it with no skill at all.

Ilyan knew because his uncle arrived at the Hall before breakfast wearing yesterday's coat, carrying the same basket with three jars of plum preserve left in it, and announced that he had enjoyed an excellent night.

Examiner Sael looked up from the morning register.

"You are wearing two different boots."

Teren looked down.

He was.

One was black. The other was a dark brown close enough to have survived a poorly lit bedroom.

"Fashion," Teren said.

Sael returned to the register.

"Naturally."

Ilyan laughed before he could stop himself.

Teren pointed at him.

"You are supposed to be distressed on my behalf."

"I contain multitudes."

"You contain insolence."

The ordinary exchange helped more than either of them admitted.

It did not change the fact that a lawful oath-working had been sitting inside Teren's mind for eighteen years because the people who were supposed to review it had failed to do so. It did not change the sealed witness category attached to that working. It did not answer who had moved him away from the infirmary before the scheduled examination, or whether anyone had understood what the transfer would interrupt.

It did, however, give them something to do with their mouths while they waited for Judge Vaun.

She arrived with Enara and a packet of blank request forms.

Teren eyed the forms.

"Those look threatening."

"They are paper."

"That has not prevented paper from ruining my week."

Vaun sat at the long review table.

"Today we are not trying to recover your memory."

Teren's humor disappeared.

"I know."

"Say why."

He looked irritated, which Ilyan suspected was precisely why she had asked.

"Because the compartment still protects a category that may include a living witness. Because the release condition requires authorized review. Because tearing at it to satisfy me could expose someone who has done nothing to deserve that."

"Good."

Teren rubbed both hands over his face.

"I am going to develop a pathological response to that word."

Enara put a small cup of tea beside him.

"Drink."

He obeyed.

Vaun divided the morning into three questions.

Who ordered Teren's transfer from Eiravel Memorial Infirmary?

Which office inherited responsibility for the release review after he left?

And whether the oath itself contained a lawful mechanism for review without exposing the protected witness identity.

The third question belonged to Mireth and Scholar Silae.

The first two belonged to records.

Ilyan was beginning to understand that most investigations became less romantic the closer one got to the answer.

There were fewer midnight revelations than stories promised.

There were more filing systems.

---

Mireth arrived with leaves in her hair.

Not decorative leaves.

Actual leaves.

Caelen followed her into the Hall carrying her satchel and looking far too pleased with himself.

Ilyan stared at them.

Mireth stopped.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"You have the face."

"What face?"

"The one Damaris complains about."

Caelen set the satchel on the table.

"She fell into a hedge."

Mireth turned on him.

"You were not asked."

"He was going to invent something better."

"I was not."

"You absolutely were."

Mireth pulled a twig from her hair and threw it at Caelen.

He caught it.

"The Green Covenant keeps its older oath books in a garden archive," she said. "The path is being repaired. A gardener had placed a plank across the mud. The plank objected to me personally."

Ilyan looked at her boots.

They were clean.

Caelen's were not.

Mireth saw him notice.

"He caught me."

Caelen sat beside her.

"Mostly."

She elbowed him without looking.

Silae entered a moment later and saved them all from further inquiry.

The older jurist carried no leaves and no visible sympathy.

She placed three copied pages on the table.

"Emergency category compartments," she said. "Contemporary doctrine, doctrine in force eighteen years ago, and the amendment adopted after Silver Night."

Vaun read the old rule first.

The working could be released in one of three ways: fulfillment of the stated condition; consent of every living authority named in the rider; or judicial substitution when an authority was dead, dissolved, unreachable, or legally incapable of acting.

Ilyan looked at Teren.

"So this can be reviewed without finding Mara."

Silae's gaze sharpened.

"Without finding the protected person. Do not make the category narrower than the record allows."

He corrected himself.

"Without finding the protected person."

"Yes."

Teren leaned forward.

"Then why wasn't it?"

Silae turned to the second page.

Because the old rule had assumed the office surviving the emergency would initiate substitution.

If an oath named three authorities and one died, the remaining office was expected to seek a replacement. If two offices merged, the successor inherited the obligation. If the protected category expired, the responsible custodian was expected to certify that fact and schedule release.

Expected.

The word did a great deal of work.

"There was no automatic notice?" Ilyan asked.

"Not under the old rule."

"No periodic review?"

"Not unless the rider required one."

Teren stared at the page.

"So if everyone who remembered the obligation stopped being responsible for me—"

"The paper still existed," Silae said. "The duty still existed. But nothing forced the duty into someone's hands."

Mireth tapped the third page.

"The modern rule does."

After Silver Night, category compartments acquired mandatory review dates. Unreleased workings generated notices at the first interval, again at six months, and annually thereafter. Dissolution of an issuing office required transfer of every open release obligation by individual accession reference.

Caelen read the amendment date.

"Eight months after."

"Yes," Silae said.

"Because of this?"

"I don't know. The amendment cites several emergency failures. It does not name Teren."

Teren smiled without amusement.

"It would be flattering to have personally improved administrative law."

"You may have been one of many people injured by a bad rule."

"Less flattering."

"More likely."

Ilyan watched his uncle absorb that.

There was a strange cruelty in discovering that an intimate injury might not have required an intimate enemy.

A person could lose eighteen years of memory because three offices each possessed one-third of a duty and none possessed the whole alarm.

But the transfer still mattered.

Somebody had moved Teren before his scheduled examination.

That could have been necessary.

It could also have been the moment the obligation fell between institutions.

Vaun asked Silae to certify the historical doctrine and the modern amendment. Silae agreed, then looked at Teren.

"If the current custodian and the court can establish the protected category remains protected, a substituted review can test whether the compartment can be narrowed, partially released, or released under continued non-disclosure."

"Meaning I might remember what I did without learning who I protected."

"Possibly."

"Possibly is becoming my least favorite word."

Mireth reached across the table and squeezed his hand.

Teren looked surprised.

Then he squeezed back.

"Thank you."

She nodded.

No speech followed.

They had all become better at that.

---

The transfer order arrived before noon.

It was disappointingly ordinary.

Teren had been moved from the Eiravel annex to a convalescent ward maintained by the Hall of Veils for injured Order personnel during the Silver Night emergency. Not a Valeric estate. Not a hidden Crown site. Not a remote witness house.

Three streets away.

Ilyan read the destination twice.

"He moved three streets."

Sael nodded.

"Apparently."

"And that was enough?"

"Jurisdiction is capable of crossing much shorter distances."

Vaun gave Sael a look.

He returned to the paper.

The transfer had been medically justified. Eiravel needed beds for burn and crush casualties arriving from the Academy district. Teren no longer required infirmary treatment. The Hall had beds, staff, and its own physicians.

The order moved fourteen patients that afternoon.

Teren was one of them.

No one had singled him out for a private carriage.

No family seal appeared.

No Valeric instruction appeared.

Ilyan felt a theory die quietly and was almost grateful.

Then Sael turned the page.

The rider continuation had not gone with Teren.

The infirmary returned it under sealed custody to Joint Emergency Coordination, exactly as its medical note said.

Teren went to the Hall ward.

The obligation went elsewhere.

"There," Vaun said.

One word.

Enough.

The transfer had split patient from release instrument.

The Hall physicians received an exhausted Order officer recovering from smoke, cuts and prolonged wakefulness. His ordinary treatment summary mentioned a protected mnemonic procedure but did not contain the category or release rider. It instructed them not to probe protected memory without authorization.

They obeyed.

Joint Emergency Coordination received the sealed rider continuation and still possessed the obligation to arrange review.

For six days.

Then Joint Emergency Coordination dissolved.

The temporary body had never been intended to survive the crisis. Its functions were distributed among permanent offices as the capital returned to ordinary administration.

Vaun requested the dissolution schedule.

Sael was already moving toward the archive door.

"I know where the copy is."

Ilyan followed.

Sael stopped.

"You do not."

"I can help."

"You can wait."

"Why?"

"Because you have not eaten."

Ilyan stared at him.

From the table behind them, Teren said, "I like him."

"You are wearing different boots."

"Irrelevant."

Mireth laughed.

Caelen handed Ilyan half a roll.

"Eat."

Ilyan took it.

Adulthood, he had discovered, involved a surprising number of people policing one another's lunches.

---

Thessa found him on the Hall steps with the second half of the roll in his mouth.

She was wearing Warden grey and carrying a rolled plan beneath one arm.

"Romantic," she said.

He swallowed.

"You should see me with soup."

"I have. It is why I keep returning."

She kissed him anyway.

A pair of clerks came down the steps behind them and did not so much as slow.

That had changed too.

A week ago Ilyan would have noticed being seen.

Now he noticed only that Thessa's lower lip was cold from the wind and that she had a smear of charcoal near her wrist.

"Wall?" he asked.

"Wall."

"Alive?"

"Technically. We found the old drain."

"Was it where the plan said?"

"No."

"Of course not."

"It was where the eighty-year-old mason said it would be."

"You had an eighty-year-old mason available?"

"We had six."

He smiled.

She leaned against him for a moment, shoulder to shoulder.

"How is Teren?"

"Angry. Tired. Wearing two boots."

"Two boots is traditional."

"Different colors."

"Advanced."

He told her about the transfer.

Three streets. Fourteen patients. No Valeric intervention in the order. The rider going back to Joint Emergency Coordination while Teren went to the Hall ward.

Thessa listened without interrupting.

"So the man and the duty separated."

"Yes."

"And the temporary office dissolved."

"Six days later."

She looked toward the Hall doors.

"That sounds stupid enough to be real."

"Silae says the law was changed afterward."

"That sounds even more real."

He laughed.

She did not immediately join him.

"My captain asked again."

His amusement faded.

"About the old movement request?"

"About whether I am still assisting the Hall."

"What did you say?"

"Yes."

"And?"

"She wants the request formalized if I pull anything else. Not because she thinks I'm doing something wrong. Because if another office asks why Western Warden records are being used in an eighteen-year-old inquiry, she wants an answer with a file number."

Ilyan nodded.

It was reasonable.

That was the uncomfortable part.

"Can Vaun do that?"

"Probably. I told my captain I'd bring the request tomorrow."

"You didn't have to keep helping."

Thessa turned her head.

"Do not become noble at me."

"I wasn't."

"You were preparing to."

"I was going to say your job matters."

"It does. So does this. Those facts can occupy the same room."

She caught the front of his coat and pulled him closer.

"Also, I enjoy having an excuse to interrogate you about whether you ate."

"Abuse of authority."

"Terrible."

He kissed her.

The rolled Warden plan pressed awkwardly between them.

Thessa moved it to the other hand without breaking the kiss.

Ilyan admired competence wherever he found it.

When she drew back, she touched her forehead to his.

"Tonight?"

"If your wall permits."

"The wall has lost voting privileges."

"Good."

She left for the western works office.

Ilyan watched her cross the courtyard until she disappeared through the gate.

Then he went back inside before Sael could come looking for him.

---

The dissolution schedule was worse than the transfer order.

Not sinister.

Worse.

It was tidy.

Joint Emergency Coordination had existed for eleven days after Silver Night. At dissolution, its functions were distributed by subject.

Casualty reconciliation went to the Crown emergency secretary.

Protected witness relocation returned to Contingency.

Credential technical review went to the Joint Office for Order and Credential Integrity.

Medical claims went to the emergency treasury desk.

Personnel recovery returned to the affected Orders.

Security custody went to whichever permanent office owned the underlying material.

The schedule was sensible if one imagined every file contained one kind of problem.

Teren's did not.

His rider continuation concerned a person who was simultaneously an Order officer, a former temporary emergency chair, a medical patient, a holder of protected witness-routing knowledge, and one half of a credential-preservation action.

The dissolution clerk had classified the sealed rider by its cover category.

**Protected witness continuity.**

It went to Contingency.

Contingency opened only the outer custody leaf, saw that the protected receiving supervisor named in the rider was Pel Ardis, and discovered he was dead.

The inner category remained sealed.

Under the old rule, Contingency should then have sought judicial substitution.

Instead, a clerk sent a query to the Joint Office for Order and Credential Integrity because the outer leaf also referenced credential preservation.

The Joint Office answered that it held the technical discrepancy but not the protected witness authority.

The file went back.

Contingency then noted that the patient was an Order officer and sent a release-responsibility query to the Hall.

The Hall answered that it held the patient but not the rider and therefore could not authorize access to the protected category.

The query went back again.

Ilyan read the three exchanges in silence.

Teren stood behind him.

"They asked each other."

"Yes."

"Nobody ignored it."

"No."

"They just kept answering correctly."

Sael's expression was bleak.

"Within their own authority."

That was the failure.

Not a missing form.

Not one clerk throwing a file into a cellar.

Three offices had each correctly refused to exercise authority they did not possess.

No one had possessed authority to compel the three pieces back into one room.

The final Contingency notation read:

**Hold pending successor receiving authority or judicial substitution.**

Then the accession moved into the protected dormant series.

No review date existed under the old law.

No automatic notice existed.

Ardis remained dead.

Teren recovered physically, returned home, and eventually resumed a life in which he did not remember that the obligation existed.

The file waited.

For eighteen years.

Ilyan sat back.

"Who should have initiated substitution?"

Vaun answered from the end of the table.

"Contingency."

"So it is their failure."

"Partly."

He waited.

She slid the dissolution schedule toward him.

The temporary Joint Order chair function had not been assigned a permanent successor because it had been created for the emergency and terminated with it. The Hall received personnel responsibility for Teren, not the extinct chair's unresolved powers. The Joint Office received the credential case, not the witness rider. Contingency received the rider, but its named receiving authority was dead.

"Contingency had the file," Ilyan said.

"Yes."

"They could have sought substitution."

"Yes."

"They didn't."

"Correct."

"Then why partly?"

Vaun looked at Teren.

"Because someone marked the matter for hold rather than closure. That preserved the obligation. Someone else failed to establish a review date. The Hall treated the compartment as protected and did not violate it. The Joint Office preserved the discrepancy. Each action prevented one kind of harm while allowing another to continue."

Teren sat beside Ilyan.

"That is a very elegant way to say everyone did their job and I still lost eighteen years."

Vaun's face softened.

"Yes."

No one tried to make it better.

---

They found the transfer physician at sunset.

Not in person.

In a retirement register.

Doctor Aven Sorel had been thirty-eight during Silver Night, an Eiravel physician seconded to emergency medical-security work. He was now fifty-six, alive, retired from hospital practice, and teaching clinical oath ethics two mornings a week at a small college across the river.

The transfer order carried his signature.

So did the compartment procedure.

Teren looked at the name for a long time.

"Nothing."

Enara said, "That is allowed."

"I know."

"You sound disappointed."

"I am becoming tired of having permission not to remember my own life."

She accepted that without correction.

Vaun requested an interview for the following morning.

Before the runner left, Teren stopped him.

"Wait."

The young man froze.

Teren looked at Vaun.

"Can we ask one thing now?"

"What?"

"Whether Sorel remembers why I was transferred before the release examination. Not what I knew. Not who the protected person was. Just why I left."

Vaun considered it.

Then she added the question to the sealed note.

The runner departed.

Teren watched the door close.

"Thank you."

"It is a proper question."

"I know. Thank you anyway."

---

Ilyan reached home late.

Thessa was asleep on his bed exactly as predicted, diagonally, one bare foot outside the blanket and his pillow trapped under her arm.

He stood in the doorway and looked at her.

The lamp on the bedside table was still burning low. Her Warden coat hung from the chair. A folded engineering plan lay on the floor where it had apparently lost an argument with gravity.

He picked it up.

Thessa opened one eye.

"Do not read that."

"I wasn't."

"It is drainage. You will become excited and ask questions."

"I have standards."

"You spent a week excited about keys."

He set the plan on the table.

"Fair."

She lifted the blanket.

He undressed enough not to bring the Hall into bed with him and climbed in.

Thessa rolled against him immediately, warm and half asleep, one leg sliding between his. Her mouth found the side of his neck.

"You smell like archive."

"You say that as though it is not irresistible."

"I have made poor choices."

He put an arm around her.

For several minutes he listened to her breathe.

Then she said, eyes still closed, "You found something."

"How do you know?"

"You are thinking loudly."

He told her.

Not every accession number. Not every office title. Just the shape.

Teren had moved three streets with fourteen other patients. The rider had gone back to the emergency office. The emergency office dissolved. The file moved among three permanent institutions, each of which correctly refused to do the part belonging to someone else. Contingency finally held it pending a substitution nobody initiated.

Thessa was quiet.

"So no villain required."

"Not for that part."

"And maybe one anyway."

"Maybe."

"Annoying."

"Extremely."

Her fingers moved slowly across his chest.

"Does it change Teren for you?"

Ilyan thought about the question.

"Yes."

She waited.

"I kept looking at the missing memory like somebody had to have taken it from him. Now I know he gave part of it up on purpose. Then the system failed to give it back."

"That is different."

"It feels different."

"Those aren't the same statement."

He looked down at her.

She smiled against his shoulder.

"I've been spending time with you."

"Terrible influence."

"Catastrophic."

He kissed her hair.

"He made me promise not to expose the protected person even if he asks me to later."

"Good promise."

"I know."

"Hard promise."

"Yes."

She shifted higher until their faces were level.

"Then remember he made it while he was frightened and wanted his memory back. That's the version of him who gets a vote tomorrow too."

Ilyan stared at her.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"That was useful."

"I am occasionally magnificent."

"Occasionally?"

She bit his lower lip, lightly.

"Careful."

He kissed her before she could continue.

The investigation remained where it was.

That was one of the things he was learning to value about kissing her. Desire did not solve anything. It did not need to. Her body against his was not a clue. The warmth under the blanket was not an argument. The soft sound she made when his hand settled at her waist proved nothing except that she wanted him there.

For a little while, that was enough.

Later, when the lamp had gone out and the room was dark, Thessa said, "Your uncle's two boots are still funny."

Ilyan laughed into the pillow.

"Go to sleep."

"Make me."

He did his best.

---

Doctor Sorel's answer reached the Hall shortly after dawn.

He remembered Teren.

Not because Teren had been important.

Because Teren had argued.

The old physician's note was brief.

**Transfer was ordinary bed redistribution. Patient objected because protected review remained incomplete. I advised that medical transfer did not terminate review obligation. Patient requested written assurance. Assurance entered on rider return slip.**

Ilyan read the line twice.

Teren read it three times.

"I knew," he said.

Enara stood beside him.

"Then."

"I knew the review still had to happen."

"Yes."

"I asked them to write it down."

"Yes."

Teren sat very still.

Something in his face eased.

Not relief exactly.

Recognition without memory.

The knowledge that the man he had been had noticed the danger and tried to protect himself from it.

Ilyan understood why that mattered.

Teren had not wandered passively out of the infirmary and forgotten to care.

He had objected.

He had asked for paper.

The paper had survived.

The system had still failed him.

But he had been there inside his own life, making choices.

Vaun turned to the attached copy of the rider return slip.

Sorel's assurance was present.

Beneath it was a receiving mark from Joint Emergency Coordination.

And beneath that, added two days later, a custody notation directing the black routing selector to be surrendered separately from the sealed rider because the temporary chair authority had ended.

Ilyan felt the room narrow.

Teren leaned closer.

The surrender instruction named no recipient.

It did contain a second accession reference.

A selector custody receipt.

The same class of record that had eventually shown **C.V.** returning the black key.

Chapter Nine had left them asking how Corren came to possess it.

Now they had the first document describing the moment it was supposed to leave Teren's office.

Vaun put one finger beside the accession number.

"Next."

Ilyan nodded.

Not because he had a theory.

Because this time they had a receipt to find.
