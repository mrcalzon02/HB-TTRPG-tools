# Chapter Seven — Leaf Seventy-Three

The Crown kept its emergency appointments in a room with painted birds.

Ilyan had expected iron.

Iron doors, perhaps. Bars. A clerk with a face designed by generations of people being told no. Something suitably severe for the place where eighteen-year-old secrets went to acquire dust and constitutional dignity.

Instead the antechamber of the Crown Secretary's archive had a pale green ceiling covered in swallows. They flew between painted branches in little blue-black flocks, their wings picked out with enough silver that they shifted when the morning light moved across the plaster.

He stared at them while Judge Vaun argued quietly with a deputy registrar.

Teren sat beside him on a narrow bench and did not offer conversation.

That was considerate.

They had ridden most of the night, slept badly at a post house, changed coaches before dawn and reached the capital while the bakers were still throwing wash water into the gutters. Ilyan had eaten because Thessa would ask. He had also washed, shaved and changed his collar because appearing before Crown records looking as though he had been dragged behind the coach would not make leaf seventy-three more truthful.

It only felt as though it should.

The deputy registrar disappeared through a walnut door.

Vaun returned to the bench.

"The book exists."

Ilyan looked up.

"You saw it?"

"I saw the accession entry."

Teren rubbed both hands over his face.

"That is not the same thing."

"No."

Vaun sat opposite them.

She had changed nothing about herself since Larkfen except her gloves. Ilyan suspected judges possessed some private discipline by which fatigue could be converted into sharper tailoring.

"The appointment leaf is restricted under an emergency identity provision that has never been formally lifted," she said. "The registrar agrees my existing order reaches the office and date. He does not agree it reaches the protected-routing identity attached to the office."

Ilyan looked at the walnut door.

"So we came back for another argument."

"We came back for the correct argument."

Teren gave a tired sound that might have been a laugh.

Ilyan did not.

"How long?"

"The registrar is asking Crown counsel."

"How long does Crown counsel take?"

"When awake?"

"They are awake."

Vaun glanced toward the windows.

"That is an assumption."

Teren laughed properly this time.

Ilyan looked at both of them and, despite himself, smiled.

Then the walnut door opened.

The deputy registrar returned with an elderly woman in a plum-colored morning coat, her silver hair braided close to her skull and a chain of office hanging from one shoulder. She carried no papers.

"Judge Vaun."

Vaun stood.

So did Teren and Ilyan.

"Counsel Serev."

The woman looked at Ilyan first.

Not rudely.

Not kindly either.

Professionally.

"Lord Valeric."

"Ilyan is sufficient."

"It usually is until someone wants access to Crown emergency records."

He had no answer to that.

Serev turned to Vaun.

"Your order reaches the appointment. It does not reach subsequent protected witness routing. The difficulty is that the leaf contains both."

"Can they be separated?"

"Physically, no. Legally, yes."

Vaun waited.

Serev looked almost disappointed not to be interrupted.

"The registrar will permit inspection of the appointment block and countersignature. The protected-routing notation beneath it will remain masked. No copy leaves the room today. You may take a certified transcription of the visible fields after comparison by two Crown clerks."

"Accepted."

Ilyan looked at Vaun.

She did not look back.

He understood anyway.

Do not bargain past what they needed simply because the door had opened.

Serev's gaze moved to Teren.

"You are named in the historical custody chain."

"Apparently I spent my youth making trouble for my old age."

"Most people do. Yours was unusually well indexed."

Teren inclined his head.

Serev led them through the walnut door.

The room beyond was smaller than the antechamber and much uglier. The painted birds ended at the threshold. Here there were grey shelves, grey tables, grey document boxes and two clerks whose expressions suggested color itself had failed a records examination.

A book waited on a felt cradle.

It was bound in dark blue leather, the Crown seal stamped so deeply into the cover that the edges had cracked white with age.

Ilyan stopped three steps away.

Book II.

Bloomtide, 264 S.M.

Nothing about it looked important enough.

He was beginning to distrust that feeling.

One of the clerks checked Vaun's order. The other checked Ilyan and Teren against the admission list. Counsel Serev signed the viewing register. Then the first clerk turned the pages.

Sixty-eight.

Sixty-nine.

Seventy.

Ilyan heard paper move and the blood in his ears.

Seventy-one.

Seventy-two.

The clerk stopped.

A narrow grey mask had already been prepared and laid over the lower half of the next page.

Leaf seventy-three.

The appointment block remained visible.

Ilyan read the first line.

**Emergency Joint Order Liaison Chair — West-Tower Review.**

The second line named the predecessor.

**Pel Ardis — recused.**

The third line contained the successor.

For several seconds Ilyan could not make the letters into a person.

Then they became familiar.

Not his father.

Not Neral Voss.

Not some stranger waiting conveniently in the archive to become the villain of the week.

**Teren Valeric.**

Beside him, Teren stopped breathing.

Ilyan looked at his uncle.

Teren's face had emptied.

"No," he said.

Nobody moved.

The Crown clerk's hand remained beside the page.

Vaun said, "Do you recognize the appointment?"

Teren stared at it.

"No."

"Do you recognize the signature?"

The appointment carried three marks.

A Crown emergency authorization.

A Joint Order acceptance.

And beneath them, in a hand Ilyan had seen on birthday notes, weapons inventories and the labels Teren used for preserves because he believed every pantry was one badly marked jar away from collapse:

**T. Valeric.**

Teren put one hand on the edge of the table.

"That's mine."

Ilyan felt something inside him go very quiet.

"You said you didn't remember taking the chair."

"I don't."

"You signed it."

"I can see that."

Serev lifted a hand.

"The page is not to be touched."

Teren's fingers had not crossed the felt, but he pulled them back anyway.

Ilyan wanted to ask six questions at once.

He asked none.

Vaun did.

"Counsel, what did the chair carry?"

Serev looked at the visible office description.

"Read what is exposed."

Vaun did.

The chair held coordination authority over Joint Order review, emergency technical custody disputes, and protected-routing disputes arising from west-tower operations. It did not grant ordinary command over Academy staff, Tribunal Contingency, or Crown security. It did grant authority to receive sealed determinations from those bodies and decide which Joint Order office was entitled to act on them.

Ilyan looked at Teren.

Teren had been the person Dethril's sealed direction was addressed to.

Not merely the office whose outer sheet he countermarked.

The person.

"You said you couldn't open it," Ilyan said.

Teren's eyes remained on the page.

"I believed I couldn't."

"You were the chair."

"According to this, yes."

"Then you could."

"According to this, yes."

The repetition made anger flash hot through Ilyan.

"Stop saying that."

Teren looked at him.

There was no defensiveness in his face.

That made it worse.

"What would you prefer I say?"

"That you remember."

"I don't."

"You remember marking the direction."

"Yes."

"You remember Ardis leaving."

"Yes."

"You remember being told a successor had protected-routing authority."

"Yes."

"And somehow the part where the successor was you is gone?"

Teren closed his eyes.

"Yes."

The word sat between them.

Ilyan hated it.

He hated the room. He hated the swallows outside. He hated leaf seventy-three for being so perfectly intact. Most of all he hated that Teren looked frightened rather than caught.

Caught would have been easier.

Vaun stepped between the question and whatever Ilyan might have done with it.

"Counsel Serev. Is there an acceptance packet?"

"There should be."

"Here?"

"Not necessarily. Emergency appointments were accepted through the appointing office, then distributed to the receiving institution. The Crown book proves the appointment was entered and accepted. It does not prove what the appointee subsequently received or read."

Ilyan laughed once without humor.

"Of course it doesn't."

Serev ignored him.

Vaun said, "Where would the acceptance packet go?"

"Joint Order administration, unless the appointment itself carried a restricted-routing rider."

Her eyes dropped toward the grey mask.

Ilyan followed them.

The hidden lower half of leaf seventy-three.

"And whether it did is under the mask," he said.

"Yes," Serev said.

Vaun did not ask to see it.

Instead she requested the certified transcription.

The two clerks began comparing the visible text line by line.

Teren moved away from the table.

Ilyan followed him into the antechamber because not following would have been impossible.

The painted swallows were still flying nowhere.

Teren stood beneath them with both hands braced on the back of the bench.

"Say it," Ilyan said.

"I don't know what to say."

"Try the truth."

Teren flinched.

Ilyan regretted it immediately.

Not because it was false.

Because he had chosen the word to hurt.

Teren noticed that too.

Of course he did.

"I remember being angry after Ardis recused," Teren said. "I remember arguing that the technical review should remain independent. I remember a sealed appointment arriving. I remember a custody direction later crossing my office. I remember believing the successor chair was someone above me."

"Why?"

"I don't know."

"Who told you?"

"I don't know."

"Did you take an oath?"

Teren looked toward the walnut door.

"Probably."

"Probably?"

"Emergency chairs swore acceptance. If I signed that, I almost certainly did."

"You don't remember swearing it."

"No."

Ilyan turned away.

The windows looked down on a Crown courtyard where gardeners were replacing rain-flattened flowers around a fountain. A cart passed under the arch with baskets of bread for some office kitchen. Two young clerks crossed the paving stones sharing an umbrella badly enough that both were getting wet.

The city had the indecency to continue.

Behind him Teren said, "You should consider the obvious possibility."

Ilyan turned back.

"Which one?"

"That I am lying."

He stared at him.

Teren's mouth tightened.

"Do not spare me the hypothesis because you love me."

There it was.

Not uncle.

Not duty.

Love.

Ilyan felt his anger change shape.

"I already considered it."

"Good."

"I don't believe it."

Teren looked almost offended.

"That is premature."

"You are criticizing my suspicion of you for being insufficient?"

"I am criticizing your conclusion."

"I haven't made one."

"You just said you don't believe I'm lying."

"I don't."

"Based on what?"

Ilyan opened his mouth.

Stopped.

Based on the way Teren looked afraid.

Based on years of being able to tell when his uncle was embarrassed, furious, evasive, delighted, lying badly about birthday gifts, lying well to creditors, or pretending not to notice Ilyan coming home drunk at seventeen.

Based on love.

Which was evidence of a kind and proof of nothing.

"You look like someone took a piece out of your head," Ilyan said.

Teren went still.

Neither spoke.

Vaun opened the walnut door.

She had heard enough to understand the silence.

"That is now a question," she said.

Ilyan looked at her.

"Not an answer."

"No."

Teren straightened.

"What do we do?"

Vaun handed Ilyan the certified transcription.

"We find the acceptance packet. Then we find out whether your memory is merely poor, professionally compartmented, deliberately altered, or being interpreted through eighteen years of later assumptions."

"Can you test memory alteration?" Ilyan asked.

"Sometimes."

"Reliably?"

"Not from a corridor conversation."

Teren gave a short nod.

"Test me."

Vaun studied him.

"That is not a casual consent."

"I know."

"Memory examination can expose unrelated material."

"I know."

"And if someone interfered with you eighteen years ago, a crude attempt to prove it may destroy what remains."

That stopped him.

Vaun continued.

"We do the records first."

Teren looked at Ilyan.

Ilyan nodded.

Records first.

He was beginning to understand why institutions had procedures even when every part of him wanted to skip them.

---

Thessa was waiting outside the Hall of Veils when they returned.

She was also covered in mud.

Not metaphorically.

Actual brown road mud streaked the lower half of her Warden coat, one knee and the side of one boot. Her hair had been braided for work and had partly escaped. She held a folded survey map under one arm and a paper parcel in the other.

Ilyan stopped on the steps.

For one absurd second, leaf seventy-three vanished.

Thessa saw his face.

Her smile disappeared.

She did not ask what happened until they were close enough that the clerks entering behind him had passed.

"Bad?"

"Complicated."

"That is usually your word for bad with paperwork."

Teren came up the steps behind them.

Thessa looked at him.

Then back at Ilyan.

Her eyes sharpened.

"Oh."

Ilyan almost laughed.

"How did you get all that from looking at him?"

"He looks like someone kicked him in the soul."

Teren sighed.

"I preferred the old days when you were intimidated by me."

"I was never intimidated by you."

"You hid behind Ilyan when you were twelve."

"You had a sword."

"I was teaching swordsmanship."

"Exactly."

The exchange did what neither man had managed in the archive.

It made Teren look like himself for a moment.

Thessa handed Ilyan the paper parcel.

"Food."

"I ate."

"Wonderful. This is also food."

He looked inside.

Two honey cakes.

"You crossed the city from a failed retaining wall to feed me?"

"No. I crossed the city because my briefing ended here and because I wanted to see you. The cakes were on the way. Do not make them carry more evidentiary weight than they can survive."

Vaun, three steps below them, looked up.

Thessa smiled sweetly.

The judge kept walking.

Ilyan kissed Thessa.

He did not plan to.

He simply put one hand against the clean part of her coat, bent toward her and found her mouth.

She made a small surprised sound and then caught his collar.

The kiss lasted long enough for Teren to discover a passionate interest in the Hall's front doors.

When Ilyan drew back, Thessa's eyes remained half closed for a heartbeat.

"That bad," she said.

"Yes."

She touched his cheek with two muddy fingers, realized what she had done, and laughed.

"Sorry."

"You are not."

"No."

He kissed the inside of her wrist before she could move her hand.

That quieted her.

The desire between them did not solve anything.

It did remind him that the world contained things other than records left by frightened people eighteen years ago.

Thessa looked over his shoulder at Teren.

"Do you need me to leave?"

Ilyan considered the question.

"No. But I need to tell you something that stays with the Seven until Vaun clears it."

"Then not on the steps."

She tucked the survey map more firmly beneath her arm.

"I have a Warden records room with a door and terrible tea."

Teren said, "I am suddenly very fond of terrible tea."

They went inside.

---

Thessa listened without interrupting.

That was unusual enough that Ilyan noticed.

The little Warden room smelled of damp wool, ink and whatever herb someone had once believed improved government tea. Rain ticked against the high window. Thessa sat on the edge of the table rather than behind it, one muddy boot hooked over the rung of a chair.

Ilyan gave her the facts.

Ardis recused.

Teren appointed successor chair.

Teren's signature.

The office had authority to receive the sealed direction Teren remembered only countermarking.

Teren did not remember holding the office.

The protected-routing rider remained hidden.

When he finished, Thessa looked at Teren.

"Do you remember anything that only the chair should have known?"

Teren frowned.

"Such as?"

"Don't know. That's why I'm asking you. Did people suddenly start bringing you problems that made no sense for your job? Did you have keys you shouldn't have had? Did somebody start calling you by a title you hated?"

Teren's expression changed.

Ilyan saw it immediately.

"What?"

"Keys."

Thessa sat straighter.

Teren looked down at his hands.

"I had a second key ring for perhaps a week. I remember because I hated carrying it. Heavy brass, three long keys and a little black one."

Ilyan's pulse quickened.

"What did they open?"

"I don't know."

"You don't remember?"

"I remember returning them."

"To whom?"

Teren shut his eyes.

"A woman. Crown service, I think. Grey hair."

Thessa said, "Old?"

"No. Grey early. Maybe forty. Maybe younger."

"Name?"

"No."

Ilyan leaned against the table.

A memory.

Not enough to prove anything.

Enough to look for a key issue register.

Thessa was already there.

"Keys get signed out."

Teren opened his eyes.

"Usually."

"Where?"

"If they were Joint Order rooms, administration. If Crown protected routing, Crown security custody. If Tribunal—"

"One problem at a time," Ilyan said.

Teren looked at him.

Then smiled faintly.

"You are learning."

"Don't make me regret it."

Thessa slid off the table.

"I have to go back to the wall before someone decides a Warden's romantic life is a valid structural repair material."

Ilyan caught her hand.

"Tonight?"

Her expression softened.

"Yes."

"My place?"

"If you are awake."

"I will be."

"That was not a challenge. Sleep if you need to."

"I want to see you."

She stepped closer.

"Then say that."

"I just did."

"Good."

She kissed him once, quick and warm, then turned toward Teren.

"If you remember any more keys, titles, strange rooms or mysteriously important pieces of stationery, tell him before he develops another facial expression like the one on the steps."

"I will make a list."

"Excellent."

At the door she looked back at Ilyan.

The glance was brief.

It still followed him for the next hour.

---

The acceptance packet was not in Joint Order administration.

That would have been too easy.

What survived was an accession stub showing that the packet had arrived under Crown seal on the night of Ardis's recusal and had been transferred the next morning into a temporary protected-routing compartment.

The compartment number matched nothing in the Hall's current system.

Examiner Sael found the obsolete index in a cabinet behind three years of arbitration schedules.

"Here," he said.

Ilyan bent over the page.

Temporary compartment PR-6.

Opened for west-tower continuity review.

Closed eleven days after Silver Night.

Contents transferred under a Crown security receipt.

Destination:

**Emergency appointments supplemental custody.**

Ilyan stared at it.

"Back to the Crown."

Sael nodded.

Teren sat at the other end of the table with both hands wrapped around tea he had forgotten to drink.

Vaun asked, "Receipt number?"

Sael pointed.

There was one.

And beneath it, a return annotation.

**Three-key set received with compartment closure. One black witness-routing key returned separately.**

Teren stood so fast his chair struck the wall.

Nobody spoke.

He came around the table.

The annotation had a date.

Two days after the compartment closed.

And an initial beside the separate return.

Not Teren's.

**C.V.**

Ilyan felt the room contract.

Corren.

His father had returned the black key.

Teren whispered, "I gave the keys to a woman."

Sael said, "You remember giving her all four?"

Teren opened his mouth.

Stopped.

"No."

"What do you remember?"

"The weight being gone."

Ilyan looked at the receipt again.

Three brass keys returned with compartment closure.

The black witness-routing key returned two days later by Corren Valeric.

That did not prove Teren had given it to Corren.

It did not prove Corren had taken it from him.

It did not prove what the key opened, when Corren acquired it, or whether its delayed return was improper.

But the black key existed outside Teren's remembered return.

And his father had possessed it afterward.

Vaun said, "Now we have a second question."

Ilyan did not need to ask which.

What happened during those two days?

Teren sat again.

His face had gone pale.

"I want the memory examination."

Vaun looked at him.

"Records first."

"We have records."

"We have enough records to justify a careful examination. Not a reckless one."

"Then careful."

Sael said quietly, "Teren."

He looked at him.

"If someone altered you, wanting the missing piece back is not the same as being ready for it."

Teren's laugh was brittle.

"I have spent eighteen years believing I failed to understand a system I was apparently running. I would like the dignity of discovering whether I was incompetent, deceived or tampered with."

Ilyan crossed the room.

He put a hand on his uncle's shoulder.

Teren looked up.

Ilyan did not tell him it would be all right.

He did not know that.

"Careful," he said.

Teren covered Ilyan's hand with his own.

"Careful."

Vaun gathered the accession copies.

"Tomorrow morning. I want a memory physician present, not only an examiner. No probing for content until baseline integrity is mapped."

Teren nodded.

Sael began writing the request.

Ilyan looked again at the initials beside the black key.

C.V.

His father had approved Mara's independent authority.

His father had known her private authentication phrase.

His father had returned the witness-routing key after Teren's remembered custody ended.

Those facts did not acquit him of Silver Night.

Nothing they had found did.

They did something more troublesome.

They made Corren's choices inside the disaster look deliberate in more than one direction.

Ilyan folded the certified copy and gave it back to Vaun.

Outside, Bloomtide rain had begun again, soft against the Hall windows.

Somewhere west of the river, Thessa was standing beside a broken retaining wall and swearing at stone.

Tonight she would come to him.

Tomorrow they would find out whether someone had taken a week of Teren's life and left the edges behind.

And before either of those things, Ilyan still had three oath appeals waiting on his desk.

He looked at Sael.

"Which one first?"

Sael glanced at the stack.

"The mill partnership."

"The goat?"

"The goat."

Teren stared at them.

Ilyan sat down.

The world, infuriatingly and mercifully, contained more than one kind of truth.
