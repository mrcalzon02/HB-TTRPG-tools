# Chapter Eight — The Edges Left Behind

Thessa arrived after midnight with clean hair, a bruised shoulder and enough appetite to empty Ilyan's kitchen.

He heard her key in the outer lock while he was standing barefoot beside the stove, staring at a pan he had forgotten to remove from the heat.

"That smells ambitious," she called.

"It was eggs."

"Was?"

He lifted the pan.

Thessa came through the doorway carrying her boots in one hand. She had changed out of Warden grey into a loose dark shirt and trousers, but the day remained visible on her: a scrape across one knuckle, damp curls at her temples, the stiffness with which she rolled her left shoulder after dropping the boots beside the wall.

Ilyan looked at the eggs.

"They have become something else."

"Put them down before they become evidence."

He did.

She laughed, crossed the kitchen and kissed him.

The kiss was not hurried. That was the first thing he noticed. The second was the warmth of her body after the rain outside, the third the faint scent of soap and wet stone in her hair. She put both hands around his waist and stayed there after their mouths parted.

"You waited up."

"I said I would."

"I also told you to sleep."

"I ignored the less useful instruction."

"Very adult of you."

He rested his forehead against hers.

For a while neither spoke.

The apartment had never seemed especially small until Thessa began belonging in it. Her coat over his chair. Her hairpin beside the washbasin. One of her gloves abandoned on the shelf three visits ago and never reclaimed because, she maintained, it was strategically positioned.

Tonight the closeness helped.

She felt the tension in him before he decided whether to explain it.

"Still leaf seventy-three?"

"And the key."

"The little black one."

He nodded.

Thessa drew back enough to see his face.

"Tell me what you are doing with it."

"Nothing."

"That was not what I asked."

He sighed.

She took his hand and pulled him toward the table. A parcel waited beneath her arm. She opened it to reveal cold roast chicken, bread, a wedge of sharp white cheese and two pears.

"Where did that come from?"

"The Warden night kitchen."

"You stole supper."

"I am an officer of the Western Wardens. I requisitioned supper."

"Did you sign anything?"

"No."

"Then you stole it."

"Eat."

They did.

The ordinary work of tearing bread and cutting cheese gave him time to sort his thoughts. Thessa did not fill the silence for him. She had become better at that since graduation. Or perhaps he had become better at noticing when she had always done it.

"I keep wanting the key to mean something definite," he said at last.

"It does mean something definite."

He looked at her.

"Your father returned it."

"Yes."

"Two days late."

"Separately."

"Yes."

"Those are definite."

He rubbed his thumb against a crumb on the table.

"They aren't enough."

"No."

"I hate that."

"I know."

The answer was gentle and entirely unsympathetic to his desire for an easier world.

He smiled despite himself.

Thessa ate a slice of pear.

"Do you think Teren is lying?"

"No."

"Do you think he could be?"

"Yes."

"Do you think someone changed his memory?"

"I think something is wrong with it."

"Different answer."

"Vaun has infected you."

"She would be appalled by the suggestion."

Ilyan leaned back.

"Teren remembers Ardis leaving. He remembers the appointment arriving. He remembers believing somebody else had taken the office. He remembers the keys. He remembers giving them back. He doesn't remember being appointed, swearing the oath, receiving the authority, reading the direction, or whatever happened with the black key."

Thessa's expression had gone thoughtful.

"That is a very tidy hole."

"Yes."

"Which makes you suspicious."

"Yes."

"Of alteration."

"Of everything."

She reached across the table and covered his hand.

"Good."

He blinked.

"Good?"

"Yes. Everything is the correct number of things to be suspicious of until tomorrow."

"That is terrible investigative doctrine."

"I am not an investigator."

"You found the key."

"I asked an old man whether he had carried keys. That is the sort of brilliant technique for which the Wardens will eventually build me a statue."

He laughed.

Her thumb moved once across his knuckles.

"Tomorrow," she said, "you let the people who know memory work tell you what they can actually establish."

"And if they establish nothing?"

"Then you still have the records."

"And if they establish he was altered?"

"Then you have a new problem."

He looked at her.

She squeezed his hand.

"Ilyan, there is no version where you wake tomorrow and Silver Night becomes simple."

He knew that.

Hearing it from her still hurt.

He turned his hand beneath hers and caught her fingers.

"Stay."

Her gaze changed.

"I intended to."

"I mean stay."

"I know what you mean."

The room seemed quieter.

Thessa stood first.

She came around the table and stopped between his knees. He put his hands at her waist. Her shirt was soft beneath his fingers, still warm from her body. She touched his hair, then his cheek.

There were many things they could have said.

Most of them would have been attempts to make language carry what their bodies already understood.

He kissed her instead.

Later, when the lamp was out and the rain had become a soft hiss beyond the shutters, Thessa lay curled against him with one leg thrown over his and her face tucked beneath his chin.

Ilyan was almost asleep when she said, "You are thinking."

"No."

"You changed breathing."

"That is an outrageous invasion of privacy."

"Go to sleep."

He kissed the top of her head.

This time he did.

---

The Hall of Veils kept its memory rooms underground.

Not because memory work required darkness. Examiner Sael told Ilyan that before Ilyan could ask.

"The old rooms were built beside the archive vaults," he said. "The vaults are below grade because fire rises and nobles eventually set everything on fire."

Ilyan looked at him.

Sael continued walking.

"That was not a joke."

"It was absolutely a joke."

"History is not obliged to stop being funny merely because people died."

The stair descended through pale stone into a corridor warmed by copper pipes. The air smelled faintly of lavender, wax and something mineral Ilyan could not name.

Teren walked between Ilyan and Judge Vaun.

He had dressed carefully.

That worried Ilyan more than if he had looked frightened.

Teren's dark coat was brushed, his collar exact, his hair tied back without a strand loose. He looked like the man who had taught half the Academy's younger nobles how not to stab themselves during first-year sword drills. Only his hands betrayed him. He kept flexing the fingers of his right hand as if testing whether they still belonged to him.

At the end of the corridor, a woman waited beside an open door.

She was small, perhaps seventy, with dark skin, close-cropped silver hair and a physician's green cord wrapped twice around one wrist. No House badge showed on her coat.

"Physician Enara Voss," Vaun said.

Ilyan felt Teren stiffen.

The physician noticed.

"No relation to Neral Voss," she said.

Teren let out a breath.

"Thank you."

"I have spent thirty years saying that in rooms like this."

She looked at Ilyan.

"You are the nephew."

"Ilyan."

"And an interested investigator."

"Yes."

"Those are not the same role."

"No."

"Good."

She looked at Vaun.

"Consent?"

"Voluntary. No content probing until baseline integrity is mapped."

Enara nodded.

"Teren Valeric, I need to hear that from you."

Teren stepped forward.

"I consent to baseline memory-integrity examination and noninvasive associative testing. I do not consent to compelled retrieval, mnemonic reconstruction or dream induction unless we stop and discuss it afterward."

"Excellent."

Ilyan glanced at him.

Teren saw.

"I did read the form."

"I wasn't—"

"You were."

Enara pointed into the room.

"Everyone inside."

The memory room contained no dramatic apparatus. There was a padded chair, a low table, three shaded lamps and a shallow silver basin set into a stone stand. Shelves held glass vials, folded cloths, tuning forks, sealed packets of herbs and several objects Ilyan could not identify.

One wall was covered by a curtain.

Enara closed the door.

"Before anyone becomes impressed by the basin," she said, "it is water."

Thessa would have liked her.

Sael took a seat near the wall. Vaun remained standing until Enara pointed at a chair.

"Judges also sit."

Vaun sat.

Ilyan almost smiled.

Teren lowered himself into the padded chair.

Enara began with questions so ordinary that Ilyan initially thought she was wasting time.

His name.

The season.

What he had eaten.

How he had slept.

The route from his house to the Hall.

The first sword he had owned.

His mother's voice.

The Academy practice yard in rain.

The smell of the west dormitory kitchens.

Teren answered steadily.

Then Enara changed the pattern.

She asked for the same memories backward.

She asked him to distinguish what he remembered directly from what he knew because someone had told him later.

She gave him three objects to handle—a brass button, a piece of cedar, a square of black wool—and asked what associations came first without forcing them into narrative.

The button reminded him of a cadet coat.

The cedar of Ilyan's grandfather's wardrobe.

The wool of funeral clothes and, after a pause, the old Academy examination curtains.

Enara wrote nothing while he spoke.

Sael did.

After nearly an hour she placed both hands on the rim of the silver basin.

"Now we narrow."

Teren's jaw tightened.

"All right."

"Silver Night. Do not tell me the story. Tell me the first thing your body does when I say the words."

"My shoulders tighten."

"Where?"

"Here."

He touched the muscles below his neck.

"Anything else?"

"Teeth."

"Clench?"

"Yes."

"Breathing?"

"Shallow."

"Image?"

Teren swallowed.

"The west tower burning."

"Direct memory?"

"Yes."

"Certainty?"

"High."

"Before or after you knew students were dead?"

"After."

Enara nodded.

"Pel Ardis."

Teren's eyes moved left.

Ilyan saw it.

"Annoyance," Teren said.

"Image?"

"His hands. Ink on two fingers."

"Direct?"

"Yes."

"Recusal."

Teren's fingers curled against the chair.

"Anger."

"Image?"

"A door closing."

"Which door?"

"I don't know."

"Do not find one."

Teren looked at her.

She repeated, "Do not find one. If the image does not contain the answer, leave it without one."

His shoulders eased a fraction.

"All right."

"Successor chair."

Nothing happened.

That was what made Ilyan afraid.

Teren did not flinch. His gaze did not shift. His breathing did not change.

Enara waited.

"What do you have?"

"Words."

"Whose?"

"Mine now. From yesterday. From the register."

"Anything older?"

"No."

"Emergency appointment."

Teren's brow furrowed.

"A sealed packet."

"Direct?"

"Yes."

"Open or closed?"

"Closed."

"Your name on it?"

"I don't remember."

"Your hand on it?"

A pause.

"Yes."

"Feeling?"

"Impatience."

"Why?"

"I don't know."

"Good."

Teren gave her a strange look.

"Good?"

"You didn't invent a reason."

She continued.

"Second key ring."

His right hand closed.

"Weight."

"Where?"

"Right side. Belt."

"Keys?"

"Three brass. One black."

"Black key."

His breathing changed.

Ilyan leaned forward before he could stop himself.

Enara raised one finger without looking at him.

Teren's eyes had closed.

"What changed?" she asked.

"Cold."

"In the room?"

"No. Hand."

"Which hand?"

"Left."

His left hand was open on the chair arm.

"What else?"

"Soap."

"What kind?"

"I don't know."

"Image?"

"A cuff."

"Whose?"

"I don't know."

"Color?"

"Grey."

Ilyan thought of the woman Teren remembered.

Enara did not ask.

"Do you see a face?"

"No."

"Do you need to?"

Teren's eyes opened.

"No."

"Return the keys."

His breathing became shallow again.

"Table."

"Direct?"

"I think."

"Certainty?"

"Medium."

"Keys on it?"

"Brass."

Ilyan's skin prickled.

Enara's voice did not change.

"How many?"

Teren stared at nothing.

"Three."

Silence.

Ilyan heard Sael's pen stop.

Teren's face went white.

"Three," he said again.

Enara leaned closer.

"Do not go after the fourth."

"But—"

"Do not."

His hands began to shake.

"Teren. Look at me."

He did.

"Name five things in this room."

He blinked.

"What?"

"Five."

"The lamp. Your cord. Basin. Curtain. Ilyan."

"Four objects and a nephew. Acceptable. Four sounds."

"Pipe. Pen. Someone walking outside. Ilyan breathing."

"Three physical sensations."

"Chair. Collar. My left hand is cold."

"Stay here."

He swallowed.

"I am here."

The tremor eased.

Enara waited another minute before she moved.

Then she turned to Vaun.

"Baseline testing is finished."

Ilyan could barely keep his voice level.

"What did you find?"

Enara looked at him.

"Enough to tell you what I did not find."

He waited.

"Teren's general autobiographical memory is coherent for his age. There are ordinary weaknesses, later-story contamination and several places where he knows facts without retaining a vivid episodic memory. Nothing remarkable."

"And the appointment?"

"The appointment interval is remarkable."

Teren stared at the floor.

Enara continued.

"Not because he cannot narrate it. People fail to remember offices, paperwork and unpleasant weeks all the time. What interests me is the boundary."

She looked at Sael.

"His associative responses remain rich immediately before the appointment and resume around several events afterward. The office itself produces almost no older sensory or emotional associations despite evidence that he accepted it, carried its keys and performed at least some custody function."

Ilyan said, "So someone erased it."

"No."

The answer came sharply enough to stop him.

Enara folded her hands.

"I can say the gap is unusually bounded. I can say the black key produces preserved somatic and sensory associations that do not match his conscious narrative. I can say his remembered key return appears incomplete: when asked without being told the later receipt, he retrieved three brass keys on a table and no black key."

Teren looked up.

"But alteration?"

"Possible."

"How possible?"

"I do not turn uncertainty into numbers for anxious relatives."

Ilyan disliked her briefly.

She noticed and did not care.

"There are other explanations," she said. "Acute stress. A magically reinforced secrecy oath. A protective compartment imposed with consent. Repeated later retelling that displaced the original memory. Certain sleep or mnemonic preparations. Deliberate interference. More than one of those at once."

Sael said, "Could an emergency chair have sworn a compartment oath?"

"Certainly."

Teren frowned.

"I would remember that."

Enara looked at him.

"That sentence is the problem we are examining."

He shut his mouth.

Vaun asked, "Can you distinguish voluntary compartment from hostile alteration?"

"Not today."

"What would you need?"

"The acceptance oath, if it survives. Any restricted-routing rider. Medical or magical treatment records from the week after Silver Night. A list of substances used in the west-tower response. And preferably no one asking him to remember harder in the meantime."

Teren's expression tightened.

"You want me not to think about it."

"I want you not to excavate your own mind with a shovel because you are frightened."

That landed.

Ilyan thought of Teren last night, alone in his house, knowing that a book carried his signature where his memory carried nothing.

"What about the cold hand?" he asked.

Enara looked at Teren, not Ilyan.

"May I discuss observations in front of him?"

Teren almost laughed.

"Please."

"The cold sensation may be meaningless. It may be an ordinary bodily association. It may be linked to a person, room, object, spell, injury or emotion. The soap and grey cuff may belong to the woman he already remembers or may not. We do not promote fragments into a scene merely because the scene would be useful."

Vaun nodded once.

Teren looked exhausted.

"Can we look under the curtain now?"

Enara smiled for the first time.

"No."

He stared at her.

"What is under it?"

"My shelves."

"Why cover them?"

"Because everyone invents significance for objects they can see during associative testing."

Teren began to laugh.

It came out rough, then helpless.

Ilyan laughed too.

Even Vaun's mouth moved.

Enara waited until the room settled.

Then she said, "There is one more thing."

The laughter stopped.

She addressed Teren.

"When I said 'successor chair,' you had no older response. When I said 'emergency appointment,' you had one. The sealed packet. Impatience. Your hand on it."

Teren nodded slowly.

"Meaning?"

"Meaning the event may not be absent. The category may be."

Ilyan frowned.

Enara tapped one finger against the table.

"If I remove the name of an office from memory but leave the morning, the room, the paper, the people and the work, a person may retain a week full of fragments they no longer know how to organize."

Teren went very still.

"The edges."

"Yes."

"What would do that?"

"A secrecy working can. So can some forms of targeted mnemonic interference. So can a person who spent years refusing to think about a period until the organizing frame decayed. We need records."

Records first.

Again.

Ilyan no longer resented the phrase.

He feared what it might find.

---

They left Teren with Enara for a private medical interview.

Ilyan had not expected to be excluded.

He was.

He disliked it for exactly three seconds before remembering that Teren was entitled to possess thoughts that did not belong to his nephew, his judge or his investigation.

Sael took him upstairs.

The Hall seemed indecently bright after the memory rooms.

At Ilyan's desk, the goat appeal waited beneath a stone weight.

He stared at it.

Sael put a second file beside it.

"Do not."

"Do not what?"

"Give me work."

"You are employed here."

"My uncle may have had his memory altered."

"Yes."

"My father returned a witness-routing key two days after it should have been returned."

"Yes."

"And you want me to decide whether a goat belongs to a mill partnership."

Sael considered.

"The goat died eleven years ago."

Ilyan looked at him.

"Then why are we still deciding?"

"That is the interesting part."

Despite himself, Ilyan opened the file.

The dead goat had produced two daughters before its death. Those daughters had produced a line of unusually sturdy pack goats now used by three villages. The original partnership agreement assigned "the goat and all increase thereof" to the mill partnership. One heir argued that the phrase meant offspring only during the original goat's life. Another maintained that the entire breeding line remained partnership property.

Ilyan read three pages.

Then four.

By page six he was angry for completely different reasons.

Sael sat across from him working on his own correspondence.

"Who writes 'all increase thereof' about a goat?"

"A man who believed himself immortal."

"He died six months later."

"Evidence against the belief."

Ilyan looked up.

"That was a joke."

"No."

"You are becoming less convincing."

The door opened.

Judge Vaun entered carrying a narrow envelope.

Ilyan forgot the goats.

"Acceptance packet?"

"No."

She handed it to Sael.

"Crown supplemental custody responded to our preservation request. The acceptance packet is still sealed pending review of the protected-routing rider. But they released the key schedule because the physical keys are no longer active security instruments."

Sael opened the envelope.

There were four lines.

Three brass keys.

One black.

The brass keys opened the temporary west-tower review room, the protected evidence cabinet and the inter-order dispatch chest.

The black key opened no door.

Ilyan read that twice.

"What?"

Sael slid the schedule toward him.

**Black key: routing selector, witness-continuity cabinet.**

Ilyan looked at Vaun.

"A selector?"

"Apparently the cabinet had multiple sealed compartments. The black key did not unlock the cabinet itself. It selected which protected compartment could be opened by the cabinet key."

"So somebody needed both."

"Yes."

"Who had the cabinet key?"

Vaun put another paper on the desk.

The surviving issue schedule named the office.

**Contingency witness receiving.**

Mara's chain.

Ilyan felt the old investigation and the new one lock together with an almost physical click.

Not proof.

A mechanism.

Mara's independent authority had placed her inside witness continuity. Teren's emergency chair office had carried the selector. Neither alone could open a protected witness compartment.

"Two-person control," Sael said.

Vaun nodded.

"Designed to prevent either office acting alone."

Ilyan stared at the schedule.

"And my father returned the selector."

"Two days after the three brass keys."

"Which means he had one half."

"It means he returned one half."

Ilyan exhaled through his nose.

"Yes."

He could hear Thessa telling him not to make the key carry more weight than it could survive.

He hated that everyone he loved had apparently joined a conspiracy to improve his evidentiary habits.

"Could Corren have lawfully held it?" he asked.

Vaun answered, "We don't know yet."

Sael turned the key schedule over.

"There is a transfer reference."

Of course there was.

Ilyan leaned in.

The black selector had been issued with Teren's set. Its return receipt bore Corren's initials. Between those two events, a transfer line pointed to a protected custody docket.

Not a name.

A docket.

Vaun read the number.

"That is covered by the same rider the Crown is reviewing."

Ilyan sat back.

"Tomorrow?"

"Possibly."

He looked at her.

She raised an eyebrow.

"I did not create Crown records procedure."

"You enjoy it."

"I enjoy procedure that prevents frightened people from destroying the thing they want to understand."

That was aimed at him.

Fairly.

Sael returned to the key schedule.

"Two-person control matters for another reason."

Ilyan waited.

"If the system functioned as designed, possession of the selector alone did not allow Corren, Teren or anyone else to access the witness compartment."

"Mara or another authorized receiving bearer would also be needed."

"Or their key."

Ilyan thought of Mara's credential. Her authority surviving local staffing substitution. Her ability to act if the ordinary receiving chain failed.

"What happened to the cabinet key?"

Vaun's gaze sharpened.

Sael was already searching the accession index.

They found the answer twenty minutes later.

The cabinet key had not been returned.

It had been retired in place when the witness-continuity cabinet was sealed after Silver Night.

The retirement notation carried an explanation.

**Receiving key unavailable. Cabinet sealed by dual override under protected witness preservation.**

Ilyan read it once.

Then again.

"Mara had it."

Sael said, "The office had it. We do not yet know the individual custody at that moment."

"But if she was the continuity bearer—"

"Likely is not known."

Ilyan closed his eyes.

"Right."

Vaun leaned against the table.

"Now ask the useful question."

He opened his eyes.

"If the receiving key was unavailable, how did they perform a dual override?"

Sael's expression changed.

"Exactly."

The retirement note gave two override authorities.

Joint Order liaison chair.

Contingency witness receiving supervisor.

Teren's office.

Ardis's old side of the chain.

But the date was three days after Silver Night.

Teren did not remember holding the office.

Ardis was dead.

Mara was missing.

And Corren returned the selector five days after Silver Night.

Ilyan felt the shape of the next problem.

"Who signed the override?"

Vaun looked at the restricted docket reference.

"That," she said, "is under the rider."

---

Teren emerged from the memory rooms shortly before dusk.

He looked tired but not shattered.

Ilyan counted that as a victory.

They told him about the selector.

He listened without interruption, then asked to see the key schedule.

His finger stopped above the words **routing selector**.

"I knew that."

Ilyan's heart kicked.

"What?"

Teren looked irritated with himself.

"Not the key. The mechanism. Two-person witness cabinets. I remember teaching junior examiners why they existed."

"Does that bring anything back?"

"No."

He looked at the paper again.

"Which is beginning to feel insulting."

Vaun said, "Enara?"

"Same conclusion. Don't dig."

"Good."

Teren gave her a dark look.

"I am surrounded by people who have discovered restraint."

Ilyan thought of Thessa.

"It is terrible."

They left the Hall together.

Bloomtide had cleared. The streets shone beneath a low evening sun, every roof edge and carriage wheel throwing back gold from the day's rain.

At the bottom of the steps, Teren stopped.

"I am going home."

Ilyan looked at him.

"Alone?"

"Yes."

"You sure?"

"No. But I am still going."

That was fair.

Teren touched Ilyan's shoulder.

"I have spent the entire day being observed. I would like to sit in my own kitchen and be uninteresting."

"Can you manage that?"

"I have preserves to relabel."

Ilyan laughed.

Teren smiled, then sobered.

"If I remember something—"

"Write it down. Don't chase it."

"Enara told you too?"

"She did not need to."

"Insufferable."

He left.

Ilyan watched until his uncle turned the corner.

Then he went to find the Seven.

---

They met at Mireth's rooms because Mireth had food, Caelen had somehow acquired wine, and Lyrion had been banned from choosing the location after suggesting a salon where three ambassadors were dining.

Damaris and Rhyl arrived together.

Not merely at the same time.

Together.

Rhyl's hand rested at the small of her back as they came through the door. Damaris did not move away from it when she saw everyone looking.

"Don't," she said.

Lyrion raised both hands.

"I have said nothing."

"Your face has."

"My face is innocent."

Thessa, already stretched across one end of Mireth's sofa, said, "Your face has never been innocent."

Lyrion sat beside her and stole a piece of cheese from her plate.

She slapped his wrist without looking.

Caelen poured wine.

Mireth leaned against his shoulder while he did it, close enough that he had to reach around her.

The Seven had become less subtle in less than a week.

Or perhaps they had simply lost the institutional architecture that once gave them reasons to pretend.

Ilyan took the empty chair beside Thessa.

She immediately put her feet in his lap.

He looked at them.

"Retaining wall," she said.

"That explains why they are my problem?"

"It explains why they hurt."

He began rubbing one ankle.

Thessa smiled into her cup.

Damaris watched this with interest.

"Do not."

"I said nothing."

"Your face has."

Lyrion pointed at her.

"Thank you."

For several minutes they ate.

That mattered.

The room smelled of roast onions, wine and Mireth's flowering window vines. Outside, evening bells crossed one another from three districts. Caelen had loosened his collar. Rhyl sat close enough to Damaris that their knees remained touching. Mireth had one hand resting on Caelen's thigh with the absent familiarity of someone who had stopped asking herself whether she wanted to touch him.

They were still themselves.

Ilyan needed that before he told them.

Then he did.

Leaf seventy-three.

Teren's appointment.

The bounded memory gap.

The three brass keys and the black selector.

Corren's delayed return.

The morning examination.

The two-person witness cabinet.

The missing receiving key.

The dual override.

Nobody interrupted until he finished.

Damaris spoke first.

"Who knew Teren was chair at the time?"

"We don't know."

"Who was supposed to know?"

"Joint Order administration, Crown appointments, whoever was inside the protected routing chain."

"His family?"

"No idea."

Rhyl said, "Your father?"

"No idea."

Mireth frowned.

"If the appointment itself was restricted, Corren could have known through his liaison authority without Teren's ordinary colleagues knowing."

"Yes."

Caelen set down his cup.

"And if Teren's memory was deliberately altered, whoever did it would have needed access to him."

"Probably."

"That is not a small list during an emergency."

"No."

Thessa shifted her feet in Ilyan's lap.

"Lyrion."

He looked at her.

"You are doing the face."

"What face?"

"The one where you have decided everyone else is approaching the question from the wrong end."

Lyrion sighed.

"I do not have a face for that."

All six of them looked at him.

"Fine."

He leaned forward.

"We are asking who could have done something to Teren. That is useful. But if the office was secret enough that he could lose the category and still retain pieces of the week, ask who benefited from everyone later believing he was only an examiner."

Silence.

Ilyan felt something shift.

Damaris nodded slowly.

"If Teren remained publicly ordinary, then responsibility for chair decisions would be harder to trace."

"Or easier to attribute to the institution generally," Lyrion said.

Mireth added, "Or to Ardis, if records stopped at his recusal."

Caelen looked at Ilyan.

"Did they?"

"The Hall index knew a successor existed. Identity restricted to Crown book."

"So anyone without Crown access could spend eighteen years believing the office passed into fog."

"Yes."

Rhyl rubbed his thumb along the rim of his cup.

"That does not tell us whether hiding Teren protected him or protected someone else."

"No," Ilyan said.

Thessa nudged his thigh with her heel.

He resumed rubbing her ankle.

Damaris's expression had gone distant.

"What?"

She looked at him.

"The River Ledger does this with emergency commercial agents."

"Erases their memory?"

"No. Hides the person behind the office."

Lyrion smiled.

"Less exciting."

"Usually healthier."

Damaris continued.

"If a bonded agent is acting in a politically dangerous negotiation, the ledger may expose the authority but restrict the identity. It protects the agent from pressure and protects counterparties from using family ties to challenge the transaction."

Rhyl said, "So secrecy itself is not suspicious."

"No. But afterward, the identity is supposed to become auditable under controlled access."

Ilyan looked at her.

"Which it did. Leaf seventy-three."

"Yes."

"So the system worked."

Damaris hesitated.

"The record system worked."

That distinction mattered.

Mireth said, "And Teren did not."

Nobody liked the sentence.

Mireth winced.

"I mean his memory."

"I know," Ilyan said.

Thessa's foot went still beneath his hand.

Caelen looked around the room.

"What do we actually do?"

The question was so simple that it cut through everything else.

Ilyan answered.

"Tomorrow Vaun tries to get the protected-routing rider and the override signatures. Enara wants treatment records, oath language and anything used medically or magically in the west-tower response. Sael is checking whether the Hall preserved an acceptance oath outside the Crown packet."

"And us?" Caelen asked.

Ilyan looked at the Seven.

A week ago they had been students.

They had spent years believing adulthood would arrive as a set of robes, oaths, appointments and permission to stay out too late without lying to anyone.

Instead it had arrived as family instructions, sealed evidence, lovers, dead classmates, old crimes and the discovery that every institution had doors they had never noticed while they were protected.

"We go to work," he said.

Rhyl laughed softly.

"That is disappointingly sensible."

"It keeps happening."

Damaris leaned into Rhyl.

"Tomorrow I can check whether the Ledger ever carried emergency bonded traffic for the west-tower response. Not names. Just whether a protected commercial route existed."

Mireth said, "I can ask Silae about oath effects that preserve sensory memory while restricting categories. General doctrine only."

Caelen nodded.

"I can ask custody training whether dual-control witness cabinets are still used and what an override normally requires. No historical case."

Lyrion lifted his cup.

"I can find out whether anyone at Silver Bough remembers the emergency chair as a person or only an office."

Thessa said, "I have a retaining wall."

Everyone looked at her.

"What? It is still broken."

Ilyan laughed.

She smiled at him.

"And after that, I can ask Warden records whether any west-road protected movement happened in the days after Silver Night. If Mara left the capital through a Warden-controlled road, there may be a movement count without a name."

Ilyan stopped laughing.

Thessa saw it.

"Count," she repeated. "Not destination. Not identity."

"Right."

"No opening her location chain."

"Right."

She watched him until satisfied.

The Seven began talking over one another after that.

Not about conspiracy.

About tomorrow.

Caelen had dawn drill. Mireth had a Green Covenant hearing. Damaris had to explain to Lady Meris why three River Ledger clerks now knew she could ask unusually expensive questions. Rhyl had been assigned a boundary survey. Lyrion had lunch with a diplomat he disliked. Thessa's wall remained stubbornly collapsed.

Their lives had not paused for Silver Night.

That was beginning to feel less like an insult and more like protection.

When they finally left, Thessa walked home with Ilyan.

At his door she caught his sleeve.

"You did better today."

"At what?"

"Not making every new fact become the answer."

He considered pretending to be offended.

Instead he kissed her.

She leaned into him, smiling.

"I hate that this is attractive," he murmured.

"What?"

"Being corrected."

Thessa laughed against his mouth.

"That is fortunate."

"For you."

"For everyone."

He opened the door.

Behind them, Bloomtide moonlight silvered the wet street.

Tomorrow there would be another record.

Another bounded fact.

Another person who remembered too much or not enough.

Tonight, for a few hours, Ilyan chose not to chase the missing center of Teren's week.

The edges were still there.

For now, that was where the truth lived.
