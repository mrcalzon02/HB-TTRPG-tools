# Chapter Thirteen — The Cost of Keeping Seven

The first rule lasted until breakfast.

Not because anyone broke it.

Because Damaris discovered, over coffee and a plate of pears, that a rule which sounded excellent in a warm Green Covenant workroom became much less elegant when applied to a friend sitting three feet away.

She found Rhyl at the little bakery behind the River Ledger annex, where the tables were too close together and the proprietor had long ago stopped pretending not to know the names of half the clerks who ate there. Bloomtide sunlight came through the front windows in long pale bars. Outside, carts rattled over damp paving stones. Inside, butter and yeast had defeated every other smell.

Rhyl had already ordered for her.

"You look offended," he said.

Damaris sat down.

"I am deciding whether I am offended."

"Take your time. The pears are good."

He pushed the plate toward her.

Damaris did not take one.

"Did you speak to someone from the north road yesterday?"

Rhyl's hand stopped around his cup.

It was not a dramatic movement. Most people would not have noticed it.

Damaris did.

"Yes."

"About the old protected routes?"

Rhyl looked at her for a moment.

"I can't tell you."

There it was.

The phrase they had agreed should not itself count as betrayal.

Damaris hated it immediately.

She reached for a pear slice because refusing breakfast had never improved an argument.

"I am not asking for a name."

"I know."

"I am asking whether the conversation concerned the route inquiry."

"And I can't tell you that."

"Why?"

Rhyl leaned back.

"That is dangerously close to asking me to explain the thing I cannot tell you."

Damaris bit the pear harder than necessary.

The answer was reasonable. That made it worse.

Rhyl watched her chew.

"Do you want me to lie?"

"No."

"Do you want me to say I can't tell you?"

"Apparently not."

"Then we have discovered a flaw in yesterday's magnificent constitutional settlement."

She glared at him.

He smiled into his cup.

Damaris had spent enough time around contracts to know that principles were cheapest before the first disputed application. Nobody objected to confidentiality when the confidential thing belonged to somebody else. Nobody disliked discretion until discretion stood between them and an answer they badly wanted.

She set the pear down.

"Can you tell me whether I need to change what I am doing?"

Rhyl's expression changed.

Not much. The amusement went away.

"Yes."

"Do I?"

"No."

Damaris waited.

Rhyl added, "Not because of anything I learned yesterday."

That was information.

Bounded information, carefully chosen.

She felt some of the irritation leave her shoulders.

"Can you tell me whether one of us is in immediate danger?"

"Not from anything I learned yesterday."

"Can you tell me whether Vaun should be warned?"

Rhyl considered that longer.

"No warning from me is required on the basis of that conversation."

Damaris nodded slowly.

The shape of the rule became visible between them.

Not tell me everything.

Not trust me blindly.

Tell me what I need to do if your secret changes my responsibilities.

Rhyl reached across the table and stole the pear slice she had abandoned.

"Better?"

"Marginally."

"Excellent. I aim for marginal improvement."

She caught his wrist before he could withdraw his hand.

He looked at her fingers around him, then at her face.

Damaris knew the bakery was full of Ledger clerks. She knew three of them would notice. She knew her aunt Meris could probably reconstruct her breakfast companions from expense ledgers if she ever became sufficiently bored.

She kissed Rhyl anyway.

It was brief and warm and tasted faintly of coffee.

When she sat back, the woman at the next table continued reading her account sheet with heroic concentration.

Rhyl looked pleased with himself.

"Do not interpret that as winning the argument."

"I would never."

"You are interpreting it as winning the argument."

"I am interpreting it as evidence that the argument has benefits."

Damaris laughed despite herself.

Then she took out the folded seed inventory on which Ilyan had written their first rules.

"We need another one."

Rhyl groaned.

"Before breakfast?"

"Especially before breakfast."

---

By noon, the problem had become less theoretical.

The request for the paired receiving record had reached the Crown archive, the old Joint Office registry and a sealed River Ledger cross-index. Three institutions agreed that a paired receiving mechanism had existed. Two agreed on the accession family under which its surviving records ought to be filed. None would yet say whether the particular receiving record still existed.

That was ordinary enough.

The difficulty came when Caelen arrived at the Hall with a message for Ilyan and refused to repeat it in front of Teren.

Teren noticed.

Of course he noticed.

He was sitting near the window in Vaun's workroom with a stack of old appointment abstracts and the air of a man who had spent enough decades around institutions to recognize when a younger relative was suddenly being careful.

Caelen stopped inside the door.

"Ilyan. A word."

Ilyan looked up from Sael's notes.

"Here?"

Caelen's eyes flicked once toward Teren.

"Outside."

Teren lowered the abstract in his hand.

"How subtle."

Caelen did not apologize.

That, more than anything, made Ilyan get up.

They stepped into the corridor.

Caelen waited until the door shut.

"I have a Crown access problem."

"With the receiving record?"

"I can't tell you."

Ilyan stared at him.

Caelen's mouth tightened.

"Yes. I know. We invented this yesterday and it is already unbearable."

"What can you tell me?"

"Something I learned in Crown service may affect how you frame a request. I cannot give you the underlying record, the office that produced it, or the person attached to it."

"Can you tell Vaun?"

"Not without authorization I do not currently have."

"Can you ask for authorization?"

"Yes."

"Will asking expose what we're investigating?"

Caelen looked annoyed.

"Possibly."

Ilyan leaned against the corridor wall.

This was exactly the problem they had described. It was also much worse when the person withholding the information was Caelen, who had once slept on the floor beside Ilyan's Academy bed because Ilyan had woken shaking after Silver Night and refused to admit he was afraid to be alone.

"Does it concern Corren?"

Caelen's face closed.

Ilyan immediately regretted the question.

"Don't answer that."

"I wasn't going to."

"I know."

They stood in silence.

At the far end of the corridor, a clerk carried a tower of files past them and tried not to look interested.

Ilyan lowered his voice.

"What do I need to do differently?"

Caelen relaxed by a fraction.

"Do not ask only whether the paired mechanism was exercised. Ask whether any receiving-side authority was reassigned, superseded or held in reserve during the two days Corren had the selector."

Ilyan frowned.

"That's specific."

"It is as specific as I can be."

"Why does it matter?"

"Because a negative exercise record may not mean the receiving side was inactive."

"That sounds like something Vaun needs."

"I agree."

"But you can't tell her why."

"Not yet."

Ilyan rubbed both hands over his face.

"Fine. Ask for authorization."

"I already did."

"Of course you did."

Caelen leaned beside him against the wall.

For a moment they were simply two tired young men in formal coats standing outside a room full of older people and old mistakes.

"Are you angry?" Caelen asked.

"Yes."

"At me?"

Ilyan considered it.

"No. I am angry that I have to decide whether I trust you without being allowed to inspect the reason I should trust you."

Caelen nodded.

"That seems fair."

"And I hate that you are being reasonable about it."

"You have been spending too much time with your family."

Ilyan laughed once.

Then he looked at Caelen.

"If the authorization is refused?"

"I tell you it was refused."

"And then?"

"Then I can still tell you what action I think is safe. I just cannot prove why."

That was the second shape of the rule.

A friend could offer advice without offering the source.

The others were not required to obey it.

Trust did not become authority merely because evidence was unavailable.

Ilyan pushed away from the wall.

"Come inside. Tell Vaun the request should be broadened. Nothing else."

"Teren will know I withheld something."

"Yes."

"He may resent it."

"He can join the club."

When they returned, Teren looked from one of them to the other.

"Have we finished being mysterious?"

"No," Ilyan said.

Teren's eyebrows rose.

Caelen addressed Vaun.

"I recommend adding reassignment, supersession and reserve receiving authority to the paired-record request. I cannot presently disclose the basis for that recommendation. I have requested permission to do so."

Vaun looked at him for several seconds.

"Does your undisclosed basis create an immediate safety concern?"

"No."

"Does it require suspension of the present inquiry?"

"No."

"Does it create a conflict requiring you to withdraw?"

"Not to my knowledge."

Vaun turned to Sael.

"Broaden it. Mark the recommendation as uncorroborated pending source authority."

That was all.

No speech about trust. No demand that Caelen prove friendship by violating Crown duty. No suspicion manufactured because a young officer had behaved like an officer.

Teren watched Caelen for another moment, then returned to his papers.

Ilyan could not tell whether his uncle approved.

That, too, would have to remain unknown.

---

Mireth objected to the word *rules*.

"Rules are things somebody enforces," she said that evening. "Who exactly are you proposing as magistrate of friendship?"

They were back in the Covenant workroom, though this time Mireth had opened the windows. The rain had finally gone. Damp spring air moved through the room carrying the smell of wet leaves and turned soil from the courtyard below.

Caelen sat on the sill beside her. His shoulder touched hers whenever either shifted. Neither appeared to notice anymore, which meant everyone else did.

Damaris had rewritten Ilyan's seed-inventory notes onto clean paper.

Rhyl had drawn a deer in the margin.

Damaris had crossed it out.

Rhyl had drawn another.

"Principles," Lyrion suggested.

"Worse," Thessa said. "Principles are rules wearing expensive clothes."

"Practices," Mireth said.

"That sounds agricultural," Caelen replied.

Mireth elbowed him.

"Good. Agriculture works."

Ilyan sat on the floor with his back against Thessa's legs. She had taken the chair behind him and was idly combing her fingers through his hair while reading Damaris's draft over his shoulder.

It was difficult to feel politically endangered under those circumstances.

Not impossible.

Just difficult.

Damaris tapped the page.

"The first practice survives. We do not ask each other to violate an oath, protected confidence, client duty, source promise or lawful custody restriction."

"Add family confidence," Mireth said.

Lyrion shook his head.

"Too broad. Families call everything confidential when they mean embarrassing."

"Some family confidence is real."

"Then it fits protected confidence when it is real."

Mireth considered and nodded.

Damaris continued.

"Second: 'I can't tell you' is not evidence of betrayal."

"But it also isn't evidence of innocence," Rhyl said.

The room went quiet.

He shrugged.

"It can't be. Otherwise any of us can hide anything by saying the magic words."

Lyrion pointed at him.

"That."

Damaris wrote it down.

Ilyan felt Thessa's fingers pause in his hair.

"So what happens when one of us thinks the hidden thing matters?" she asked.

"We ask for consequence," Caelen said.

Everyone looked at him.

He explained the corridor conversation without revealing its source.

"I couldn't tell Ilyan what I knew. I could tell him what question to ask differently. I could tell Vaun there was no immediate danger, no reason to suspend, no known conflict."

Damaris nodded.

"Impact without content."

"Sometimes."

"And if even the impact is protected?" Mireth asked.

Lyrion answered. "Then you say that too."

"That is going to sound suspicious."

"It may be suspicious. The point is not to abolish suspicion."

That landed harder than anything else they had said.

The Seven had spent years treating trust as the absence of suspicion. At the Academy, that had almost worked. Their lives had overlapped so completely that secrecy itself felt like a change in weather.

Now suspicion could be reasonable without becoming accusation.

A person could notice a closed door and still refrain from kicking it in.

Damaris wrote again.

"Third: if protected information changes another person's immediate safety, legal exposure, professional duty or required action, disclose the consequence as far as lawfully possible."

"Required action," Thessa repeated. "Not desired action."

"Yes."

"Good."

Rhyl leaned over the table.

"Fourth: advice based on a protected source is advice, not an order and not proof."

"And not corroboration," Ilyan said.

"Yes."

Lyrion took the pen from Damaris.

His handwriting was annoyingly beautiful.

"Fifth: if you deliberately mislead the group, you owe the group the fact of the deception once the reason for it ends, unless even acknowledging the deception remains protected."

Mireth frowned.

"That last part swallows the whole rule."

"Only if we let it."

"How do we know?"

"We don't."

Damaris sighed.

"I hate this."

"You love this," Rhyl said.

"I love contracts. Contracts have remedies."

"We could fine each other."

"You have no money."

"Then I am judgment-proof."

Damaris threw a folded scrap of paper at him.

He caught it.

Mireth laughed, and the room loosened.

That mattered.

They were not building an intelligence service. They were trying to remain friends while becoming people whose work sometimes required silence.

There was a difference.

Caelen took the pen next.

"What about urgent danger?"

Thessa's hand stopped in Ilyan's hair.

Nobody joked.

Silver Night lived beneath the question.

Urgent danger had once arrived faster than permission.

Mireth spoke first.

"If you reasonably believe one of us is in immediate danger, you warn them. Even if you cannot explain everything."

"Agreed," Rhyl said.

"And if the warning itself violates an oath?" Damaris asked.

Caelen looked down at the page.

"Then the oath and the person are actually in conflict. We cannot solve that here."

"That's not enough."

"It has to be. We don't get to invent a private law superior to every oath we take."

Thessa leaned forward.

"But we can decide what it costs."

Caelen looked at her.

"Meaning?"

"If I choose my Warden oath over warning Ilyan about something that later harms him, I may have been professionally correct. He is still allowed to be furious with me."

Ilyan turned enough to see her face.

She looked down at him.

There was no accusation in it. No test.

Just an adult truth neither of them particularly liked.

"Yes," he said.

Thessa's thumb brushed once behind his ear.

"And if I break an oath for you, you don't get to pretend the cost disappears because it was romantic."

"Also yes."

Lyrion smiled faintly.

"There. That one we should keep."

Damaris wrote:

**Duty may justify a choice without erasing its personal consequences. Love may justify a choice without erasing its institutional consequences.**

Rhyl read it.

"That sounds dangerously like a principle."

"Shut up."

---

The unresolved disagreement arrived with Mireth.

It began with a living-memory petitioner whose name she did not give them.

"I have a hypothetical," she said.

Lyrion groaned.

"It isn't hypothetical," Damaris said.

"No."

Mireth folded her hands in her lap.

Caelen's posture changed beside her. Not protective exactly. Attentive.

"Someone came to the Covenant today because they believed a remembered event might be relevant to Silver Night. I cannot tell you who. I cannot tell you what they remembered. They have not consented to investigative use."

Ilyan sat straighter.

"Is it relevant?"

"Maybe."

"To the paired route?"

"I can't tell you."

The phrase hurt less the third time.

Not much less.

Damaris asked, "Immediate danger?"

"No."

"Evidence at risk?"

Mireth hesitated.

"Possibly."

That changed the room.

Vaun could preserve evidence without forcing a witness into public testimony. The Hall had procedures for exactly that. But even asking Vaun to preserve something might reveal that a witness existed.

Ilyan felt the old instinct rise in him: push, ask, get enough to act.

He looked at Mireth instead.

She was pale around the mouth.

"What do you want from us?" he asked.

Relief flickered across her face.

"I don't know."

Caelen turned toward her.

"Do you want advice without telling us the content?"

"Yes."

Damaris drew a clean sheet toward herself.

"Then we can do that. What choices are available to you?"

Mireth breathed out.

"I can do nothing until the petitioner decides. I can explain preservation options without naming the Hall. I can ask whether they would consent to a sealed consultation with a Covenant jurist. Or I can tell them I think the matter may be important enough that delay carries risk."

"Can you preserve the memory itself without investigative disclosure?" Ilyan asked.

"With consent."

"Then start there," Rhyl said.

Mireth nodded.

Ilyan was not satisfied.

He hated that he was not satisfied.

"What if they refuse?"

Mireth looked at him.

"Then they refuse."

"Even if it matters?"

"Yes."

"Even if it could explain something about the people who died?"

Caelen's gaze shifted to him.

Mireth's did not.

"Yes."

Ilyan stood.

Thessa's hand fell away from his shoulder.

"I don't know if I can agree to that."

Nobody tried to stop him from pacing to the window.

Below, Covenant gardeners were tying young climbing roses to a trellis. Their voices drifted upward, indistinct.

"If someone knows something about Silver Night—"

"They own their memory," Mireth said.

"And the dead?"

"Do not do that."

Her voice was quiet.

It stopped him more effectively than anger would have.

Mireth rose.

"Do not put the dead on the other side of the scale from a frightened living person and ask me to choose which one deserves care."

Ilyan's face went hot.

"That isn't what I meant."

"I know."

She came closer.

"That is why I am telling you what it sounded like."

He looked away.

The workroom was very still.

This was the cost they had been circling.

Not the possibility that one of them would become a traitor.

The possibility that two loyal people could disagree because their duties protected different things.

Ilyan protected an investigation into murdered students.

Mireth protected the autonomy of a living petitioner.

Neither duty vanished because they loved the same dead friends.

"I'm sorry," he said.

Mireth's shoulders lowered.

"I know."

"I still think delay can destroy evidence."

"It can."

"And you still think consent comes first."

"In this case, yes."

He nodded.

The disagreement remained.

Strangely, that made the apology feel more real.

Caelen stood and offered Mireth his hand.

She took it without looking at him.

Ilyan watched their fingers close together and felt, not jealousy, not exclusion, but gratitude that someone was beside her who did not need to solve the argument for her.

Damaris tapped the clean page.

"Then this is another practice. We can disagree about a protected person's decision without treating the person who keeps the confidence as disloyal."

"And," Mireth said, "the keeper of the confidence does not get to demand that everyone approve of the consequence."

Damaris wrote that too.

---

They finished after midnight.

The page was not a pact. Mireth had won that argument.

At the top, in Lyrion's hand, it read:

**Things We Owe One Another When We Cannot Tell One Another Everything.**

Rhyl had drawn a third deer beneath it.

Damaris had allowed this one to remain.

There were nine practices by the end.

They would not ask for oath-breaking as proof of affection. A refusal to disclose would not itself prove betrayal or innocence. When protected knowledge changed another person's immediate safety, legal exposure, professional duty or required action, the holder would disclose the consequence as far as lawfully possible. Advice based on protected information would remain advice, not proof. Deliberate deception would be acknowledged when its protective reason ended, where lawful. Gossip would be labeled gossip. Corroboration would be sought without laundering confidential sources through friendship. Urgent danger could require choices no private rule could make painless. And nobody would treat a source, client, witness, lover or relative who trusted one of the Seven as though they had automatically trusted all seven.

At the bottom, Mireth added the sentence they had earned through argument:

**We may remain angry with one another and still remain loyal.**

Nobody objected.

They did not sign it.

That had been Caelen's objection.

"If we sign it," he had said, "someone will eventually find it and decide we founded a secret society."

Lyrion had looked around the table.

"To be fair, this is exactly how secret societies begin."

So they folded the paper and gave it to Mireth, whose workroom had produced it and whose filing system was the least likely to convert a friendship problem into Crown correspondence.

Outside, the city had gone quiet.

They separated by habit and destination.

Damaris left with Rhyl, arguing softly about whether the deer improved the document. Caelen walked Mireth toward the Covenant residences. Lyrion disappeared into the night with the serene confidence of someone who always seemed to know where the last open kitchen was.

Thessa and Ilyan walked toward his rooms.

For two streets neither spoke.

Then Thessa said, "You were unfair to Mireth."

"I know."

"Good."

"You don't have to sound pleased about it."

"I am pleased you know. Saves time."

He bumped his shoulder against hers.

She bumped back harder.

At the next corner she caught his hand.

"You are going to do it to me eventually."

Ilyan knew what she meant.

"Probably."

"You are going to ask me for something the Wardens will not let me give you."

"Probably."

"And I am going to hate saying no."

He stopped beneath a flowering pear tree.

Petals had fallen across the pavement like pale scraps of paper.

"Then say no."

Thessa looked at him.

"You say that now."

"I mean it now."

"That's not the same thing."

"No."

He stepped closer.

"But it is what I can promise. I won't make you prove you love me by betraying your work."

Her expression changed slowly.

The teasing left first.

Then some guarded thing he had not realized was there.

"That," she said, "was annoyingly well said."

"I have moments."

"Rarely."

She kissed him under the pear tree.

There was nothing hurried in it. Her hands settled at his waist. His found the back of her coat. The city was not empty; somewhere behind them a door opened and closed, and a carriage passed at the far end of the street. Neither cared enough to stop.

When Thessa drew back, she rested her forehead against his.

"My aunt is still going to eat you alive at supper."

"I had almost forgotten."

"Liar."

"See? Our new practices are already working."

She laughed against his mouth.

Then they went home.

---

The next morning, Vaun received two replies.

The first came from the Crown archive.

The paired receiving record had not yet been located.

The second came from a successor registry attached to the old Joint Emergency Coordination apparatus.

Sael read it twice before handing it across the desk.

The response did not identify a witness. It did not identify a destination. It did not say whether Corren had exercised the selector.

It said something narrower.

During the two days of Corren's documented custody, the receiving-side authority associated with the protected continuity mechanism had been **temporarily reassigned under emergency reserve procedure**.

The surviving index did not include the operative name in the unsealed portion.

It did include the authority class.

**Joint receiving authority — external continuity reserve.**

Ilyan read the line once.

Then again.

Caelen's undisclosed warning had been right.

A missing exercise record would not be enough.

Someone had changed the other half of the lock.

Vaun took the paper back.

"Now we ask who had lawful power to make that reassignment."

Ilyan thought of Caelen in the corridor. Mireth in the workroom. Rhyl refusing Damaris over breakfast. Thessa beneath the pear tree.

The Seven had spent a day learning how much friendship could survive without access.

Now the investigation had produced a question none of them could answer merely by trusting harder.

That was probably healthy.

It was certainly inconvenient.

And in three nights, Ilyan was expected to sit at Lady Maevra Eris's table while half the city tried to work out what the Seven knew.

He folded the registry reply.

"Send the authority request."

Sael reached for a clean form.

Outside the Hall, Bloomtide bells were sounding Crownday business into motion.

The city was listening.

This time, the Seven had finally decided what they owed one another before it asked them to choose.
