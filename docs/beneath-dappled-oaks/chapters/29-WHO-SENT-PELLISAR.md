# Chapter Twenty-Nine — Who Sent Pellisar

Mireth woke because Caelen was trying very carefully not to wake her.

This would have been more convincing if he had not been half out of the bed, tangled in the sheet, reaching for a shirt that had somehow travelled beneath the chair.

She watched him for several seconds.

The room smelled of rain, extinguished lamp oil, crushed starflower from the little vase on the sill, and the warm private closeness of two people who had spent considerably less of the night sleeping than either had intended.

Caelen found the shirt.

Mireth said, "Coward."

He froze with one arm in a sleeve.

"I was being considerate."

"You were escaping before my mother could discover you."

"Your mother knows I am here."

"My mother knew you were here before you did."

"That is exactly why I am escaping."

Mireth rolled onto her back and laughed into the pillow.

The sheet slid to her waist. Caelen looked at her, looked determinedly toward the window, then looked at her again with the defeated honesty she had come to enjoy in him.

There were people who became smoother after intimacy. Caelen had become worse at pretending not to want her.

It was immensely satisfying.

"Come back."

"I have drill."

"In an hour."

"Fifty-two minutes."

"You counted?"

"I am a professional."

"You were not especially professional last night."

His ears reddened.

Mireth's smile widened.

He abandoned the shirt on the floor again.

When he came back to the bed she caught him by the wrist, pulled him down, and kissed him with the lazy confidence of someone no longer negotiating whether a kiss was permitted. His weight settled beside her rather than over her. One of his hands found her waist beneath the sheet and stopped there, warm and familiar enough already to make her body answer before her thoughts had assembled themselves.

The first time they had kissed, she had needed to say that the choice was hers.

Last night she had needed to say it again.

Not because Caelen had doubted her.

Because everyone else had been so eager to own the meaning.

His mother's ribbon. Her mother's dinner. House Avariel-Caith wondering what a Vael attachment might do for court access. House Vael wondering what a Crown-trained martial officer might mean for Mireth's future. Cousins watching how long they stood together at receptions. Aunts learning the difference between a friendly visit and a door closed after midnight.

They had spent weeks refusing to let other people turn desire into policy.

Eventually Mireth had become irritated by the possibility that refusing the policy might also mean refusing themselves.

So she had invited him upstairs.

Nothing about the night had been strategic.

That, she suspected, would become politically inconvenient almost immediately.

Caelen kissed the corner of her mouth.

"You're thinking."

"Terrible habit."

"About what?"

"Whether your mother has a clerk assigned to the question of my bedsheets."

He made a wounded noise.

"Do not say things like that while I am touching you."

"Why?"

"Because I would like to retain some respect for my family."

His hand tightened slightly at her waist.

Mireth felt the laugh leave her.

There it was again: the sudden narrowing of the world that still surprised her, even after a night in which surprise had repeatedly proved itself renewable. The room did not disappear. The rain still ticked at the glass. Somewhere below them a servant opened the kitchen shutters. A cart rattled over the street.

But Caelen was close, warm, unguarded, and looking at her as if the fact of her body beside his remained astonishing.

She touched the small scar beneath his jaw.

His eyes closed for a moment.

The tenderness of that reaction affected her more than the practiced courtly beauty of half the capital.

"Stay until forty minutes," she murmured.

"Forty-five."

"Forty-two."

"Done."

He kissed her again.

By the time he actually left, he had twenty-seven.

---

Lady Sereth Vael-Aesryn was at breakfast.

Of course she was.

Mireth descended wearing a fresh dress and the expression of a woman who had done nothing remotely noteworthy with her evening.

Her mother looked up from a plate of honeyed oats.

"Good morning."

"Good morning."

"Sleep well?"

Mireth poured tea.

"Perfectly."

Sereth buttered toast.

"How fortunate."

Mireth waited.

Her mother ate the toast.

This was unbearable.

"You're not going to say anything?"

"About what?"

Mireth narrowed her eyes.

Sereth's composure broke first. Her mouth curved.

"Oh, darling. I am one hundred and forty-three years old. I was not waiting downstairs with a candle."

"That would have been horrifying."

"It would also have been cold."

Mireth hid her face behind the cup.

Her mother let her suffer for another moment.

Then, more gently, "Are you happy?"

Mireth lowered the tea.

"Yes."

"Good."

"That's all?"

"No. There are approximately thirty additional questions, several of which are none of my business and most of which I will not ask before breakfast."

Mireth laughed.

Sereth reached for the jam.

"The one question that is my business is whether you still feel the choice was yours."

"Yes."

"Then I am pleased for you."

A pause.

"And politically irritated."

"There she is."

"Do not be smug. You have transformed a courtship people were already trying to interpret into an intimate association they will interpret more aggressively."

"I know."

"His family will know soon."

"I know."

"Your cousins already know."

Mireth stopped.

Sereth's smile became positively wicked.

"Caelen left through the front door with his shirt buttoned incorrectly."

"Oh, gods."

"Third and fourth buttons."

"He was late."

"I assumed enthusiasm rather than blindness."

Mireth put her forehead on the table.

Her mother patted her hair.

"The children are having sex," Sereth said solemnly to nobody. "Civilization trembles."

"Mother."

"Eat your breakfast. You have Covenant work."

That was the end of it.

Which was somehow more embarrassing than a lecture.

---

By midmorning Mireth was elbow-deep in root rot.

The Green Covenant had assigned her to a glasshouse where three imported silver pears had developed black lesions around their lower roots. Scholar Silae cared very little about Mireth's newly complicated romantic life and considerably more about whether the infection had entered the irrigation bed.

Mireth loved her for it.

She worked with her sleeves rolled above the elbow, hair tied back, yesterday's pleasure reduced to an occasional private warmth when she remembered Caelen's face at some particularly unguarded moment.

It did not make her less competent.

It did not make the pears less diseased.

It simply existed alongside everything else.

At noon a Crown courier delivered a note.

Not from Caelen.

From Ilyan.

**Pellisar tasking order is finally open at chair-secretariat level. Damaris is coming because apparently commerce has taught her how to smell a lie through paper. Vaun says you may attend if Covenant permits.**

Below it, in another hand:

**He means accounting. He is becoming dramatic again. — D**

Mireth smiled.

Silae read the note over her shoulder.

"Go."

"I haven't asked."

"You have looked at the door four times."

"I was thinking about lunch."

"You were thinking about the investigation."

Mireth untied her apron.

Silae added, without looking up from the diseased root, "And tell Caelen that if he distracts one of my junior affiliates into arriving late again, I will invoice the Crown for lost horticultural labor."

Mireth stared.

Silae finally looked at her.

"I am two hundred and eleven."

Apparently everyone in Aurelith had conspired to make privacy impossible.

---

The chair-secretariat archive had none of the romance of secrecy.

It had grey shelves.

It had grey tables.

It had a grey-haired clerk who disliked dust and had arranged three sharpened pencils at exact right angles to the blotter.

Pellisar Veyran's tasking file was twelve pages.

Ilyan had already read it once.

Damaris was reading it backward.

Mireth sat beside her.

"Why backward?"

"Because instructions are written to sound inevitable. Expenses are written by people who want to be paid."

"That did not answer me."

"It answered the better question."

Judge Vaun, across the table, did not look up.

Ilyan pushed the first page toward Mireth.

Pellisar had not been appointed to investigate a family.

He had been appointed to a rotating technical assessor seat in a broader concentration review.

The initiating instruction was dull enough to inspire confidence.

Review licensed-office concentration across the projected ten-year appointment horizon. Test whether declared affiliations, ordinary succession, expected retirements and known eligibility pipelines produced excessive persistence within any professional or kinship-linked cluster. Report methodological weaknesses. Recommend lawful mitigations where indicated.

No Cluster G.

No private marriage predictions.

No named family.

No instruction to identify students.

Mireth turned the page.

The first aggregate run had produced seven clusters worth secondary review.

Cluster G was one.

"Did Pellisar choose it?" she asked.

"Not initially," Ilyan said.

Damaris tapped a table.

"The model ranked it."

The scoring memorandum was mechanical. Cluster G entered the secondary band because its projected successor depth remained high after ordinary dispersal assumptions were applied.

Pellisar had inherited it because the assessor rotation placed him over the relevant subreview.

Mireth sat back.

"So nobody sent him after G."

"Not in the first instance," Vaun said.

Ilyan pointed to page six.

"Then this."

Three days after the secondary clusters were distributed, the chair-secretariat issued a general methodological instruction to every assessor.

**Stress-test persistence assumptions against ordinary changes in adult affiliation, including marriage, order entry, household transfer, cadet formation and recognized secondary-household obligations. Do not request protected personal information absent separate authority.**

Mireth read it twice.

"That sounds reasonable."

"It is," Damaris said.

"Annoyingly," Ilyan added.

Vaun gave him a look.

He sighed. "Usefully."

The instruction did not tell Pellisar to predict anyone's marriage.

It told every assessor to test whether a concentration model collapsed when people did ordinary adult things.

Mireth thought of breakfast.

Of Caelen's badly buttoned shirt.

Of her mother already calculating what public recognition of their intimacy might mean without believing she owned it.

The state had the same problem in uglier language. People did not remain where a diagram put them. They married, took lovers, acknowledged children, entered orders, formed households, moved between cities, refused family expectations, accepted them, inherited unexpectedly, or fell in love with someone politically inconvenient.

A ten-year model that assumed none of that would be useless.

"What did Pellisar do differently?"

Damaris turned another page.

"He wanted to make the uncertainty smaller."

There was the hinge.

Pellisar's first memorandum did not ask the Academy for names. It complained that declared affiliation could not adequately predict dispersal in Cluster G because the cluster's successor depth remained unusually high under every ordinary assumption he tested.

Then he proposed improving the model.

Not by asking whether marriage generally changed affiliation.

By asking whether particular eligible people were likely to form particular kinship links.

The independent statistician objected.

The chair narrowed the request.

The Academy refused the protected field.

Pellisar wrote, in a margin that had survived because bureaucrats occasionally preserved their own irritation:

**Then the model knowingly retains avoidable error.**

Damaris stared at the sentence.

"Oh, I know that man."

"You knew Pellisar?" Mireth asked.

"No. I know that sentence."

She leaned back.

"Every merchant who has ever demanded a customer's private correspondence because it would improve a risk estimate has written that sentence in some form."

Ilyan looked at the page.

"So he crossed the line because he wanted a better model."

"That is what this supports," Vaun said. "Not why he wanted it badly enough to press after objection."

"Was he told to press?"

They kept reading.

No instruction ordered him to do so.

No private note from Valeric representation appeared.

No Dmitrigical directive named Cluster G.

No House letter sat behind the tasking order.

What did exist was a disposition memorandum after the Academy refused the predictive field.

The chair recorded the refusal.

The statistician's objection was summarized.

Pellisar's model was ordered to proceed using aggregate assumptions only.

And the disposition was circulated to the standing liaison offices.

Mireth's finger stopped on the distribution list.

Dmitrigical Review.

Valeric representation.

Two other professional bodies.

Academy liaison.

Civic appointments office.

"Before Silver Night?"

Ilyan nodded.

"Months."

The room became very quiet.

Not because the page proved guilt.

It did not.

It proved something narrower and, in its way, more dangerous.

The relevant institutional circles had been formally told that the concentration review had encountered a predictive-kinship problem, that an assessor wanted person-level information, that the Academy would not provide it, and that the model would continue without it.

People who later wanted a faster, more precise, less lawful answer could have known where lawful procedure stopped.

Could.

Mireth heard Vaun's voice before the judge spoke.

"Do not make the page confess more than it knows."

"I wasn't."

"You were about to."

"I was thinking."

"Very loudly."

Ilyan laughed.

Mireth glared at him.

Damaris had gone back to the distribution list.

"Notice is not instruction."

"No," Ilyan said.

"Representation is not authorship."

"No."

"But the refusal wasn't buried inside the Academy."

"No."

Damaris tapped the page.

"That is new."

---

Rhyl was waiting outside the River Ledger when Damaris finished.

He had mud on one boot, a narrow scratch across his left cheek and a paper parcel tucked under his arm.

Damaris stopped on the steps.

"What happened to your face?"

"Branch."

"Did you win?"

"It has been warned."

She kissed him before he could say anything else.

The River Ledger was emptying around them. Clerks passed with cases and umbrellas. A senior factor glanced at Damaris, glanced at Rhyl, and continued walking with the serene indifference of someone who had survived enough office romances to classify them beneath weather.

Rhyl's free hand settled at Damaris's waist.

The kiss lengthened.

She had spent the afternoon reading instructions written by people terrified of uncertainty. Rhyl smelled of rain, leather and pine resin, and there was something profoundly restorative about wanting something that did not require a memorandum.

When they separated, he held up the parcel.

"Food."

"I love you."

His eyebrows rose.

Damaris felt her own words arrive half a heartbeat after she had said them.

Well.

There it was.

She could have retreated.

She did not.

Rhyl looked at her for a long moment.

Then he said, "I brought onion cakes."

"That is not an answer."

"No."

"Rhyl."

"I am trying not to answer because you are hungry."

She stared at him.

His mouth twitched.

"I love you too."

Damaris kissed him again with considerably less concern for the Ledger steps.

---

They ate the onion cakes in her rooms.

At least, they ate some of them.

One survived until morning.

Damaris woke tangled in linen, warm, pleasantly sore in the vague and thoroughly nonprofessional way of someone who had made several excellent decisions and at least one questionable one involving a windowsill.

Rhyl was asleep beside her on his stomach, one arm under the pillow.

His shirt hung from the lamp.

Her stockings had achieved separate political constituencies on opposite sides of the room.

Damaris considered the evidence.

Then she pulled the sheet over her face and laughed.

Rhyl stirred.

"Morning?"

"No."

He opened one eye.

"No?"

"I have reconsidered time."

"Can you do that?"

"My family has committees."

"Ah."

She lowered the sheet.

The scratch on his cheek looked absurdly innocent.

She touched it.

Rhyl turned his head and kissed her palm.

The gesture was so gentle that it reached somewhere the previous night's heat had not.

Damaris became still.

This was the dangerous part.

Not nakedness. Not desire. Not the delighted loss of dignity that had reduced two competent adults to muffled laughter because the bedframe had objected to their enthusiasm.

This.

Morning.

Being seen after.

Rhyl watching her without asking her to perform confidence.

"You're thinking," he said.

"Everyone keeps accusing me of that."

"Bad habit."

She smiled.

Then she remembered the disposition memorandum.

The smile faded.

Rhyl noticed.

He did not ask immediately.

That was one of the reasons she loved him.

Damaris sat up and gathered the sheet around herself.

"We found the Pellisar chain."

"Found who sent him?"

"Yes and no."

She told him.

Broad review. Model-selected cluster. General instruction to stress-test ordinary adult affiliation. Pellisar's own move into person-level predictive kinship. Statistician's objection. Academy refusal. Chair-imposed boundary.

Then the circulation list.

Rhyl listened.

"So the people we're worried about knew the lawful process had hit a wall."

"Some offices containing people we're worried about received notice that the process had hit a wall."

"That's more annoying."

"Yes."

"Probably more useful too."

"Also yes."

He sat up beside her.

The sheet shifted. Damaris looked at him.

Rhyl caught the look.

"Are you listening?"

"Heroically."

"To which part?"

"Several parts."

He laughed.

She leaned into him, forehead against his shoulder.

For a little while the investigation waited.

Not because it had ceased to matter.

Because they were adults, alive after a night that had once seemed impossible to imagine when the Academy courtyard was full of empty chairs.

The dead did not require them to be miserable every hour.

Damaris had taken longer to learn that than she liked.

Rhyl kissed her hair.

"What happens next?"

She knew he meant the investigation.

Probably.

"We ask what the lawful remedies could actually do."

"And couldn't."

"Yes."

She traced the scratch on his cheek again.

"The review had options. Recusal. Stagger appointments. Train more candidates. Broaden recruitment. Disclosure rules. Slow, boring institutional things."

"Things that take years."

"Exactly."

Rhyl's expression changed.

"Silver Night was fast."

Damaris looked at him.

There it was.

Not proof.

A comparison.

The legitimate process had identified a concentration problem and proposed remedies designed to alter institutions over time.

Somebody else had helped create a catastrophe that altered a generation in a night.

Between those facts remained a gap large enough to ruin lives if they crossed it carelessly.

But for the first time, the shape of the gap was visible.

Damaris reached for the notebook on her bedside table.

Rhyl caught her wrist.

"No."

"What?"

"You are not writing an evidentiary memorandum naked."

She looked down at herself.

Then at him.

"That sounds like a rule invented by cowards."

"It is a rule invented by a man who would like breakfast."

She considered.

"Onion cake?"

"One survived."

"Then civilization may continue."

---

Thessa's western letter reached Ilyan that afternoon.

The wagon tallies were worse.

Two wagons billed against the replacement-stone purchase had never entered the quarry yard on the dates claimed.

A third had been retired from district service eleven months earlier after an axle failure and sold for parts.

The subcontractor named on the road account remembered providing hauling crews during the flood response but denied selling stone.

Thessa had not accused him of theft.

Ilyan could tell this had cost her spiritually.

She was following the payment instrument next.

At the bottom she had written:

**If the money also turns out to be imaginary I am going to throw a clerk into a drainage ditch. Not a particular clerk. I will select one by lawful rotation.**

Ilyan laughed.

Then he read the postscript.

**I received your oral-testimony remark. You are a menace. I have composed several responses unsuitable for government post. Assume vigorous rebuttal upon my return.**

He folded the letter carefully.

Across Vaun's workroom, Damaris was explaining the Pellisar disposition to Neral. Mireth had returned to the Covenant. Caelen was at drill. Rhyl had vanished north again after, according to Damaris, "a morning of extremely important diplomatic negotiations," which caused Vaun to look over her spectacles and Damaris to become interested in a wall.

Ilyan chose not to ask.

He was learning.

He set Pellisar's original tasking beside the disposition memorandum.

The answer to the chapter's question was less satisfying than conspiracy and more useful than suspicion.

Who sent Pellisar?

The chair-secretariat sent him into a legitimate concentration review.

The model sent Cluster G into his secondary workload.

A general methodological instruction told him to test whether ordinary adult affiliations would disperse projected concentration.

Pellisar himself proposed crossing from aggregate assumptions into predictive private kinship because he believed the model remained unnecessarily inaccurate without it.

The institution stopped him.

And then the institution told its standing liaisons that it had stopped him.

That was as far as the evidence went.

Ilyan wrote it down.

No adjectives.

No accusation.

No convenient villain hiding between the lines.

Then beneath it he wrote the next question.

**What could the lawful remedies not do?**

Vaun read over his shoulder.

"Good."

"Only good?"

"Would you prefer a medal?"

"After this many registers, perhaps."

"You may have a biscuit."

"I withdraw my complaint."

Rain moved across the windows.

Somewhere west, Thessa was following money through bad roads.

Somewhere across the city, Mireth was probably pretending that half her household had not noticed Caelen leave in the morning.

Damaris had a faint mark below one ear she had unsuccessfully attempted to hide with her collar, and Ilyan was sufficiently devoted to continued life not to mention it.

The Seven were no longer students waiting together beneath the same oak for adulthood to happen to them.

They were scattered through offices, glasshouses, roads, barracks, bedrooms, archives and wet country districts. They were being watched, employed, desired, used, loved and occasionally very obviously kept awake past sensible hours.

Silver Night had tried to erase a future before they reached it.

The future, Ilyan thought, was proving stubborn.

He turned the page.
