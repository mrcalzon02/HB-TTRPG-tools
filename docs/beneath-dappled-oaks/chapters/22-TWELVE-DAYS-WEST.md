# Chapter Twenty-Two — Twelve Days West

Eight days was long enough to become a nuisance and too short to become a season.

Thessa discovered this on the first morning after the receiving abstract, when a Western Warden clerk delivered three packets to the Eris-Vey house before breakfast and informed her that one contained revised bridge tables, one contained her temporary posting authority, and one contained a list of things she was forbidden to bring because previous assessment officers had apparently mistaken a three-month road survey for colonization.

The forbidden list included personal tents, private horses, more than two formal coats, decorative armor, unregistered enchanted cookware, and "household staff beyond one approved field servant."

Thessa read the last item twice.

"Who brought a household?"

The clerk, a square-faced woman with iron-grey hair and no interest in gossip before tea, said, "Lord Hareth Eris-Daeron."

"How many?"

"Eleven."

"To inspect roads?"

"His valet maintained that mud was hostile to dignity."

Thessa put the paper down.

"Was he wrong?"

"Not in a way relevant to transport assessment."

That was how the last eight days began: not with grief, nor vows, nor the kind of dramatic farewell that poets preferred, but with baggage regulations.

Ilyan arrived while she was arguing over whether a second pair of field boots counted as professional equipment or evidence of decadent eastern softness.

He had brought breakfast wrapped in cloth from the corner bakery and an expression suggesting he had slept better than he wanted anyone to know.

"Two pairs," he said after hearing the dispute.

Thessa looked at him. "Traitor."

"I have seen what one pair becomes after three days of rain."

The clerk pointed at him. "Keep him."

"I intend to."

The words came out easily enough that neither of them moved for half a heartbeat.

Then the clerk asked whether he was going west too.

"No," Thessa said.

Ilyan said, "No."

The clerk marked something on her sheet and went back to boots.

The rest of the morning survived.

---

The investigation survived too.

That mattered more than Ilyan expected.

For weeks, every new document had seemed to require his presence as though the past might rearrange itself if he left the room. Judge Vaun cured him of this by refusing to indulge it.

The request comparing the initiating and receiving copies of JEC-R/6 went forward without him. So did the petition for the commission's formation minutes. Examiner Sael sent him to his ordinary duties and, when Ilyan asked whether anything had arrived, looked over the top of a register and said, "If it had, you would be told."

"That sounds dangerously like trust."

"It is delegation. Do not romanticize it."

So Ilyan copied oath commentary, attended a lecture on evidentiary contamination, and spent an afternoon helping Thessa decide which shirts could survive three months of western laundries.

The Seven learned the same lesson in different forms.

Damaris had grain contracts to review with her aunt. Rhyl disappeared for two days with Red Hart riders checking a route north of the city and returned smelling of pine smoke and horse. Mireth spent an entire Rootday at a Green Covenant hearing over a disputed orchard memory. Caelen was summoned to a Crown martial dinner and complained afterward that the most dangerous thing there had been the fish. Lyrion acquired invitations to three salons and claimed attendance at all three was a burden of state. Nobody believed him.

Their old student habit had been to gather whenever anything happened.

Adult life did not permit that generosity.

By the sixth day before departure, they had managed one supper together.

It was held in Damaris's rooms because she possessed the largest table and because Rhyl had arrived with a basket of mushrooms that Mireth immediately confiscated for inspection.

"They are mushrooms," Rhyl said.

"That is what poisonous mushrooms rely upon."

"I ate one on the road."

Mireth stared at him.

"That is not evidence in your favor."

Damaris laughed into her wine.

Thessa sat between Ilyan and Lyrion while the room filled with the familiar disorder of people who had once eaten almost every meal within shouting distance of one another. It struck her, suddenly and painfully, that this was already becoming something they had to arrange.

Not lost. Arranged.

There was a difference.

Caelen was telling Damaris about the Crown dinner. Mireth had accepted the mushrooms after cutting three apart. Rhyl was stealing bread from Damaris's plate with such regularity that it had become unclear whether theft still described the arrangement. Lyrion had one ankle hooked around Thessa's chair and was reading her posting itinerary upside down.

"You are not taking the southern coach road," he said.

"No."

"Good. It is terrible."

"You have never traveled it."

"I have heard complaints from people whose standards I respect."

"Courtesans?"

"Caravan masters."

"Less reliable."

Damaris raised her glass. "Objectively false."

Ilyan smiled, but Thessa felt the small tension in his hand where it rested against her thigh beneath the table.

She covered it with hers.

He did not look at her.

That was all right.

They did not have to turn every touch into a discussion of leaving.

Later, when plates had been pushed aside and Caelen had begun an argument with Lyrion about whether diplomatic immunity extended to cheating at cards, Damaris asked Thessa what the western assignment actually required.

Not why she was going. Not whether she was sure. What the work was.

Thessa found herself grateful enough to answer at excessive length.

She would ride with a small assessment party through the western approach districts, compare reported road conditions against maintenance ledgers, inspect bridge load markings, review convoy delays, and report where military transport assumptions no longer matched civilian infrastructure. Some roads had been patched so many times that their official classifications were fiction. Several bridges were strong enough for ordinary carts but not for Warden supply trains. Two mining districts were complaining that military priority rules were damaging ore traffic. Everyone involved believed somebody else had caused the problem.

"So," Rhyl said, "you are going west to discover that roads are made of politics."

"Roads are made of stone," Thessa said.

Damaris shook her head. "Accounts."

"Mud," said Mireth.

"Promises," said Lyrion.

Caelen considered. "Dead horses, if badly managed."

They looked at Ilyan.

He had been watching Thessa.

"Distance," he said.

The room quieted for exactly long enough to become dangerous.

Then Thessa kicked him under the table.

"Appalling."

"I was invited to contribute."

"You were invited to be less miserable."

"That was not stated."

Lyrion leaned across the table. "Ilyan, as your friend, I must tell you that if you become poetic before she has even left, we will be forced to kill you."

"Again?" Caelen asked.

"Socially."

"Much slower."

The room recovered.

Thessa loved them for it.

---

Five days before departure, her aunt Maevra inspected her kit.

Lady Maevra Eris did not do this because she doubted the Warden quartermaster. She did it because she had spent enough of her life around military organizations to understand that quartermasters could be entirely correct while officers remained capable of forgetting socks.

She found no missing socks.

She did remove one formal gown.

"You need one."

"I have two dinners on the route."

"The same gown can survive two dinners."

"People will notice."

"Then they will know you are working."

Thessa folded her arms.

Maevra looked at the second gown, then at her.

"Is this for work or because you expect someone interesting?"

"Aunt."

"You are an adult. I am asking a logistical question."

Thessa laughed despite herself.

"Work."

"Then one."

The second gown vanished.

Maevra sat on the edge of the trunk and ran one finger down the posting order.

"You asked for this kind of work before graduation."

"I know."

"Good. Remember that."

Thessa's amusement faded.

Maevra's gaze remained on the paper.

"People will say you were sent away because of Valeric. Others will say you were rewarded because of him. Some will decide your aunt wanted you removed from the investigation. Some will decide I wanted a pair of Eris eyes in the west while everyone here stared at dead students. Most of them will be wrong in ways that flatter themselves."

"And the ones who are right?"

"Will still have to prove it."

Thessa smiled.

Maevra did not.

"You wanted field assessment before you knew what his mouth tasted like. Do not surrender that fact to gossip merely because gossip is louder."

Heat climbed Thessa's face.

"You could have phrased that differently."

"Why?"

"Because you are my aunt."

"I have been your aunt longer than you have been embarrassed by sex."

"That is not helping."

"It was not intended to."

Then Maevra's expression softened.

"Go west because you chose west. Miss him because you chose him too. They are not competing truths unless you make them compete."

Thessa looked down at the posting order.

"Three months is not very long."

"No."

"It feels long."

"Yes."

Maevra stood and kissed her forehead.

"Take the better boots."

---

Four days before departure, Ilyan received a note from Vaun.

The paired-copy comparison had been authorized. The comparing clerk would report only whether the notice classes matched and whether either copy contained a contemporaneous objection to authenticity or timing. Nothing else.

No result yet.

He read the note once, folded it, and put it away.

Thessa watched him from the other side of his room.

"That's all?"

"That's all."

"You're not going to the Hall?"

"There is nothing to go there for."

She smiled.

"Look at you."

"Do not make this unpleasant."

"I would never."

She was packing his room with herself in small ways without meaning to. A hair ribbon on the washstand. Her spare comb beside his. A Warden belt draped over his chair. Two stockings drying by the window because rain had caught them between houses.

He had begun noticing these things because soon they would be absent.

That evening they went nowhere.

They ate cold chicken, cheese, and pears in bed because neither wanted to dress again after bathing. Thessa read bridge tables until Ilyan took them from her hands. She retaliated by taking his book. He kissed the inside of her wrist. She put her foot against his chest and told him he was using unfair methods.

"Effective methods."

"That is how corruption begins."

"With your ankle?"

"Many institutions have fallen for less."

Later, with the lamps low and rain moving softly against the windows, she lay with her head on his shoulder and said, "I am excited."

He was quiet for a moment.

"I know."

"I needed to say it."

"I know that too."

"Are you angry?"

"No."

She lifted her head.

He met her eyes.

"Jealous," he said. "Of roads, apparently. Which is humiliating."

Her mouth twitched.

"Bridges too?"

"Especially bridges. They get your full attention."

"Some of them are very large."

"Cruel woman."

She kissed him.

There was nothing hurried in it. They had spent enough nights together now to know the shape of one another's pauses, where laughter belonged, which touches meant more and which merely felt good. The coming separation sharpened everything without making any of it new.

Afterward she slept with one leg thrown over his and her hand curled against his ribs.

Ilyan remained awake a little longer.

He did not promise himself that distance would change nothing.

That seemed childish.

Distance would change things because distance was a thing.

The better promise was that they would notice what it changed.

---

Two days before departure, the Seven failed to meet at all.

This was, Thessa thought, perhaps the most convincing evidence that they had graduated.

Messages crossed the city instead.

Mireth sent dried mint for the road and a note telling Thessa not to let western cooks murder it in boiling water. Caelen sent a small whetstone stamped with the Crown quartermaster's mark and swore in writing that it had been acquired lawfully. Damaris sent a folded list of reputable inns on the first half of the route, annotated with which proprietors extended credit and which considered credit a personal insult. Rhyl sent nothing until evening, when a Red Hart runner arrived with a compact field knife and no note at all.

Lyrion appeared at the Eris-Vey door after dark carrying wine.

"I am the gift," he announced.

"Return yourself."

"No receipt."

He stayed an hour.

They did not speak much about the investigation. They spoke about Academy instructors, about the first time Thessa had broken a practice spear over Caelen's shoulder, about Mireth once convincing them that a pond spirit demanded pastries, about dead classmates without making every memory a funeral.

When Lyrion finally stood to leave, he hugged Thessa hard enough to make her grunt.

"Write," he said into her hair.

"You hate letters."

"I hate writing them. Receiving them is delightful."

"Parasite."

"Diplomat."

He kissed her temple and left.

Thessa stood in the doorway after him for a while.

---

Departure morning arrived clear.

That seemed rude.

The Warden yard was awake before dawn, lanterns burning along the stable wall while grooms moved between horses and baggage frames. The assessment party was small: Thessa, a senior road engineer named Captain Veyra Sorn, two Warden riders, a civilian bridge clerk, a survey mage, and six support staff rotating with established posts along the route. No private household. No eleven servants. One poetry book.

The western road left through Gateward Arch and bent toward the hills where the first sunlight had begun to turn the stone pale gold.

Thessa wore field grey beneath a dark green riding coat, her posting badge new enough to catch every lantern. Her hair was braided close for travel. Ilyan had watched her do it in silence before dawn.

Now the Seven were present in fragments.

Damaris had arrived first because merchants respected departure times. Rhyl came with her, still fastening one glove. Mireth carried a paper packet of breakfast cakes. Caelen arrived in uniform because he was due at the palace immediately afterward. Lyrion came last and denied being late because the horses had not moved yet.

They crowded Thessa badly.

That helped.

Mireth made her promise to eat. Damaris made her promise not to let Warden accounting officers classify every delay as weather when contractors were plainly at fault. Caelen told her to avoid being crushed by a bridge. Rhyl advised her that if a bridge appeared likely to crush her, she should move.

"Excellent," Thessa said. "I am surrounded by expertise."

Lyrion kissed her cheek.

"Come back unbearable."

"I am already unbearable."

"More so. We must all improve."

Then there was Ilyan.

The others, with a competence born of friendship, found things to do several yards away.

Thessa looked at him.

He had dressed carefully. She could tell because every button was correct.

"Eight days," he said.

"You counted."

"I have skills."

"Three months."

"I know."

She touched the front of his coat.

Around them the yard continued: buckles, hooves, low voices, the creak of leather, Captain Sorn arguing with a groom about a survey case. Nobody stopped because two young adults were having an important morning.

That made it easier.

"I want to go," Thessa said.

"I know."

"I want to come back."

His expression changed.

"I know that too."

"Good."

He put one hand against the side of her neck.

"I don't want you to leave."

"Good."

He laughed once, softly.

"You are impossible."

"You have had ample warning."

She kissed him in the Warden yard with six friends pretending not to watch and half a military detachment genuinely too busy to care.

It was not a desperate kiss. That surprised her. She had expected desperation because songs had taught her that departure demanded it.

Instead it was familiar.

His thumb moved once beneath her ear. Her hand caught in his coat. He tasted of tea. She knew exactly how he would breathe when she drew away.

That familiarity hurt more than drama would have.

Captain Sorn called, "Eris-Vey."

Thessa closed her eyes.

"That's me."

"Unfortunately," Ilyan said.

She kissed him once more, quick and hard.

Then she turned away before either of them could invent a reason not to.

Her horse was a compact bay Warden mare named Brindle who had already decided Thessa was acceptable because Thessa carried apple pieces. She mounted, checked the baggage strap herself, and settled into the saddle.

The assessment party formed beneath Gateward Arch.

Thessa looked back.

The Seven had become six on the stones below her.

No.

That was wrong.

They were still seven.

They were simply no longer all in one place.

She raised two fingers to her brow.

Caelen returned the gesture. Rhyl lifted a hand. Mireth was crying without embarrassment. Damaris leaned against Rhyl's shoulder. Lyrion shouted something obscene enough that Captain Sorn looked interested.

Ilyan only watched her.

Thessa turned west.

The gate opened onto morning.

---

By noon, Ilyan had attended his examination block.

He considered this an achievement.

Sael did not congratulate him.

At the end of the session, the examiner handed him a sealed Hall notice.

Ilyan looked at it.

For one ugly instant he hated the timing.

Then he hated himself for hating it.

The world had not agreed to keep its revelations away from departure days.

He broke the seal.

The comparison clerk's answer contained two findings.

The initiating and receiving copies of JEC-R/6 recorded the **same notice class**.

The receiving copy recorded **no contemporaneous objection to the initiating notice's authenticity or timing**.

Nothing in the authorized comparison disclosed what the notice class was. Nothing established reception status. Nothing identified a bearer.

Ilyan read it twice.

Then he folded it.

"Well?" Sael asked.

"The copies agree. The receiver did not object to authenticity or timing. We still don't know the class."

Sael nodded.

"And?"

Ilyan looked toward the western windows.

"And Thessa is probably past the third toll marker by now."

Sael waited.

Ilyan put the notice into his case.

"Both things can be true today."

"Yes."

Outside, Bloomtide sunlight lay across the Hall steps. Couriers moved through the square. Somewhere west of the city, a Warden assessment party was measuring roads that would continue to exist whether the Hall understood Silver Night or not.

Ilyan went to find the others.
