# Chapter Five B — The Packet Remembered Twice

Rhyl found them in Aereth's room.

Judge Vaun had opened the shutters despite the rain. Grey light lay across the desk, the washstand and the narrow strip of carpet beside the bed. Her clerk was kneeling beside a travelling chest with a sheet of paper balanced on one knee. Talia stood near the door. Damaris sat beside Aereth at the window.

Aereth looked worse than when Rhyl had last seen him.

Not ill, exactly. Smaller.

That was a strange thing to think about a man who had spent most of Damaris's childhood filling rooms simply by entering them.

Rhyl stopped inside the doorway and took off his wet cloak.

Damaris looked at his empty hands first.

He liked that she did not ask whether Pellin had run.

"He's with his sister," Rhyl said.

Talia's shoulders loosened.

"Lessa?"

"Marsh lung. He says the healer isn't worried. Lessa disagrees with the healer's personality."

Talia almost smiled.

"That sounds like Lessa."

Vaun looked up from the washstand. "Did Pellin agree to speak with me?"

"After soup."

"Sensible."

"His sister insisted."

"More sensible."

Rhyl glanced at Damaris.

She had shifted just enough to make room beside her on the window bench.

He crossed to her and sat.

Their knees touched.

Neither moved.

Aereth watched that happen with the faint distracted recognition of an uncle noticing a courtship several rooms away from his immediate catastrophe.

"What did he say?" Vaun asked.

Rhyl gave it to her plainly.

The basket. The blue shaving cloth. The coat button. The mending room. The courier.

At the green-stone ring, Talia frowned.

At the cloves, Damaris did.

At the sealed packet, Aereth stopped breathing for a moment.

Rhyl noticed because everyone else did.

"Say that again," Aereth said.

Rhyl looked at Vaun.

She gave the smallest nod.

"Pellin says a River Ledger courier brought a council packet for you. He carried it upstairs. He says you told him you'd already read it."

Aereth's fingers closed around the edge of the chair.

"I had."

No one answered.

Aereth looked from face to face.

"I remember reading it."

Damaris turned toward him. "What do you remember?"

"The levy tables."

His answer came immediately.

"The western dredging appropriation. Two bridge requests. Varanel's objection to the warehouse assessment."

Damaris knew the names well enough to know none of that sounded invented for effect.

"Where were you?"

"Here."

"At the desk?"

"Yes."

"Who brought it?"

Aereth opened his mouth.

Nothing came.

His eyes moved toward the desk as though someone might still be standing there.

"I don't know."

Rhyl said, "Pellin says when he brought the packet, the wax was still across the fold."

Aereth stared at him.

"No."

Talia's hand tightened around the cuff of her sleeve.

Rhyl continued carefully. "He says you told him you'd read it. He pointed out it was sealed. You took it, broke the wax and read it."

"No."

The second denial was quieter.

Damaris felt Aereth's shoulder against hers.

He had gone rigid.

"Then later," Rhyl said, "Pellin says you asked Talia why the packet hadn't arrived."

Aereth looked at Talia.

She did not look away.

"You did," she said.

He closed his eyes.

"And you told me I'd been reading it."

"Yes."

"I thought you were mocking me."

"Yes."

He breathed out slowly.

Damaris watched the rain strike the sill.

Nobody filled the silence for him.

Finally Aereth said, "I remember it twice."

Vaun folded her hands.

"Tell me."

"I remember reading it before Pellin came."

He swallowed.

"And I remember him bringing it."

"Do either of those memories feel less real?"

Aereth laughed once.

There was no humor in it.

"Wouldn't that be convenient?"

Vaun waited.

He rubbed both hands over his face.

"No."

Damaris said, "Then don't choose one yet."

Aereth looked at her.

"You make that sound easy."

"It isn't."

"Which one do you believe?"

She considered lying to him for perhaps half a heartbeat.

Not because she wanted to deceive him.

Because he was frightened and she loved him and there were kinds of mercy that arrived wearing the same coat as dishonesty.

"I believe you remember both."

His face tightened.

"That isn't what I asked."

"It's what I know."

For a moment she thought he might be angry.

Instead he leaned back and stared at the ceiling.

"Gods. You really have become a Caladren."

"Cruel."

"Precise."

"Worse."

Rhyl's hand settled beside hers on the bench.

Not over it.

Close enough that the warmth of his little finger touched hers when either of them breathed too deeply.

Vaun went to the desk.

"Where is the packet now?"

Talia answered. "Document tray. Unless he moved it."

Aereth looked offended.

Then looked ashamed of being offended.

The tray held eleven folded papers, three council circulars, two invitations, a grain memorandum and the remains of a broken green wax seal.

Vaun did not touch anything at first.

"Which?"

Aereth pointed.

His finger shook.

The packet lay beneath a river-maintenance notice.

Vaun's clerk brought clean paper and a pair of narrow tongs from his case.

Rhyl watched Aereth watching them.

The man seemed fascinated by the care being taken with an object he had apparently handled several times without remembering all of them.

"Who else has touched it?" Vaun asked.

"Me," Aereth said.

Talia said, "Me. I put it in the tray after he left it on the breakfast table."

"Pellin," Rhyl added.

Vaun looked at him.

"According to Pellin."

Rhyl nodded.

"According to Pellin."

The judge turned the packet over.

The broken wax bore the council's river-office mark.

Nothing leapt from the paper. No hidden rune flared. No curse announced itself out of professional courtesy.

Vaun's clerk began noting the folds, the wax, the cord, the handwriting.

Damaris found herself relieved by the boredom of it.

Boredom meant somebody was still doing this properly.

"The courier," she said.

Rhyl nodded. "Pellin didn't know him."

"Ledger grey?"

"Yes."

"Badge?"

"He didn't mention one."

"Did you ask?"

"No."

Damaris looked at him.

Rhyl shrugged. "I asked what he remembered."

She smiled despite herself.

"Good."

He turned his hand on the bench.

Her little finger slipped between two of his.

Aereth noticed.

Again.

This time his mouth moved faintly.

Damaris refused to be embarrassed while an uncle was apparently losing arguments with his own memory.

She lasted nearly four seconds.

Then she pulled her hand back.

Rhyl's smile was unbearable.

"Do you have a receiving book?" he asked Talia.

"For the house? Of course."

"Couriers sign?"

"Usually."

"Usually?"

"If they're delivering to the steward's office. Personal packets sometimes go through the front porter. Council and commercial traffic goes in the book because Meris likes being able to prove exactly when everyone disappointed her."

Damaris said, "That is the most accurate description of my aunt's filing system I have ever heard."

Talia looked pleased.

Vaun said, "Bring it."

Talia left.

Aereth watched the sealed tincture bottle in Vaun's case.

"How long?"

"For the medicine?"

"Yes."

"The tribunal laboratory can give me a preliminary comparison by evening if nothing complicated appears. Longer if something does."

"And the powder?"

"Same."

Aereth nodded.

He looked toward the packet.

"I have a council session tomorrow."

Damaris turned sharply.

"No."

Aereth raised an eyebrow.

"You are not my physician."

"No, I'm the relative who has just listened to you describe remembering the same packet twice."

"The levy vote matters."

"So does not voting twice because you remember doing it once."

Rhyl coughed into his fist.

Aereth glared at him.

"Sorry."

He was not sorry.

Vaun said, "You needn't decide about tomorrow today."

Aereth looked at her.

"That sounded suspiciously reasonable."

"I practice."

Talia returned carrying a thick narrow ledger bound in brown leather.

She placed it on the cleared corner of the desk and opened to a ribbon.

"Three days ago."

The page was divided into morning and afternoon deliveries.

Coal. Wine. Two letters. A parcel from a tailor. Physician's tonic.

Then the council packet.

Damaris leaned over it.

The runner had signed in a quick angular hand.

**Eren Vale.**

Rhyl read it once.

"Know him?"

Talia shook her head.

Damaris did too.

The name meant nothing to her.

That was almost refreshing.

"Ledger runner registry will," she said.

Vaun looked at her.

"Can you query it without altering the record?"

"Yes."

"Can you query it without announcing this investigation to your family?"

Damaris's answer took longer.

Rhyl felt her go still beside him.

"No."

Aereth frowned. "Why not?"

"Because my cipher access is audited."

She looked at Vaun, not Aereth.

"If I search a bonded runner credential, the query will exist. Meris may not see it immediately, but the family can see that I used my access and what registry I touched."

Rhyl said nothing.

Damaris noticed that too.

She looked at him.

"You're allowed to have an opinion."

"I do."

"And?"

"You already know the cost."

"That isn't an opinion."

"Fine. My opinion is that if you do it, you should do it because you think it's worth doing, not because everyone in this room is looking at the girl with the useful family key."

Something warm and deeply inconvenient moved through her chest.

Aereth looked between them.

"Oh, that's going badly for both of you."

Damaris stared at him.

"You are not well enough to be funny."

"Apparently I remember being funny twice."

Even Vaun smiled.

Damaris put both palms on the desk.

The name waited beneath them.

Eren Vale.

A young man in good boots who smelled of cloves.

Maybe.

A green-stone ring.

Maybe.

A sealed packet Aereth remembered reading before it was opened.

That part, at least, had two witnesses now and one frightened participant.

"I'll query it," she said.

Vaun nodded once.

"Then we preserve the result and the audit trail."

Damaris looked at Rhyl.

"You can come with me."

"All right."

"You don't have to."

"I know."

That answer was becoming a problem.

They used the small writing room downstairs because it had a Ledger cipher plate set into the desk.

Meris's house had three.

Of course it did.

Damaris unlocked the brass cover with her personal token, pressed two fingers to the glass and waited while pale script rose beneath the surface.

Rhyl stood behind her shoulder.

Not close enough to read until she shifted aside.

She did.

"Search: bonded runners. Aurelith district. Eren Vale."

The plate dimmed.

A line of text appeared.

Then another.

Damaris read them twice.

"Well?" Rhyl asked.

"He exists."

"That's almost disappointing."

"Temporarily bonded runner credential. Issued six months ago. Commercial and council carriage."

She scrolled.

Then stopped.

"Suspended."

"When?"

"This morning."

Rhyl leaned closer.

"Why?"

Damaris read the administrative note.

"Duplicate sponsor seal."

Neither spoke for a moment.

"Meaning?" Rhyl asked.

"Meaning somebody found another credential sponsored under the same authorization seal. Could be clerical duplication. Could be fraud. Could be a legitimate replacement entered badly."

She looked at him.

"It means exactly what it says until we know more."

"You have been spending too much time with Vaun."

"I hate all of you."

"No, you don't."

She looked back at the plate before he could see how much she liked hearing that.

There was a route-history tab.

Damaris hesitated.

Rhyl saw it.

"More audit?"

"Same query session."

"Your choice."

She opened it.

Seven deliveries appeared beneath the credential.

Reedglass was the most recent.

The others meant little at first glance: a physician's cooperative, a bonded warehouse, a council annex, a private townhouse, a tribunal office.

Then Damaris reached the oldest line.

She stopped.

Rhyl read over her shoulder.

Neither of them needed to say the name aloud.

**Concord Academy of Aurelith.**

Four days before Silver Night.

Rhyl's hand settled on the back of her chair.

Damaris could hear the rain again.

"That doesn't tell us what he carried," he said.

"No."

"Or who received it."

"No."

"Or whether it had anything to do with Silver Night."

She turned her head.

"You really have been spending too much time with Vaun."

"Terrible influence."

Damaris copied the registry result onto clean paper, including the query number and audit mark.

Her handwriting remained steady.

She was proud of that.

Then she closed the route history and locked the cipher plate.

Somewhere in the Caladren system, a record now existed that Damaris Caladren-Ilyra had searched a suspended courier credential on a rainy Bloomtide afternoon.

Meris would eventually know.

Perhaps before dinner.

Damaris stood.

Rhyl was close enough that she had to tilt her head to look at him.

"Still think I should have done it?"

"You thought you should."

"Coward."

"Absolutely."

She kissed him.

Not long.

Not carefully either.

His hand caught her waist because otherwise she would have backed into the cipher desk.

For one bright moment the Academy, the courier, Aereth's memory and every watching House in Elaranth became somebody else's administrative disaster.

Then Damaris pulled away.

Rhyl looked pleasantly stunned.

"That," he said, "was not evidentiary discipline."

"Say another word and I'll deny it happened."

"Twice?"

She laughed despite herself.

Then they took the copied route history upstairs.
