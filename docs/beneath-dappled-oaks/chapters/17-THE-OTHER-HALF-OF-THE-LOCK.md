# Chapter Seventeen — The Other Half of the Lock

The first useful thing Examiner Sael said about JEC-R/6 was that everyone had been calling it a lock.

It was not.

A lock kept something shut.

The old Joint Emergency Coordination instruments had been designed to keep something possible.

That distinction occupied most of Ilyan's morning.

He sat at the long eastern table in the Hall of Veils with three copied forms, a pot of coffee that had ceased being hot before anyone admitted it was bad, and a growing suspicion that the people who had designed emergency witness procedure had hated simple verbs.

Sael had arranged the forms in chronological order.

The first authorized a receiving capacity.

The second vested it.

The third recorded exercise.

Ilyan looked from one to the next.

"So activation isn't use."

"No."

"Corren's memorandum says it was active."

"Yes."

"And the index says it activated the morning after Silver Night."

"Yes."

Ilyan waited.

Sael drank the coffee, made a face, and continued reading.

"You're enjoying this."

"Not the coffee."

"You know what I mean."

"I know you know what the forms mean now."

Ilyan leaned back.

The eastern windows stood open to a Bloomtide morning that had finally decided not to rain. The courtyard smelled of wet stone and young leaves. Somewhere below, a clerk laughed. The sound rose through the window and vanished among the shelves.

He touched the first form.

"Created means the offices agreed the mechanism could exist."

"Yes."

The second.

"Activated means someone lawfully occupied the receiving capacity."

"Or the capacity became available under the conditions written into the commission."

"Without anyone necessarily receiving anything."

"Correct."

The third.

"Exercise is the part we're actually trying to prove."

Sael nodded.

The exercise form was almost offensively plain. It had spaces for the initiating selector, the receiving authority, the class of protected material, a paired confirmation mark, and an accession destination that could itself be sealed under a separate authority.

No witness name was required on the face.

No route.

No physical destination.

The people who had designed the procedure had understood that a record could prove that something happened without announcing whom it happened to.

That made Ilyan like them slightly more than he had expected.

"If Corren exercised the selector," he said, "this existed."

"Something in this record class existed."

"And if he didn't?"

"It should not."

"Should."

Sael glanced at him.

"Eighteen years is a long time to develop affection for that word."

Ilyan looked again at the blank exercise form.

"Where would it have gone?"

"Not necessarily where the commission went."

"Of course not."

"That is not perversity."

"It feels like perversity."

"It is compartmentation."

"Which is perversity with a filing cabinet."

Sael's mouth moved despite himself.

"The receiving authority retained its own exercise proof. The initiating side retained a paired abstract. Under ordinary closure, the two abstracts could be compared without opening protected contents."

Ilyan stopped smiling.

"And JEC-R/6 was not ordinary closure."

"No."

Corren's memorandum had already told them why. Someone seeking closure had possessed enough information that reuniting the ordinary paired records might have exposed a protected witness path.

So the records had been separated.

Not destroyed.

Not necessarily missing.

Separated.

Ilyan felt the shape of the question change beneath his hands.

For weeks, absence had looked like a hole.

Now it looked like two shelves in two buildings, each holding half a sentence.

Sael gathered the forms.

"Judge Vaun has authorization for the initiating-side abstract."

"Already?"

"She asked yesterday."

"Before we knew what we were asking for."

"She knew what she was asking for."

Ilyan considered this.

"I find that irritating."

"Most education is."

---

Thessa accepted the western posting before luncheon.

She signed the packet in the Warden annex, sanded the ink, and stared at her name until the clerk opposite her politely pretended not to notice.

Three months.

The western assessment circuit began in twelve days.

She would ride first to Low March, then south along the old military road, then west through two districts whose bridges had apparently entered open rebellion against structural engineering. After that came mine-road inspections, convoy timings, wayhouse capacity, flood damage, and a week attached to a Warden quartermaster whose reports had made Lady Maevra use the phrase *creative arithmetic* in a tone suggesting possible homicide.

It was exactly the kind of work Thessa wanted.

That did not make twelve days feel generous.

She left the annex with the posting tube under one arm and nearly walked into Lyrion.

He caught her by the shoulders.

"There she is."

"Who?"

"The woman abandoning us for bridges."

"The bridges need me."

"Ilyan will be devastated to learn he ranked below timber."

"Stone, mostly."

Lyrion put a hand to his heart.

"Worse."

Thessa laughed and shoved him aside.

They walked together beneath the arcade. Bloomtide had filled the little courtyard beds with white starflowers and something purple that had escaped its assigned border and was making a determined campaign toward the drain.

"You signed?" he asked.

"I signed."

His teasing vanished without ceremony.

"Good."

"That's all?"

"Do you want a speech?"

"Absolutely not."

"Then good."

He bumped her shoulder with his.

Thessa had known Lyrion long enough to hear what he did not say. He would miss her. He was pleased. He had opinions about the political usefulness of a Warden officer spending three months in western transport districts and was choosing, for once, not to offer them before she asked.

"Maevra says the assignment was being considered before the Hall mess."

"I believe her."

"Why?"

"Because if she invented a three-month infrastructure circuit merely to position you around Ilyan's investigation, she deserves to rule the continent and we should stop resisting."

Thessa snorted.

"Also," Lyrion added, "you've been complaining about western convoy planning since second year."

"It deserves complaint."

"You've brought diagrams to dinner."

"Once."

"Three times."

"One diagram. Three dinners."

"Ah."

They reached the outer steps.

Lyrion looked at the posting tube.

"Have you told him?"

"He knew I was going to accept."

"That isn't what I asked."

Thessa gave him a look.

"I'm going to the Hall now."

"He's there?"

"Probably."

"Then I withdraw the question before you hit me with your new professional paperwork."

She kissed his cheek.

Lyrion blinked.

"What was that for?"

"Not making this complicated."

"Give me time."

"I have twelve days."

"Plenty."

---

The initiating-side abstract arrived at the Hall in a grey envelope with no House seal and two signatures across the flap.

Judge Vaun opened it in front of Ilyan, Sael, and Teren.

Corren was not present.

That had been Ilyan's choice.

His father had given his statement. The next question belonged to records that could contradict him. Ilyan did not want to watch Corren's face while they opened them and then spend the afternoon wondering whether surprise had been genuine.

Teren had asked to attend because the commission had passed through his temporary-chair packet.

Vaun had allowed it.

The abstract was one sheet.

Sael read it first.

His expression did not change.

He handed it to Vaun.

Hers did.

Only slightly.

Ilyan hated both of them.

"Well?"

Vaun slid the page across the table.

The abstract recorded the initiating authority associated with the black selector.

It recorded lawful custody transfer from Teren's temporary chair authority.

It recorded Corren's subsequent custody.

It recorded JEC-R/6 as paired receiving capacity.

Then, in the field reserved for exercise confirmation, there was no confirmation mark.

There was a notation.

**PAIR STATUS REFERRED — RECEIVING ABSTRACT HELD UNDER SEPARATE COMMISSIONING AUTHORITY. DO NOT CLOSE AS UNUSED.**

Ilyan read it twice.

"That's not exercise."

"No," Vaun said.

"It's also not non-use."

"No."

Teren leaned over the page without touching it.

"Who wrote the referral?"

Sael pointed to the lower margin.

A clerk code.

Not a name.

Teren frowned.

"I don't know it."

"You aren't expected to," Vaun said. "It belongs to the Joint Office registry staff."

Ilyan looked at her.

"Can we resolve it?"

"Already requested."

He gave her a flat stare.

Vaun ignored him.

Sael pulled the blank forms back toward them.

"The important part is the instruction."

"Do not close as unused," Ilyan said.

"Yes."

"Meaning whoever wrote this knew absence of the paired mark was not enough to classify the selector as unused."

"Yes."

Teren sat down.

His fingers pressed against the edge of the table.

"I remember that phrase."

No one moved.

Teren shut his eyes.

Ilyan watched the muscles in his jaw tighten.

"Don't chase it," Sael said.

Teren opened his eyes again, irritated.

"I know."

"Then don't."

"I said I know."

He breathed out.

"It isn't a scene. It's a phrase. Someone said it in the chair room. Maybe read it. Maybe dictated it. I don't know."

Ilyan waited.

Teren stared at the abstract.

"Do not close as unused."

His thumb rubbed once over his forefinger.

"There was red wax on something. Not Valeric red. Brighter."

"Anything else?" Vaun asked.

"No."

She accepted that.

No one asked him to try harder.

Ilyan was grateful for that too.

Teren looked at him.

"I hate this."

"I know."

"No, you don't."

The words came sharper than Ilyan expected.

Teren pushed back from the table.

"You know what it is to learn something terrible. You know what it is to suspect family. You know what it is to find a record that changes what you thought happened."

His voice dropped.

"You do not know what it is to recognize your own life by the corners."

Ilyan said nothing.

Teren looked immediately ashamed of the anger and immediately angrier because of the shame.

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be."

"I shouldn't—"

"You should."

Teren stopped.

Ilyan looked at the abstract between them.

"You're right. I don't know."

For a moment neither spoke.

Then Teren nodded.

It did not repair anything.

It did not need to.

Vaun waited until he sat again before continuing.

"We have one half."

Sael shook his head.

"We have proof that one half refused to certify non-use without the other."

Vaun considered that.

"Better."

Ilyan looked at the empty confirmation field.

"So Corren can still be telling the truth."

"Yes."

"He can also be lying."

"Yes."

"And the record was designed so neither conclusion can be reached from his side alone."

Sael nodded.

Ilyan laughed once without humor.

"Someone eighteen years ago was more disciplined than I am."

"Several people," Vaun said. "Try not to take it personally."

---

The clerk code resolved before sunset.

Her name had been Pell Aris.

She had died eleven years ago at seventy-four, leaving behind a husband, two daughters, an excellent service record, and no apparent involvement in any conspiracy anyone had ever bothered to accuse her of.

Her registry notes had survived.

That was the useful part.

Pell had made the referral because the receiving-side abstract was not missing.

She had seen its transfer authorization.

The abstract itself had been removed from the Joint Office under a sealed commissioning-class privilege and sent to the accountability archive of the second commissioning authority.

The transfer entry did not identify that authority by name.

It did identify the authority family.

**Concord-protected civic office.**

Ilyan stared at the words.

Teren leaned closer.

"Not Crown."

"No," Vaun said.

"Not House."

"No."

"Not Tribunal."

Sael tapped the existing commission index.

"The Tribunal Contingency Secretariat is already the visible first commissioner. It cannot also be the sealed second under this classification."

Ilyan stood and walked to the window.

The afternoon had turned warm. Across the courtyard, someone had opened every window in the copying room. Sheets of waste paper had been weighted beneath inkstones along the sill.

"Civic office," he said.

"Protected civic office," Vaun corrected.

"How many?"

"In Bloomtide 264?"

"Yes."

Sael was already reaching for the registry catalogue.

"Depends how narrowly the commission used the term."

"Naturally."

Teren rubbed his face.

"Could the Academy qualify?"

Sael paused.

Ilyan turned.

That was the first question anyone had asked that made the room feel smaller.

Vaun answered carefully.

"The Academy itself, probably not. A chartered civic office operating within or alongside it might."

"Student protection?" Ilyan asked.

"Possibly."

"Memorial office?"

"After Silver Night, yes. Before it, not in its later form."

"Commonwealth magistracy?"

"Certain sealed functions."

"Election office?"

"Wrong authority family for witness continuity."

Sael found the old catalogue.

He ran one finger down the page.

"There were nine Concord-protected civic authority classes in 264 that could accept sealed inter-office custody. Four could receive protected persons or testimony."

"Four," Ilyan said.

"Four classes. Not four offices."

"Of course."

Sael ignored him.

"Chartered civic witness offices. Protected magistrates' registries. Certain academy-independent student guardianship offices. And compact-created emergency civic commissions."

Teren went still.

Ilyan noticed.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"Teren."

His uncle looked at the catalogue.

"The red wax."

No one spoke.

"You said bright red," Vaun said.

"Yes."

"Does one of those use it?"

Teren shook his head.

"I don't know. I remember thinking it was ugly."

Sael's eyebrows rose.

"That is impressively specific."

"I have always had standards."

The joke came out brittle but recognizable.

Ilyan felt some small part of his chest loosen.

Teren continued.

"It had grit in it. The wax. Fine pale grit. I remember rubbing a flake between my fingers and someone telling me not to."

Vaun looked at Sael.

Sael was already turning pages.

"Seal compounds aren't normally indexed here."

"Where are they?"

"Procurement."

Ilyan almost smiled.

Of course they were.

The secret second half of an eighteen-year-old witness mechanism might be narrowed not by forbidden magic, recovered memory, or a confession, but by whoever had bought sealing wax.

The world was occasionally considerate enough to be ridiculous without becoming implausible.

"Can we search without identifying the bearer?" he asked.

"Wax purchases?" Vaun said. "Yes."

"Then do that."

Sael closed the catalogue.

"Tomorrow."

Ilyan looked at the windows.

The sun was still well above the roofs.

"It's not late."

"It is for the procurement archive."

"Why?"

"Because they close early on Moonday."

"Why?"

Sael shrugged.

"Their archivist sings in a temple choir."

Ilyan stared at him.

"That is the most reasonable obstruction we've encountered."

"Try to enjoy it."

---

Thessa was waiting in his rooms when he returned.

Not hers.

His.

That was unusual enough that he stopped in the doorway.

She had removed her boots and taken possession of the chair beside the window. Her stockinged feet rested on his desk. The western posting tube lay across her lap.

"You have terrible chairs," she said.

"You have your feet on my work."

"You have no work there."

"I could."

"Then I would move them."

Ilyan shut the door.

For several seconds he simply looked at her.

Thessa lowered her feet.

"Bad?"

"Complicated."

"Ah."

She held out one hand.

He crossed the room and took it.

She pulled him between her knees and wrapped her arms around his waist.

Ilyan rested his hands on her shoulders.

He could smell soap in her hair and leather from the posting tube and the faint clean-metal scent of the Warden annex that seemed to cling to every uniform stored there.

"I signed," she said into his coat.

He looked down.

"You did?"

"This afternoon."

Something inside him tightened.

He had known she would.

He had wanted her to.

Knowledge did not make the thing less real when it arrived.

"Twelve days," she said.

"That's soon."

"Yes."

He touched the back of her neck.

Thessa tipped her head against him.

"You can hate it."

"I don't hate it."

"You can hate part of it."

He laughed softly.

"I hate the part where you aren't here."

"Good."

"Good?"

"I was going to be offended if you found three months without me professionally convenient."

He bent and kissed her.

There was nothing tentative left in the way Thessa kissed him now. She caught his coat in both hands and drew him down until his balance became her problem. His knee struck the side of the chair. She laughed against his mouth, and he kissed her again because he wanted the laugh back.

When they separated, her cheeks were flushed.

"Terrible chair," he murmured.

"Your technique lacks discipline."

"I'm grieving."

"Twelve days early."

"I prepare thoroughly."

She bit his lower lip lightly enough to make the lie obvious.

Then she looked at him.

Really looked.

"Tell me what you can."

The wording mattered.

Ilyan sat on the edge of the desk.

"Corren's side can't prove use or non-use. The record explicitly refused to call the selector unused without the paired receiving abstract."

Thessa nodded.

"And the other half?"

"It existed. It was transferred out under the second commissioner's authority. We narrowed the commissioner to a protected civic authority family."

"That's progress."

"Four possible classes."

"Still progress."

"Teren remembered sealing wax."

She blinked.

"Wax."

"Bright red. Grit in it."

"That is either extremely useful or the beginning of a very disappointing afternoon."

"Procurement archive tomorrow."

"Of course."

He smiled.

Thessa reached for his hand.

"How was Teren?"

Ilyan's smile disappeared.

"Angry."

"At you?"

"At having pieces of himself arrive without the rest. He said I don't know what that's like."

"Do you?"

"No."

"Then he was right."

"Yes."

She squeezed his fingers.

No defense of him because she loved him. No condemnation of Teren because Teren had hurt him.

Ilyan leaned forward until his forehead rested against hers.

"I am going to miss you."

"I know."

"That wasn't an argument against going."

"I know that too."

"I need you to know both."

Thessa's mouth curved.

"Ilyan."

"What?"

"I know you."

It was not entirely true.

No lover knew another person completely. No friend did. No parent, sibling, judge, examiner, or archivist did. The last weeks had cured him of any appetite for claiming otherwise.

But she knew this part.

He kissed her again.

Outside, evening bells began across the city, one tower after another taking up the sound.

Thessa's posting tube rolled off her lap and struck the floor.

Neither of them reached for it.

---

The procurement archive opened at the second bell the next morning.

Its archivist was still humming.

She was a broad-shouldered woman named Eda Pell with silver threaded through black hair and a temple-choir pin fastened crookedly at her collar. She listened to Sael's request, read Vaun's authority, and asked three questions before allowing anyone past the front desk.

"You want seal compound purchases by authority class, not office name?"

"Correct," Sael said.

"Bloomtide 264?"

"And the preceding month if the stock was purchased early."

"Compound description?"

Teren answered.

"Bright red wax. Fine pale grit."

Eda looked at him.

"Shell grit or stone?"

Teren frowned.

"I don't know."

"Did it sparkle?"

He closed his eyes briefly.

"No."

"Chalk, then. Probably."

She disappeared into the stacks.

Ilyan looked at Sael.

"Probably?"

"Do not bully the archivist who knows the wax."

"I wasn't."

"You were thinking loudly."

Eda returned with two ledgers and a box of sample seals.

The samples were not evidence from the case. They were procurement references: thumb-sized discs stamped with dates and supplier marks so clerks could identify approved compounds.

Teren examined them one by one.

Blue-black.

Green.

Brown-red.

Royal crimson.

Then he stopped.

The disc beneath his finger was almost offensively bright.

Red with a faint cloudy grain through it.

He did not touch it at first.

"That."

Eda checked the ledger.

"Chalk-red civic privacy compound. Harder to lift cleanly from parchment than ordinary wax. Shows tampering around thread holes."

Ilyan felt Sael become attentive beside him.

"Who used it?"

Eda looked at Vaun's authority again.

"In 264? Three purchasing classes. Civic witness offices. Student guardianship commissions. Compact emergency civic commissions."

Three.

Not four.

Vaun said, "Can you distinguish purchases in the week before Silver Night?"

Eda opened the second ledger.

Her finger traveled down a column.

"Civic witness offices had standing stock. No special purchase. Student guardianship bought two packets at the start of Bloomtide. Compact emergency civic commissions..."

She turned a page.

Stopped.

"One packet issued from reserve stores three days before the west-tower deaths."

The room became quiet.

Ilyan did not ask who received it.

He had learned something.

Sael did.

"Can the issue be identified without opening the commission?"

Eda nodded.

"Authority number only."

"Read it."

She did.

It was not JEC-R/6.

It was a separate civic commission number.

Vaun wrote it down.

"What does that establish?" Teren asked.

Sael answered before Ilyan could.

"That a compact emergency civic commission drew the same unusual seal compound three days before Silver Night. Nothing yet establishes that the wax you remember came from that issue."

Teren nodded.

"Good."

Ilyan looked at him.

His uncle's face was pale, but steady.

"Good?"

"I am tired of my memory being the strongest thing in the room."

Ilyan understood that.

Vaun closed the ledger.

"Then we find the commission number."

Eda raised a hand.

"You don't find that here."

Vaun looked at her.

"Where?"

"Sunder Compact civic commissions are registered through the Commonwealth seal office. If it was protected, the public ledger gives you purpose class and appointing authority, not personnel."

"That's enough."

"Usually."

Eda took back the sample box.

Ilyan watched the bright red disc disappear beneath the lid.

They had not found the other half of the lock.

They had found the office family that could have held it, a separate commission created on the same day, and a physical detail in Teren's damaged memory that independently matched a real administrative practice.

More importantly, they had learned what the initiating record had been trying to tell whoever came later.

Do not close as unused.

Not because use was proven.

Because the proof lived elsewhere.

Vaun tucked the copied commission number into her folio.

"Commonwealth seal office," she said.

Sael nodded.

Teren looked at Ilyan.

For once, neither of them tried to guess what they would find.

They walked out into Bloomtide sunlight with one less possibility and one more door.
