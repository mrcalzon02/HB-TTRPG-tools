# Beneath Dappled Oaks — World Almanac of the Sunder Moon

This document is active setting canon for **Beneath Dappled Oaks**.

It exists so stories do not take place in an empty timeless court. Every chapter should know what season it is, what the roads are doing, what food is available, what people are harvesting or preserving, what caravans are moving, what the mines need, what councils are in session, what festivals are approaching, what debts are due, which religious observances matter, and which political fights become urgent because winter, flood, harvest, taxation, or transport cannot be postponed merely because two attractive nobles have made their private lives exceptionally inconvenient.

The current year is **282 S.M. — 282 in the Epoch of the Sunder Moon**.

---

# I. The Epoch of the Sunder Moon

## The Delphimi Wars and Year Zero

The present civic order dates itself from the end of the **Delphimi Wars**, when the previous tyrannical royal regime was overthrown and the state that followed deliberately refused to choose between restoring an unrestricted monarchy and abolishing the Crown altogether.

The settlement that ended the wars became known as the **Sunder Compact**.

The year of the final settlement is **Year 0 S.M.**

The first full civil year under the new order is **1 S.M.**

The present story era is **282 S.M.**

The phrase **Sunder Moon** has two meanings.

First, it refers to the visible scar across the moon, a permanent dark fracture or rift that appeared during the final period of the Delphimi Wars. Whether the lunar wound was caused by war magic, divine intervention, an astronomical event that happened to coincide with the collapse of the old regime, or some combination of all three remains a subject of theology, astronomy, political mythmaking, and extremely impolite academic conferences.

Second, the Sunder Moon is the political metaphor of the age: one realm deliberately divided into two legitimacies that are required to govern together.

The Crown did not vanish.

The republic did not replace it.

They were chained to one another.

That compromise has prevented another absolute monarchy for nearly three centuries and has also ensured that passing a national measure of any complexity can make a person nostalgic for siege warfare.

# II. The Thirteen-Month Year

The civil year contains **13 months of 28 days each**, for a total of **364 days**.

The moon completes one visible cycle in exactly 28 days. Every civil month therefore begins at the dark moon and ends as the moon disappears again.

There are no floating lunar festivals. If a rite is held on the full moon, everyone knows that it falls on the fourteenth day of every month.

This reliability shapes law, finance, agriculture, courtship, religion, trade, and magic.

## The seven-day week

Each month contains four exact weeks.

The weekdays are:

1. **Crownday** — courts, official proclamations, appointments, royal business, formal audiences.
2. **Ledgerday** — contracts, banking, wholesale markets, guild accounts, debt payments.
3. **Rootday** — agricultural business, village councils, water allocations, field and forest matters.
4. **Forgeday** — crafts, mines, industrial guilds, apprenticeships, inspections, works contracts.
5. **Veilday** — schools, magical orders, archives, examinations, investigations, scholarly business.
6. **Moonday** — religious observance, evening markets, social gatherings, betrothal announcements, public performances.
7. **Hearthday** — customary rest day, household meals, family meetings, private worship, funerary remembrance.

Courts and councils can sit on any day in an emergency, but calling a full political session on Hearthday is an excellent method of discovering how many enemies one actually has.

## The lunar quarters

Every month follows the same lunar progression:

- **Day 1 — Dark Face:** the moon is absent or nearly absent.
- **Day 7 — First Horn:** first quarter.
- **Day 14 — Bright Sunder:** full moon. The ancient fracture is most visible across the bright face.
- **Day 21 — Last Horn:** last quarter.
- **Day 28 — Closing Dark:** the final waning night before the next month.

The moon is commonly called **Saelith**, though local names vary.

At Bright Sunder the fracture appears as a dark broken line across the luminous disc. In clear weather the line can sometimes seem faintly silver-blue at its edges. Priests insist this has profound theological significance. Astronomers insist it has profound mineralogical significance. Poets have never felt obliged to choose.

## Magical associations

The lunar cycle does not make all magic stronger or weaker in the same way.

Broad cultural assumptions are:

- **Dark Face:** concealment, ward laying, private oaths, funerary rites, illicit sympathetic work, and secret travel are traditionally associated with the dark moon.
- **First Horn:** beginnings, planting, contracts, apprenticeships, courtship declarations, and new ventures.
- **Bright Sunder:** revelation, divination, public vows, truth rituals, fertility blessings, major festivals, and large magical examinations.
- **Last Horn:** severing, debt settlement, ending contracts, dismantling wards, pruning, culling, exorcism, and legal dissolution.

Practitioners disagree violently about how much of this is physically magical and how much is accumulated ritual habit.

The distinction matters very little when half a kingdom schedules its important business around the same belief.

# III. The Thirteen Months

The official civic month names are also the names most people use in ordinary speech.

1. **Thawcall**
2. **Seedwake**
3. **Leafrise**
4. **Bloomtide**
5. **Sunsreach**
6. **Haycrest**
7. **Deepdelve**
8. **Goldgrain**
9. **Amberfall**
10. **Casktide**
11. **Frostgate**
12. **Longnight**
13. **Hearthwane**

The year begins in the dangerous transition from deep winter toward thaw rather than on the first warm day. The choice is deliberate. The founders of the Sunder Compact considered survival through the last hunger of winter a more honest beginning than pretending spring had already arrived.

# IV. The Political Order — Crown and Commonwealth

The modern government is formally known as the **Concord of Crown and Commonwealth**, though most people call it the **Sunder Concord**, the **Concord Realm**, or simply **the Realm** when context is obvious.

It is intentionally half royal order and half democratic republic.

Neither half can comfortably govern alone.

## The Crown

The Crown retains substantial real authority.

The monarch and royal institutions control or strongly influence:

- foreign diplomacy;
- military command;
- royal estates;
- appointments to certain national offices;
- emergency response;
- national defense;
- recognition of noble succession;
- royal charters;
- high ceremonial authority;
- certain magical and judicial appointments;
- temporary emergency writs.

The Crown cannot ordinarily impose permanent national taxation, rewrite civil law, or create broad national obligations without council participation.

## Local magistrates

Villages, wards, boroughs, market towns, mining districts, and chartered cities elect or otherwise locally select **magistrates** under regional law.

The exact franchise varies by district.

Some places use broad household voting.

Some weight guild membership or property.

Some recognize village assemblies.

Some allow chartered institutions to appoint a limited number of magistrates.

This variation is a constant political argument and was deliberately preserved by the Sunder Compact because forcing every region into a single electoral model was one of the matters everyone had recently spent a war killing each other about.

Local magistrates govern matters such as:

- roads and bridges;
- local tolls;
- water rights;
- market regulation;
- sanitation;
- local policing;
- field boundaries;
- seasonal grazing;
- licensing;
- building and fire codes;
- local relief stores;
- winter fuel reserves;
- market calendars;
- minor courts;
- petitions to higher councils.

## Houses and local influence

The Great Houses and their cadet branches do not automatically control local magistrates.

They do, however, own land, employ people, finance roads, sponsor guilds, support temples, extend credit, marry into local families, provide legal advocates, and maintain patronage networks.

As a result, local political affairs often determine **which House can actually place a measure in front of a magistrates' council with enough support for it to survive**.

A House that loses a local voting bloc may retain enormous national prestige while discovering that it cannot get a bridge widened in a district where everyone hates its steward.

That sort of humiliation is one of the setting's healthier constitutional traditions.

## Regional councils

Local measures with regional consequences pass upward to **Regional Councils**.

Regional councils handle:

- inter-district roads;
- major bridges;
- river works;
- regional market rules;
- mine access routes;
- forest exploitation;
- water systems;
- militia coordination;
- regional taxation;
- large public works;
- certification of petitions to the National Council.

A proposal that cannot survive at the regional level almost never reaches national debate.

## The National Council

The **National Council** is divided into two equal constitutional halves.

### The Crown Bench

The Crown Bench represents royal legitimacy.

Its membership includes royal appointees, recognized high offices, chartered noble representation, and other Crown-recognized seats defined by the Compact and later law.

### The Civic Bench

The Civic Bench represents the commonwealth.

Its members are sent through the regional political structure and are accountable, by varying regional mechanisms, to local or regional constituencies.

### Why national legislation is so difficult

Most permanent national measures must clear several gates:

1. local or regional origin, where appropriate;
2. certification by a Regional Council;
3. a valid national sponsor;
4. committee review;
5. a majority of the Civic Bench;
6. a majority of the Crown Bench;
7. royal assent or the appropriate constitutional override mechanism.

In practice, controversial bills are often amended until their original sponsors no longer recognize them.

National measures involving taxation, succession, military obligation, magical licensing, road toll standards, interregional water rights, or House privilege are especially difficult.

The result is a state that changes slowly at the national level but can change rapidly and dramatically at the local level.

This makes **voting blocs, magistrate alliances, House patronage networks, guild coalitions, temple influence, and regional grudges extremely important**.

## Temporary Crown writs

The Crown may issue temporary emergency writs for matters such as flood, invasion, plague, catastrophic harvest failure, or major magical disaster.

Such writs expire unless renewed through the constitutional system.

Every monarch believes this period is too short.

Every Civic Bench believes it is suspiciously generous.

Both beliefs are traditional.

# V. The Annual Political Cycle

Politics follows the agricultural and transport year because government cannot vote grain into a granary after the roads have frozen.

The broad annual rhythm is:

- **Thawcall:** road and bridge emergency petitions; flood works; winter relief accounts.
- **Seedwake:** seed credit, planting rights, water allocations, tenant disputes.
- **Leafrise:** market openings, road tolls, first-harvest transport, local works.
- **Bloomtide:** charters, academies, appointments, courtship season, public patronage.
- **Sunsreach:** military musters, caravan licenses, regional infrastructure.
- **Haycrest:** mine provisioning, labor contracts, water and grazing disputes.
- **Deepdelve:** industrial peak; mine regulation; national summer session.
- **Goldgrain:** grain pricing, storage, tariffs, harvest labor.
- **Amberfall:** local political selection, regional coalition building, orchard and timber disputes.
- **Casktide:** tax assessments, winter contracts, granary audits.
- **Frostgate:** last road appropriations, winter closures, final national docket fights.
- **Longnight:** judicial appeals, private lobbying, House negotiations, winter courts.
- **Hearthwane:** year-end accounts, oath renewals, appointments, agenda construction for the next year.

# VI. The Great Gods

The dominant public faith recognizes a family of high deities often called the **Concordant Gods**.

Different temples disagree about genealogy, hierarchy, and whether the gods literally constitute a family. Ordinary worshippers are usually more concerned with whether the local priest will bless a bridge before it collapses.

## Aurel, the Crown of Dawn

Domains: legitimate rule, public oath, duty, lawful authority, dawn, protection of the civic peace.

Aurel is invoked at coronations, magistrate oaths, military commissions, treaties, and formal declarations.

The post-Delphimi order deliberately distinguishes **legitimate authority** from mere possession of power. This is one reason the old tyranny remains such a theological problem: the previous king possessed the Crown but, according to Concord orthodoxy, lost Aurel's sanction through tyranny.

Symbols: a rising sun behind an open circlet.

Organizations with strong ties: royal offices, Moon-Crown service, judges, civic oath-keepers.

Festival food: **sunbread**, a saffron or golden-spice round loaf broken publicly and distributed from the center outward.

## Maereth, the Seed-Mother

Domains: grain, gardens, fertility, pregnancy, childbirth, household plenty, mercy, recovery after famine.

Maereth is not a passive domestic goddess. Agricultural law, seed preservation, inheritance, food security, and reproductive politics all fall under her temples.

Her priesthood has a famously hostile relationship with practitioners of fertility curses.

Symbols: an opened seed pod held in two hands.

Organizations with strong ties: Green Covenant, midwives, seed guilds, orchard keepers, granary trusts.

Festival food: **thirteen-seed cakes**, dense honeyed cakes containing preserved seeds from thirteen cultivated plants.

## Kharun, the Hammer Below

Domains: mines, stone, metal, furnaces, craft labor, endurance, dangerous work performed competently.

Kharun is worshipped heavily in mining and industrial districts.

He is not a god of wealth. He is a god of **work that kills the careless**.

Symbols: a hammer striking a stone marked by a single bright vein.

Organizations with strong ties: miners, smiths, engineers, masons, Forgeday guilds, mine rescuers.

Festival food: **deep-pot**, a heavy miner's stew of root vegetables, beans, smoked meat or mushrooms, dark beer, and whatever can survive being carried underground.

## Selvara of the Open Road

Domains: roads, bridges, caravans, hospitality, safe passage, trade, messengers, fair measure.

Selvara's temples often maintain wayhouses and bridge shrines.

Her priests are among the loudest advocates for road maintenance because a theological commitment to travel becomes awkward when the west bridge has fallen into the river.

Symbols: three road lines meeting beneath a hanging lantern.

Organizations with strong ties: River Ledger, caravan companies, postal riders, merchants, bridge guilds.

Festival food: **road pies**, sealed portable pastries with region-specific fillings, traditionally exchanged between travelers.

## Eiravel, Keeper of the Sundered Moon

Domains: moon, memory, mourning, dreams, hidden truth, grief, remembrance of the dead.

After the Delphimi Wars, Eiravel became one of the most politically important gods.

Her temples maintain memorial rolls and often serve as neutral repositories for testimony, wills, and death records.

Symbols: a silver disc crossed by a dark line.

Organizations with strong ties: memorial societies, archivists, some Truth Beyond the Veil traditions, funerary orders, historians.

Festival food: **memory rice** or **memory grain**, pale grain cooked with dark dried fruit laid across the dish in a line representing the lunar scar.

## Faelor, the Red-Horned Warden

Domains: wilderness, hunt, borderlands, predators, courage, the right to survive beyond cultivated land.

Faelor's cult is strongest in uplands, forest margins, and frontier districts.

He represents the boundary between civilization and what exists whether civilization approves of it or not.

Symbols: a red antler above a black trail.

Organizations with strong ties: Red Hart, wardens, foresters, hunters, frontier militias.

Festival food: **juniper roast**, traditionally venison where available, though mushroom, goat, or mutton versions are common.

## Lysara of the Silver Voice

Domains: diplomacy, love, beauty, persuasion, reconciliation, poetry, music, courtship, negotiated peace.

Lysara is simultaneously beloved by romantics and regarded with professional suspicion by anyone who has ever attended a treaty banquet.

Her doctrine distinguishes persuasion from coercion, an ethical distinction that noble families have spent centuries finding creative ways to blur.

Symbols: a silver branch wrapped in a ribbon.

Organizations with strong ties: Silver Bough, musicians, ambassadors, formal matchmakers, heralds.

Festival food: **silver pears in wine**, poached fruit served whole so that cutting it becomes part of the table ritual.

# VII. The Dark Gods

The Dark Gods are acknowledged as hostile divine powers, corrupted principles, ancient entities, or theological personifications depending on doctrine.

Open cults to them are illegal in most jurisdictions.

Secret worship still exists.

## Korvath, the Chain-King

Domains: tyranny, domination, obedience through fear, ownership of persons, law as a weapon.

Korvath is the theological opposite of Aurel.

The old Delphimi tyranny is frequently accused in sermons of having "worn Korvath's crown" even when no evidence exists that the old royal household literally worshipped him.

Symbols: a closed iron crown linked to chains.

## Vesha of the Empty Cradle

Domains: spiteful sterility, corrupted fertility, lineage sabotage, jealousy of heirs, desire turned poisonous.

Vesha is invoked in accusations involving infertility fetishes, poisoned marriage beds, pregnancy sabotage, and malicious reproductive magic.

Not every fertility curse is religious.

That has never prevented frightened relatives from assuming it is.

Symbols: a black cradle bound shut with red thread.

## Morghan, the Hollow Feast

Domains: famine, hoarding during hunger, rot, waste, profiteering from starvation.

Morghan is particularly feared in granary culture.

Hoarding grain during a declared famine can carry both civic and religious condemnation.

Symbols: an empty bowl with a golden rim.

## Nhal, the Whispered Knife

Domains: betrayal, secret murder, false witness, treachery inside trusted relationships.

Nhal is feared not because enemies kill, but because friends open the door.

Symbols: a knife hidden behind an open hand.

Dark sympathetic magic involving household access is often popularly blamed on Nhal even when the curse has no religious component.

## The Ash Magistrate

Domains: corrupt judgment, punishment without justice, procedure used to conceal guilt, legal cruelty.

The Ash Magistrate is usually depicted faceless beneath judicial robes.

Some theologians insist the Ash Magistrate was once a mortal official during the Delphimi Wars.

Others insist that story was invented by magistrates who enjoy believing corruption began with someone else.

Symbols: scales filled with ash.

## Gharad Red-Hand

Domains: slaughter, vengeance without limit, feud, ecstatic violence, war that continues after its purpose is gone.

Soldiers distinguish Gharad sharply from lawful war under Aurel or defensive violence under Faelor.

Symbols: a red handprint over a broken spear.

# VIII. The Little Court — Regional and Minor Deities

Alongside the High Gods exists an enormous body of local divinities, saints, river spirits, household patrons, deified ancestors, old forest beings, mine guardians, and civic cults collectively called **the Little Court**.

The Sunder Compact generally tolerates local worship provided the cult does not demand criminal practice, human sacrifice, sedition, or allegiance to one of the recognized Dark Gods.

Theological purists hate the phrase "provided the cult does not demand criminal practice" because it makes religion sound like licensing a tavern.

Local magistrates consider that a feature.

Important examples include:

### Nera Bridgewife

Patron of bridges, ferries, flood crossings, and people waiting for travelers to come home.

Common offering: the heel of the first loaf carried safely across a repaired bridge.

### Tern's Little Lamp

A mining spirit said to appear as a steady light shortly before a dangerous collapse or pocket of bad air.

Miners leave a spoonful of oil at shaft shrines.

Kharun's formal priesthood tolerates this practice while insisting Tern is merely Kharun's servant.

Miners tolerate the priesthood's opinion.

### Grandmother Apple-Bough

Orchard guardian of the warmer western and southern districts.

The oldest tree in an orchard is sometimes left unharvested for her birds.

### The Reed King

River-marsh deity of eels, reeds, floodplain boundaries, lost boats, and people who make their living where maps become wet.

### The Three Cask Sisters

Minor goddesses of brewing, fermentation, preservation, and the wisdom not to open a sealed crock too early.

Extremely popular with brewers.

Extremely unpopular with sober theologians.

### Old White Stag

A northern and upland guardian associated with snow routes, lost hunters, and boundaries that should not be crossed casually.

### Saint Veyren of the Empty Chair

A postwar memorial saint or deified civic hero, depending region, associated with leaving a place at table for those who did not return from war.

His observance has become especially potent in the aftermath of the Academy's recent dead.

### Lady of the First Cart

A road-and-market folk deity invoked when the first spring wagon successfully reaches a town after winter.

Merchants decorate the first cart wheel with green ribbon.

### The Cellar Child

A household spirit of preserved food, cool storage, hidden mice, and jars that inexplicably survive everyone forgetting about them.

Children leave crumbs.

Adults insist they do not.

# X. The Annual Economic Logic

The Realm's economy changes character with the seasons.

## Winter is not inactivity

During Longnight and Hearthwane:

- mines continue selectively where underground temperature and stored food allow;
- smelters consume ore accumulated in the warmer months;
- smithies work through stockpiled metal;
- logging may intensify where frozen ground makes hauling easier;
- weaving, tool repair, wagon building, leatherwork, bookkeeping, and household manufacture rise;
- political negotiation moves indoors;
- seed stores are inspected and guarded;
- preserved food becomes strategic capital.

## Spring is a road crisis before it is a planting season

When thaw begins, roads are often worse than during winter.

Frozen tracks become mud.

Culverts clog.

Bridges shift.

Riverbanks fail.

A district may possess food and still be unable to sell it.

Therefore the first major economic struggle of the year is **transport restoration**.

Large families, Houses, guilds, and towns begin road work before the main spring produce becomes available.

The goal is to have commercial routes ready for the **first spring harvest**, especially overwintered greens, early roots, spring herbs, eggs, milk products, river fish, greenhouse or ward-grown produce, and early grain in warmer districts.

A House that fails to repair its road can watch a neighboring House's produce reach market first and spend the rest of the season explaining why its tenants are poor.

## Summer feeds the mines

Fresh food changes mining output.

During the cold months, mine settlements depend heavily on preserved grains, salted foods, roots, dried legumes, hard cheese, and stored beer.

When spring and summer agriculture begins producing fresh vegetables, fruit, dairy, meat, and reliable wagon traffic, mine camps can support:

- larger labor crews;
- longer underground rotations;
- more pack animals;
- more repair specialists;
- more timbering crews;
- more ore sorting;
- more distant prospecting;
- more frequent caravan departures.

As a result, **mineral output rises sharply from Haycrest through Goldgrain**.

Fresh food is therefore an industrial input.

A political dispute over a bridge carrying lettuce, onions, cheese, and beer to a mining valley can become a dispute over national iron output three months later.

## Autumn is the compression point

During Goldgrain, Amberfall, and Casktide, the Realm must simultaneously move:

- grain;
- fruit;
- wine;
- preserved foods;
- hay;
- livestock;
- charcoal;
- timber;
- ore;
- metal;
- taxes;
- winter fuel;
- military stores.

Every road is busy.

Every warehouse is insufficient.

Every toll becomes controversial.

Every caravan master suddenly has principles.

## Winter preparation

Before Frostgate closes unreliable roads, households and institutions attempt to secure:

- seed grain that must not be eaten;
- food grain that may be eaten;
- fodder;
- firewood or coal;
- lamp oil;
- salt;
- preserved meat;
- dried vegetables;
- winter medicine;
- textiles;
- shoe leather;
- spare wagon and plow parts;
- charcoal;
- mine timbers;
- emergency bridge lumber;
- brewing grain;
- winter animal feed.

The distinction between **seed grain** and **food grain** is politically sacred.

Eating the seed reserve may save a family this winter and starve it next autumn.

This single fact sits beneath a large body of law, charity, crime, debt, and religious obligation.

# XI. The Year Month by Month

# 1. THAWCALL

Season: late winter turning toward thaw.

Moon title at full: **The Water-Sunder**.

Primary concerns:
flooding, culverts, ice breakup, damaged roofs, winter hunger, road assessment, bridge inspection, seed reserve survival.

The first weeks of Thawcall are often miserable rather than hopeful.

Snow softens into mud.

Rivers rise beneath ice.

Cellars flood.

Stored vegetables rot just when people most need them.

Large Houses dispatch surveyors, engineers, tenant stewards, and road gangs to determine which routes can be made usable before first market.

### Week One — The Opening of Ditches

Drainage channels, culverts, roadside cuts, and mill races are cleared.

Village councils can conscript limited communal labor under old road obligations.

Conflict:
landowners argue over whose fields should accept runoff.

Festival:
**Ditchfire Supper** — road crews burn winter brush and share hot onion broth, black bread, and the last cured meat.

### Week Two — Bridge Oaths

On First Horn, bridge guilds and local magistrates formally inspect major crossings.

Nera Bridgewife receives bread offerings.

Regional dishes:
river districts serve eel pie;
uplands serve smoked cheese and barley cakes;
Crownheart bridges distribute sunbread.

Political issue:
who pays for repairs — local treasury, House estate, toll company, or regional council?

### Week Three — Bright Sunder Flood Vigil

The full moon coincides with high concern over meltwater.

Temples of Eiravel hold vigils for people lost during winter.

Floodplain settlements perform Reed King rites.

This is a favored period for public relief demonstrations by ambitious Houses.

A cart of donated grain can be charity, campaign theater, or both.

### Week Four — First Road Petitions

Formal road petitions begin reaching regional councils.

House rivalries intensify over which routes receive stone, timber, and labor first.

Story utility:
a damaged bridge can delay a wedding party, isolate a manor where a curse is operating, trap investigators, or determine which House gets early produce to market.

---

# 2. SEEDWAKE

Season: early spring.

Moon title at full: **The Green Sunder**.

Primary concerns:
seed grain, plowing, tenant credit, irrigation, lambing, orchard pruning, labor contracts.

### Week One — Seed Vault Opening

Maereth's temples witness the opening of communal seed stores.

Seed grain is counted in public in many districts.

Tampering with a certified seed store is both a serious crime and a religious offense.

Festival food:
thirteen-seed cakes baked from symbolic quantities, never enough to endanger planting stock.

### Week Two — The Plow Compact

Tenant contracts, field leases, and shared animal agreements are renegotiated.

Disputes:
who owns improvements;
who supplies draft animals;
whether flood damage changes rent;
whether a dead tenant's family inherits planting rights.

Young nobles assigned estate responsibilities often receive their first truly educational exposure to how quickly a romantic morning can be ruined by forty-two farmers arguing about drainage.

### Week Three — Green Sunder Blessing

The full moon sees field blessings, betrothal announcements, and new apprenticeships.

Lysaran and Maerethan rites overlap heavily.

Spring courtship traditions often begin here.

Dishes:
fresh curd cheese, first herbs, egg pies, green onion cakes.

### Week Four — Water Courts

Rootday courts hear irrigation and stream diversion cases.

House conflict:
upstream estates can ruin downstream planting.

Regional councils frequently intervene.

---

# 3. LEAFRISE

Season: established spring.

Moon title at full: **The Cart-Moon**.

Primary concerns:
first produce, reopening markets, spring grain, dairy abundance, road tolls, animal movement.

Leafrise is when the success or failure of Thawcall road work becomes visible.

### Week One — First Greens Market

The first significant fresh produce reaches urban markets.

Common goods:
greens, herbs, early peas, spring onions, radishes, mushrooms, eggs, soft cheeses, river fish.

Prices are watched carefully as indicators of winter recovery.

### Week Two — The First Cart

The first fully loaded commercial wagon to arrive from each major agricultural district is decorated with green ribbons.

Shrines of the Lady of the First Cart become covered in wheel ribbons.

Festival food:
road pies made with fresh greens and cheese.

Political meaning:
the House whose road delivers first gains prestige.

### Week Three — Bright Sunder Market Feast

Major cities hold extended night markets.

Merchants renegotiate caravan agreements for summer.

River Ledger officials publish early transport estimates.

Courtship:
public promenades become common.

### Week Four — Toll Quarrels

As traffic rises, every toll that seemed tolerable in winter becomes an outrage.

Magistrates debate temporary toll reductions.

Bridge owners insist bridges do not repair themselves through moral virtue.

---

# 4. BLOOMTIDE

Season: late spring.

Moon title at full: **The Flowered Sunder**.

Primary concerns:
orchards, courtship, academy graduation, hiring, public ceremonies, charters, early travel.

Bloomtide is one of the Realm's major social seasons.

This is the customary period for academies, magical colleges, military schools, and elite institutions to hold graduations or inductions, although exact dates vary.

For Beneath Dappled Oaks, **graduation-season scenes naturally belong in or near Bloomtide unless already committed prose establishes otherwise**.

### Week One — Garland Days

Towns decorate gates, schools, bridges, and public halls with flowers.

Students and apprentices preparing to graduate wear order-specific ribbons.

Dishes:
flower honey cakes, herb omelets, fresh trout, early strawberries or regional equivalents.

### Week Two — House Presentation

Young adults entering formal service are presented at House dinners, civic receptions, guild halls, or order ceremonies.

This is a prime period for:
marriage discussions;
courtship strategy;
recruitment;
public embarrassment;
quiet instructions from older relatives.

### Week Three — Flowered Sunder

Lysara's largest spring festival.

Music, poetry, dancing, public declarations of affection, and formal betrothals dominate.

The same week is notorious for political seductions disguised as festival romance.

### Week Four — Charter Week

Guild licenses, school appointments, new commercial charters, and some order admissions are finalized before the heavy summer work season.

---

# 5. SUNSREACH

Season: early summer.

Moon title at full: **The Spear-Moon**.

Primary concerns:
military musters, wool, long-distance caravans, construction, river traffic, summer hiring.

### Week One — Shearing and Cloth Contracts

Western districts shear sheep.

Textile Houses negotiate raw wool purchase.

Festival food:
mutton pies, fresh cheeses, mint sauces.

### Week Two — Road Muster

Regional authorities inspect main roads, wayhouses, and patrols before the largest caravan movements.

Selvara's clergy bless caravan bells.

### Week Three — Spear-Moon Muster

Moon-Crown units, local militias, and some House guards hold summer inspections.

This is formally about readiness.

It is also an opportunity for everyone to inspect everyone else's uniforms, horses, officers, and political confidence.

### Week Four — Long Road Opening

High passes and distant roads generally become fully reliable.

Luxury trade accelerates.

Diplomatic travel increases.

---

# 6. HAYCREST

Season: high summer.

Moon title at full: **The Meadow Sunder**.

Primary concerns:
hay, mine provisioning, dairy, grazing, labor, road dust, water rights.

### Week One — Hay Cutting

The hay crop begins.

Weather becomes a constant obsession.

A week of rain can alter winter livestock survival months in advance.

### Week Two — Miners' Fresh Table

Fresh agricultural supply reaches mine settlements in quantity.

Kharun temples sponsor communal meals.

Dishes:
deep-pot enriched with fresh greens;
soft cheese;
summer onions;
fresh bread;
berries.

Industrial effect:
mine staffing increases.

### Week Three — Meadow Sunder

Rural dances and workers' festivals are common.

This is one of the few major festivals shared enthusiastically by field laborers and miners because both groups are temporarily eating better.

### Week Four — Water and Grazing Courts

Dry weather produces arguments over wells, streams, pasture, and mill water.

House stewards become deeply unpopular.

---

# 7. DEEPDELVE

Season: peak summer.

Moon title at full: **The Hammer Sunder**.

Primary concerns:
maximum mining output, smelting supply, industrial accidents, mine labor, national summer politics.

Deepdelve is the industrial high point of the year.

Mines operate extended schedules while roads, food supply, and weather are favorable.

### Week One — Shaft Reckoning

Mines publish production targets and safety inspections.

Kharun's priests bless new workings.

Tern's Little Lamp receives unofficial offerings.

### Week Two — Ore Road Priority

Regional councils designate priority roads for ore and mine supply traffic.

Agricultural carriers complain that ore wagons destroy road surfaces.

Mine syndicates reply that without iron there would be no wagon hardware.

The argument is older than several dynasties.

### Week Three — Hammer Sunder

Major industrial festival.

Smithing demonstrations, mine rescue competitions, guild honors, and public feasts.

Dishes:
deep-pot;
peppered roots;
black bread;
dark beer;
fruit-and-nut bars.

### Week Four — National Summer Session

The National Council traditionally conducts one of its most important working periods here because delegates can actually travel.

Common fights:
mine taxes;
road standards;
 military procurement;
interregional tariffs;
industrial charters;
magical safety regulation.

National legislation remains difficult even when everyone agrees there is a problem.

---

# 8. GOLDGRAIN

Season: late summer.

Moon title at full: **The Golden Sunder**.

Primary concerns:
grain harvest, peak wagon demand, ore transport, storage capacity, harvest wages.

### Week One — First Grain

Early grain is cut in warmer regions.

Maereth's temples bless the first sheaves.

No first sheaf may traditionally be sold; it is divided among the household, temple, laborers, and seed store.

### Week Two — Wagon War

This is not usually a literal war.

It can feel like one.

Farmers need wagons for grain.

Mines need wagons for ore.

Merchants need wagons for everything.

Armies want wagons because armies always want wagons.

Prices for draft animals, wheels, axles, and drivers rise sharply.

### Week Three — Golden Sunder

Large harvest dances begin.

Festival dish:
new-grain bread with butter and honey.

River Cantons serve fish with fresh grain cakes.

### Week Four — Granary Inspection

Public and private granaries are inspected.

Arguments begin over storage fees and expected winter prices.

---

# 9. AMBERFALL

Season: early autumn.

Moon title at full: **The Amber Sunder**.

Primary concerns:
main harvest, orchards, vineyards, timber, local political selection, regional coalitions.

### Week One — Orchard Fall

Fruit harvest accelerates.

Grandmother Apple-Bough receives the traditional unpicked crown fruit in many orchards.

Dishes:
baked apples;
pear tarts;
nut breads;
fresh cider.

### Week Two — Local Ballot and Magistrate Season

Many districts choose, renew, confirm, or reorganize magistrates during Amberfall after the worst agricultural labor has eased but before winter.

Not all districts use direct ballots, but the month is broadly understood as the **local political season**.

House agents, guild coalitions, village blocs, religious institutions, and reform groups compete intensely.

This matters because next year's road, market, water, and tax measures depend on who controls local councils.

### Week Three — Amber Sunder

Regional fairs combine harvest celebration with political meetings.

Candidates who cannot eat politely in public have ended careers here.

### Week Four — Regional Coalition Week

Newly selected local officials begin negotiating regional alignments.

Great Houses count friendly magistrates with the obsessive attention other people reserve for money.

---

# 10. CASKTIDE

Season: middle autumn.

Moon title at full: **The Casked Moon**.

Primary concerns:
wine, beer, cider, slaughter, preservation, taxation, winter stores, debt.

### Week One — Pressing

Fruit is pressed.

Wine and cider production begins.

The Three Cask Sisters receive their annual explosion of popular devotion.

### Week Two — Slaughter and Salt

Livestock that cannot be economically overwintered are slaughtered.

Salt, smokehouses, barrels, and butcher labor become critical.

Dishes:
fresh sausage;
blood puddings in some regions;
roast root vegetables;
young cider.

### Week Three — Casked Moon Feast

A major preservation festival.

Households display preserved foods and trade recipes.

This seemingly domestic event has enormous economic importance.

The household that preserves badly in Casktide may starve in Longnight.

### Week Four — Winter Store Audit

Magistrates, estate stewards, military quartermasters, temples, mines, and guilds audit food and fuel.

Political conflict:
whether private granaries must disclose stock;
whether relief stores are sufficient;
whether export restrictions should begin.

---

# 11. FROSTGATE

Season: late autumn into first winter.

Moon title at full: **The White Sunder**.

Primary concerns:
first hard frost, last caravans, road closure, winter garrisons, final legislative deadlines.

### Week One — Frost Marks

Road keepers begin posting winter hazard markers.

Highland roads may close.

Caravan insurance and guard prices rise.

### Week Two — Last Caravan

Major commercial houses dispatch the last guaranteed long-distance caravans before winter.

Selvara's temples ring road bells at dawn.

Families wait anxiously for travelers.

### Week Three — White Sunder

First major winter religious observances.

Old White Stag shrines become important in northern districts.

Dishes:
smoked fish;
rye cakes;
hot broth;
mulled wine or cider.

### Week Four — Closing Docket

The National Council faces the traditional panic of deciding which unresolved matters must pass before winter travel makes attendance difficult.

Many bills die here.

Many others are passed in forms that cause everyone to spend Longnight discovering what they accidentally agreed to.

---

# 12. LONGNIGHT

Season: deep winter.

Moon title at full: **The Black-Silver Sunder**.

Primary concerns:
cold, fuel, stored food, indoor work, winter courts, marriage negotiation, private politics.

Longnight is physically restrictive and politically intimate.

People who cannot travel must talk to the people already near them.

This is dangerous.

### Week One — Hearth Accounting

Households adjust food rationing according to actual winter stock.

Mines reduce or concentrate operations depending local reserves.

### Week Two — Winter Courts

Appeals, inheritance matters, contract disputes, magical investigations, and House cases occupy indoor courts.

Dark Arts accusations often surface in winter because households spend more time within the limited range of hidden sympathetic fetishes.

### Week Three — Black-Silver Sunder

Eiravel's great winter memorial.

Families set an empty place for the dead.

Saint Veyren of the Empty Chair is widely honored.

For the Last Seven, this observance should carry particular emotional weight after the deaths of their classmates.

Festival dish:
memory grain with dark fruit;
plain broth;
one reserved cup left untouched.

### Week Four — The Quiet Ball Season

Elite households hold winter salons, masked balls, music evenings, and private dinners.

Marriage negotiation intensifies.

So does espionage.

So does the unfortunate tendency of young adults to make permanent decisions because someone looked extraordinary by candlelight.

---

# 13. HEARTHWANE

Season: final winter month and year end.

Moon title at full: **The Thirteenth Sunder**.

Primary concerns:
year-end accounts, debt settlement, oath renewal, seed protection, appointments, national agenda construction, reconciliation.

Hearthwane is both exhausted and dangerous.

Everyone knows exactly how much food remains.

Everyone knows which debts remain unpaid.

Everyone knows which political promises must be renewed before the year turns.

### Week One — Closing Ledgers

River Ledger houses, guilds, estates, temples, and public treasuries close annual accounts.

Debts are renegotiated.

Defaults become visible.

### Week Two — Oath Renewal

Officials, guards, some guild officers, and many magical orders renew annual oaths.

This is a prime time for detecting altered language, hidden obligations, or institutional drift.

### Week Three — Thirteenth Sunder

The year's most politically symbolic full moon.

The Moon appears for the thirteenth and final full cycle.

Public rites commemorate the Sunder Compact and the end of the Delphimi Wars.

Aurel and Eiravel share many ceremonies.

The Crown appears publicly beside representatives of the Civic Bench.

Everyone praises constitutional unity.

Everyone counts who stood closest to whom.

Festival meal:
**Thirteen Tables**, a meal in which each course or shared dish represents one month of the year.

Regional variations are enormous.

### Week Four — The Year Moot

Local councils, Regional Councils, Houses, guilds, orders, and national factions finalize priorities for the coming year.

Road projects are ranked.

Marriage negotiations are confirmed.

Appointments are announced.

Petitions are prepared.

Seed stores are sealed.

The National Council's likely docket becomes a matter of public speculation.

On Closing Dark, lamps are extinguished briefly before midnight and relit from household hearths.

The new year begins with Thawcall.

# XIII. Seasonal Conflicts and Disputes

The setting should repeatedly use seasonal conflict.

## Road priority

Who receives labor and stone first after thaw?

A mine road?

A grain road?

A royal highway?

A road serving a politically useful estate?

## Water rights

Spring flooding creates too much water.

Summer creates too little.

Both conditions create lawsuits.

## Seed debt

Poor households often borrow seed.

Repayment may be demanded in grain, labor, money, or future planting rights.

Predatory seed lending is a recurring source of political anger.

## Mine food contracts

Fresh food contracts can determine whether a mine can increase output.

Control of vegetable, cheese, beer, and meat supply can therefore become industrial leverage.

## Wagon competition

At harvest peak, wagon capacity becomes strategic.

A House controlling a wagon syndicate can influence both food and ore markets.

## Winter store disclosure

Merchants resist revealing private stock.

Magistrates fear hidden hoarding.

Temples demand relief reserves.

Nobles worry about unrest.

## Road tolls

Tolls fund roads.

Tolls also make everyone hate the people who fund roads.

## Forest rights

Timber is needed for:
mine supports;
bridges;
fuel;
charcoal;
winter construction.

Overcutting becomes a regional political fight.

## Winter fuel

Cities compete with smelters for wood, charcoal, and coal.

A severe winter can force governments to choose between warm households and continued industrial output.

## Harvest labor

Large estates need temporary labor.

Mines do not like losing workers to harvest.

Wages rise.

Contracts become contentious.

## Marriage season logistics

Political marriages are affected by roads and seasons.

A wedding that must occur before Frostgate has a hard deadline.

A delayed caravan can delay a dowry.

A closed pass can strand a bride or groom with people they would rather not spend Longnight beside.

# XIV. Religious and Organizational Food Culture

Food in Beneath Dappled Oaks should not merely decorate scenes.

It identifies institutions, regions, status, season, and political intent.

## Green Covenant

Typical ceremonial foods:
fresh herbs, thirteen-seed cake, orchard fruit, spring cheese, vegetable tarts.

Meaning:
renewal, stewardship, fertility, continuity.

## Moon-Crown / Royal Martial Fellowship

Typical ceremonial foods:
sunbread, roast meats, preserved fruit, strong wine, traveling loaves during musters.

Meaning:
service, discipline, royal legitimacy, readiness.

## Western Wardens

Typical foods:
smoked mutton, barley, hard cheese, berry sauces.

Meaning:
frontier practicality and self-sufficiency.

## Silver Bough

Typical foods:
silver pears, spiced wine, delicate pastries, carefully paired regional dishes during diplomacy.

Meaning:
hospitality as political language.

A badly chosen meal can insult a delegation.

A perfectly chosen childhood dish can soften one.

## River Ledger

Typical foods:
eel pie, river fish, braided bread, pickles, portable trade foods.

Meaning:
movement, contract, exchange, cosmopolitan river culture.

## Red Hart

Typical foods:
juniper roast, smoked mushrooms, chestnut cakes, berry cordials.

Meaning:
wild abundance, boundary life, hunting, forest memory.

## Truth Beyond the Veil

No single universal menu should define the order.

However, austere meals, divided plates, paired light-and-dark foods, or dishes involving covered vessels are appropriate ritual motifs.

Meaning:
truth concealed, truth revealed, disciplined observation.

# XV. Story Use Rules

The annual calendar should influence prose.

Before writing a chapter, determine:

1. current year;
2. month;
3. day or at least week;
4. moon phase;
5. weather and road conditions;
6. food currently fresh versus preserved;
7. major labor underway;
8. current political season;
9. upcoming festival or deadline;
10. whether a character's plan is constrained by travel, harvest, council session, mine production, seed, inheritance, marriage timing, or winter.

A chapter set in Deepdelve should not behave like Longnight.

A character should not casually serve fresh summer berries in Hearthwane unless there is a magical greenhouse, preserved supply, exceptional import, or deliberate display of wealth.

A remote estate should not summon twenty guests during Frostgate without discussing whether the roads remain open.

A mine dispute in Haycrest or Deepdelve should consider food and wagon supply.

A political House should care deeply about Amberfall magistrate selection if it intends to move local measures the following year.

A national reform proposed in Longnight should face the practical problem that many delegates cannot travel easily.

A marriage intended to secure an heir may become politically urgent before winter separates the relevant households.

A fertility curse becomes particularly explosive when discovered during marriage or inheritance negotiations.

---

# XVI. Date Style

Formal documents use:

**14 Deepdelve, 282 S.M.**

Short form:

**14 Deepdelve 282**

Very formal royal or legal writing may use:

**On the Fourteenth Day of Deepdelve in the Two Hundred Eighty-Second Year of the Sunder Moon.**

Characters commonly say:

"the third Crownday of Goldgrain,"
"Bright Sunder in Bloomtide,"
"before Frostgate,"
"after the First Cart,"
"by the Thirteenth Sunder."

This should make the calendar feel lived in rather than displayed as a reference chart.

# XVII. Current-Era Anchor

The present narrative era is:

**282 S.M.**

Two hundred eighty-two years have passed since the founding settlement that followed the Delphimi Wars and the overthrow of the prior tyrannical royal order.

The Concord of Crown and Commonwealth has therefore existed long enough that almost nobody alive remembers another system personally.

Its compromises are no longer revolutionary.

They are tradition.

Its absurdities are inherited.

Its institutions have accumulated procedure, patronage, corruption, genuine public service, family influence, heroic precedent, and loopholes.

That is essential to the setting.

The political system should not feel like a new experiment still being assembled.

It should feel like a nearly three-hundred-year-old arrangement that everyone complains about, everyone knows how to manipulate, and almost everyone fears replacing because they remember what the histories say came before it.
