# Beneath Dappled Oaks — The Little Year

This document is active setting canon.

The Realm is not culturally uniform merely because it shares a calendar, Crown, National Council, major gods, roads, markets, and high institutions.

There should be **thousands** of small regional festivals, village customs, household protections, roadside shrines, saints' observances, local taboos, lovers' places, harvest practices, mine rites, bridge traditions, fishing blessings, family superstitions, communal meals, and seasonal habits spread across the setting.

Most of them are never important enough to appear in national law.

That is precisely why they matter.

They are how people know where someone is from.

They are how a household feels like home.

They are how an outsider exposes himself without realizing it.

They are how a clever courtier proves she listened.

They are how a noble house wins or loses local affection.

They are how grand religion becomes daily life.

---

# I. The Little Year

The formal calendar described in the World Almanac is only the **Great Year**: the thirteen-month civic, agricultural, political, and religious structure recognized across the Realm.

Every locality also lives inside a **Little Year**.

The Little Year is made of practices too local, too old, too domestic, too informal, or too stubborn to require national recognition.

Examples include:

- a village feast marking the opening of a mill race;
- a local saint remembered only within three valleys;
- a bowl left under a bridge arch;
- a ribbon tied to a hawthorn before marriage;
- a spoonful of oil left at a mine shrine;
- a finger of dough placed outside the door during harvest;
- a lovers' pond where every generation pretends the next generation invented improper behavior;
- a taboo against speaking a dead person's name before sunrise during Longnight;
- a market custom requiring the first basket of apples to be sold for a copper regardless of actual value;
- a road bend where wagoners always touch two fingers to the wheel rim;
- a fishing town where the first trout is returned to the river;
- an orchard where the highest fruit is left for birds and spirits;
- a household that replaces rowan above the granary door every Seedwake while insisting it does not believe in superstition.

No automation or story pass should assume that the examples in this document exhaust the setting.

They establish the density and logic of local culture.

There should always be more.

# II. How Local Practice Works

Most minor traditions belong loosely to one or more of the following categories.

## Household protections

Small rites performed at:

- doors;
- windows;
- hearths;
- bedposts;
- cradles;
- granaries;
- cellars;
- ovens;
- stables;
- garden gates.

These are meant to discourage:

- sickness;
- hunger;
- bad dreams;
- jealousy;
- malicious spirits;
- curse-work;
- vermin;
- infertility;
- household quarrels;
- unlucky travel;
- poor harvest.

The practice may be religious, superstitious, symbolic, or merely traditional.

Characters do not always know which.

## Land-and-labor customs

Rites tied to:

- planting;
- lambing;
- bee keeping;
- orchards;
- first harvest;
- threshing;
- hay cutting;
- milling;
- fishing;
- timber cutting;
- charcoal burning;
- quarrying;
- mining;
- smelting;
- road repair;
- bridge opening;
- irrigation;
- preservation.

These customs usually contain practical knowledge even when wrapped in folklore.

## Roadside and boundary shrines

These exist at:

- bridgeheads;
- ford stones;
- crossroads;
- dangerous bends;
- ferry landings;
- pass summits;
- abandoned battle sites;
- orchard gates;
- field boundaries;
- mine entrances;
- forest edges.

Offerings are generally small:

- crumbs;
- coins;
- ribbons;
- lamp oil;
- flowers;
- salt;
- carved tokens;
- fruit;
- a swallow of drink;
- spoken thanks.

## Small community festivals

These may commemorate:

- a flood survived;
- a bridge rebuilt;
- a saint;
- the first fish run;
- the first cart of spring;
- an old marriage compact;
- the founding of a market;
- the end of a feud;
- a local military victory;
- the arrival of migratory birds;
- the opening of a mine;
- the closing of a mine;
- a miraculous survival;
- a famous local scandal that has slowly become respectable tradition.

## Reputation customs

Some customs become so associated with a place that outsiders know the practice before they know the district's exports.

Such customs are important identity markers.

A character may be mocked, welcomed, distrusted, recognized, or emotionally disarmed because of one.

# III. Cedra Delby — The Finger of Dough

Cedra Delby is a farming community and surrounding district known for steady agriculture, practical households, preserved food, and an old harvest custom called **the Finger of Dough**.

During the harvest period, especially from late Goldgrain through Amberfall, households set aside a small finger-width piece of raw bread dough when the morning bread is mixed.

That piece is not baked.

At night it is placed outside the household door, usually on a flat threshold stone, shallow dish, or little wooden board.

At dawn, the previous night's dough is removed.

The day's new bread is mixed.

A new finger of dough is reserved.

The cycle repeats each night during the harvest span.

## Meaning

The common explanation is:

**Give the good spirits a little life and a little food, and they will remember the house kindly.**

The offering is believed to strengthen benevolent threshold spirits and discourage hungry, jealous, wandering, or malicious influences from entering.

It is not meant to be extravagant.

For poorer households, a finger of dough is enough.

The offering matters because it is part of the household's own daily bread.

Wealthier families sometimes place:

- a small pear;
- a plum;
- half an apple;
- a little dish of honeyed grain;
- another seasonal fruit.

This is considered respectable but slightly showy.

A common Cedra Delby opinion is that **spirits understand bread better than display**.

## Social practice

Children are warned that if the household forgets the dough, "the night may wander in hungry."

Adults laugh at this.

Adults also remember to put the dough out.

Maerethan priests in the district generally tolerate the custom and may bless the first harvest offering.

Formal theologians have periodically argued that it gives too much religious weight to household spirits.

Cedra Delby has survived these objections for generations.

## Story use

The Finger of Dough is useful because it immediately communicates:

- origin;
- class;
- household habit;
- harvest season;
- religious temperament;
- family memory.

A Cedra Delby character living abroad may still pinch off a piece of dough unconsciously.

A noble raised by a Cedra Delby nurse may remember the custom even if his House does not.

A servant may notice that the dough was disturbed.

A curse investigator may discover that a threshold offering has been moved to conceal something beneath the stone.

A lover who remembers to place the dough outside without being reminded may communicate more intimacy than a formal gift.

# IV. Kallioused Lake — Lovers, Glowbugs, and Elderly Hypocrisy

**Kallioused Lake** is a well-known local lovers' destination.

The name may be rendered slightly differently in local spellings, but Kallioused is the accepted common form.

The lake is modest in size and surrounded in places by:

- willow growth;
- reeds;
- hidden coves;
- dark bushes;
- low grassy banks;
- multicolored aquatic plants.

On some full-moon nights the water becomes particularly beautiful.

Glowbugs gather among the reeds.

Luminous plants open or brighten.

Small floating lights, commonly called little will-o'-wisps, may be seen over the water.

Whether every such light is truly supernatural is disputed.

People rarely visit Kallioused Lake for academic clarity.

## Lovers' reputation

Young people have used the lake as a secret courtship place for generations.

Common activities include:

- first kisses;
- shared wine;
- moonlit swimming;
- private conversations;
- clandestine courtship;
- hiding in willow recesses;
- lying badly to elders about where one has been.

The elders of nearby communities repeatedly chase young lovers away from the darker banks.

They denounce:

- improper behavior;
- late-night gatherings;
- skinny dipping;
- concealed alcohol;
- indecent moonlight;
- youths returning with reeds in their hair.

Many of these same elders know the best hidden coves with suspicious precision.

Adults are not immune to the lake's reputation.

Married couples occasionally return there.

Unmarried adults continue to use it.

Respectable persons have been caught swimming without adequate clothing.

The elders frown on this.

The elders have always frowned on this.

## Bright Sunder at Kallioused

The **Bright Sunder of Bloomtide** is the lake's most famous night.

Glowbugs and luminous vegetation make the shoreline especially striking.

Young lovers commonly exchange:

- ribbons;
- flowers;
- shared fruit;
- whispered promises.

A local saying holds that a couple who exchange a ribbon beneath the full Bloomtide moon will either become serious or part within thirteen weeks.

Believers cite fulfilled examples as proof.

Skeptics note that exchanging secret ribbons at a lovers' lake already indicates that the relationship is unlikely to remain emotionally neutral.

## Cultural importance

Kallioused Lake is not merely a romantic backdrop.

It is a place where generations overlap.

A mother may forbid her daughter to visit the exact willow cove where she once kissed the girl's father.

A stern magistrate may be blackmailed with the knowledge that he was once dragged half-dressed out of the lake by the same constable whose budget he now controls.

A couple returning after twenty years may find the place altered by new paths but emotionally unchanged.

A young noble may discover that a supposedly private rendezvous location is locally famous and therefore not private at all.

# V. The Small Sacred Landscape

The roads of the Realm should be dense with minor sacred markers.

A road does not need a great temple to be religious.

## Bridge shrines

Common dedications:

- Nera Bridgewife;
- Selvara;
- unnamed local bridge spirits;
- drowned builders;
- remembered ferrymen.

Common offerings:

- bread heels;
- blue thread;
- copper coins;
- lamp oil;
- flowers;
- the first sip from a travel flask.

Some bridge guilds maintain official shrines.

Local people often add unofficial ones beside them.

## Crossroad stones

At old crossroads, travelers may:

- touch the stone;
- leave salt;
- turn a coin;
- tap a wagon wheel;
- say the destination aloud;
- avoid saying the destination aloud;
- leave a sprig of herb.

Different regions contradict one another.

That is normal.

## Mine shrines

Mine rites should be particularly persistent.

Examples include:

- a spoonful of oil for Tern's Little Lamp;
- tapping two fingers against the shaft stone;
- leaving the final bite of a meal above ground;
- refusing to whistle beneath a certain depth;
- tying red thread around a new support beam;
- naming a dangerous tunnel after someone who survived it.

Formal Kharun worship may coexist with all of these.

## Orchard shrines

Common forms:

- ribbons tied to the oldest tree;
- first fruit left at the root;
- highest fruit left for birds;
- carved wood faces in boundary trunks;
- bowls of water during drought.

## Road bends

Dangerous bends accumulate practice.

A bend where three wagons once fell may have:

- a cairn;
- a painted eye;
- a bell;
- a carved saint;
- a tradition of slowing regardless of weather;
- a curse against anyone who overtakes another wagon there.

These customs can persist long after the road itself has been improved.

# VI. The Little Court in Daily Life

The Little Court is not a fixed list.

It includes:

- minor gods;
- saints;
- local spirits;
- deified ancestors;
- civic patrons;
- mine guardians;
- river powers;
- forest beings;
- household protectors;
- figures whose theological category varies by region.

One village may call a being a goddess.

The next may call her a saint.

A third may insist she was an unusually competent widow.

All three may leave the same offering.

## Examples

### Nera Bridgewife

Patron of bridge crossings, ferries, flood travel, and people waiting for travelers to return.

### Tern's Little Lamp

Mining protector associated with warning lights, bad air, unstable stone, and unexpected survival.

### Grandmother Apple-Bough

Orchard guardian.

Oldest trees and first fruit are commonly associated with her.

### The Reed King

Marsh, floodplain, eels, lost boats, reed beds, and uncertain river boundaries.

### The Three Cask Sisters

Fermentation, brewing, preservation, patience, and sealed things best left unopened.

### Old White Stag

Northern roads, snow, lost travelers, hunters, and boundaries.

### Saint Veyren of the Empty Chair

Remembrance of those who do not return.

### Lady of the First Cart

First successful spring market journey.

### The Cellar Child

Cool storage, hidden food, jars, mice, and household preservation.

These are examples, not an exhaustive pantheon.

# VII. Thousands of Small Festivals

Each region should possess a large number of tiny observances that may not matter beyond a few settlements.

Examples of the correct scale include:

## First Lamb Supper

Held when the first healthy lamb is born after a hard winter.

The mother animal receives a decorated feed bowl.

## Mill-Wheel Morning

Marks the reopening of a mill after winter flood.

Children throw flowers into the race.

## First Swallow Day

The first reliable swallow sighting prompts children to run messages between households announcing spring.

## Bee Wake

Families inspect and bless bee skeps.

Honey from the previous year is shared.

## Ferry Song Night

A river settlement gathers around the landing and sings old crossing songs.

## Charcoal Supper

Forest workers share dark bread, smoked onions, and beer before the main charcoal-burning season.

## Pond Lantern Night

Small floating lanterns are placed on a village pond.

Officially religious.

Frequently romantic.

## Apple Shout

Villagers make deliberate noise in orchards after harvest to "wake the trees for next year."

## Threshing Race

Farm crews compete for prestige, drinks, or a symbolic prize.

## Barn Lantern

A harvest dance with lanterns hung among rafters.

Much matchmaking occurs.

## Sausage Day

Winter slaughter and preservation becomes a communal work festival.

## Cellar Opening

A household or village ceremonially opens the first major preserved stores of winter.

## Dead-Name Vigil

Local families speak the names of the dead around a fire, sometimes only after moonrise.

## Bridge Lamping

Residents replace or relight shrine lamps at a bridge before winter.

## Snow Road Marking

Communities place poles, ribbons, stones, or carved markers along routes likely to disappear beneath snow.

# VIII. Local Food as Memory

Small festivals should have small foods.

The dish need not be spectacular.

Often the cultural force comes precisely from ordinary food being eaten at one particular time.

Examples:

- onion broth after road clearing;
- apple peel cakes after orchard work;
- fresh curds on First Lamb Supper;
- rye wafers at bridge vigils;
- a single honey spoon at Bee Wake;
- black bread at mine blessing;
- pickled onions at ferry festivals;
- berry dumplings at upland moon dances;
- hot milk with spice after winter road marking;
- little fried dough knots at local saints' fairs;
- leftover crust soaked in cider after pressing.

A person can miss home because nobody elsewhere cooks the cheap festival dish correctly.

That matters.

---

# IX. Local Custom and Class

The wealthy often enlarge customs.

The poor often preserve them.

A farm household may leave a finger of dough.

A wealthy estate may leave a polished bowl with fruit.

A miner may leave one spoonful of oil.

A mine owner may fund an elaborate bronze shrine.

The underlying practice can be the same while class changes its form.

This creates opportunities for:

- class tension;
- affectionate mockery;
- imitation;
- resentment;
- patronage;
- local prestige.

A noble who performs a local custom too elaborately may appear ridiculous.

A noble who refuses it may appear contemptuous.

A noble who performs it correctly and simply may earn genuine affection.

---

# X. Local Custom and Politics

Minor tradition is politically important.

Great Houses, magistrates, guilds, temples, and candidates should care about:

- who attends local festivals;
- who pays for repairs;
- who remembers the saint's day;
- who funds the bridge lamps;
- who donates flour after a poor harvest;
- who insults a household rite;
- who knows which local dish belongs at which event.

A House may win national honors and lose a district because its steward laughed at a village's old bridge shrine.

A magistrate may gain enduring support because she paid to repair the shrine without making a speech about it.

Local political legitimacy is partly cultural literacy.

---

# XI. Local Custom and Characterization

Every major recurring character should eventually accumulate:

- customs from their birthplace;
- customs from their House;
- customs from their order;
- customs learned from servants, nurses, lovers, friends, or travel;
- at least one practice they claim not to believe in but still perform;
- at least one local food that means home;
- at least one festival memory;
- at least one place associated with young love, shame, fear, or family.

These details should persist.

A character from Cedra Delby should not remember the Finger of Dough once and forget it forever.

A person who first kissed someone at Kallioused Lake may remember the smell of willow and glowbugs years later.

Cultural memory should recur the way attraction grammar recurs.

---

# XII. Local Custom and Magic

Minor practices are not all magically effective.

Some are:

- genuine low magic;
- diluted remnants of older rites;
- symbolic religion;
- practical folk knowledge;
- superstition;
- coincidence;
- social ritual.

The setting should not always resolve which.

However, because malicious sympathetic magic also uses:

- thresholds;
- personal remnants;
- food;
- knots;
- carvings;
- household objects;
- hidden tokens;

a culture rich in harmless folk practice creates excellent camouflage for dangerous magic.

A harmless ribbon charm may resemble a forbidden binding.

A threshold offering may conceal a fetish.

A shrine token may be replaced with something malicious.

A household can therefore be full of objects that look suspicious to outsiders and perfectly normal to locals.

This should complicate Dark Arts investigations.

# XIV. Automation Rules for Story Continuation

Every Beneath Dappled Oaks story run should treat local culture as ambient world logic.

Do not force a minor custom into every scene.

Do ensure that over multiple chapters the setting continually produces signs of local life.

When entering a new village, district, estate, mine, bridge, market, shrine, festival, road, or household, consider whether at least one local practice would naturally appear.

Useful questions:

1. What do people here leave at the door?
2. What do they refuse to do after dark?
3. What local saint or spirit do they invoke?
4. What food marks this season?
5. What tiny festival is approaching?
6. What does a local child know that an outsider does not?
7. What road or bridge custom do travelers observe?
8. What local romantic place exists?
9. What old scandal has become tradition?
10. What practice would expose an outsider?
11. What custom would make a homesick character emotional?
12. What harmless folk object could be mistaken for Dark Arts work?

When inventing a new minor custom, record it in a durable setting file if it becomes plot-relevant, character-defining, geographically recurring, politically important, or likely to reappear.

Do not turn every custom into a magical effect.

Do not turn every shrine into a major god.

Do not make every village quaint in the same way.

The Realm should feel culturally dense, inconsistent, old, practical, sentimental, superstitious, political, and lived in.

# XV. Canon Anchors

The following are explicitly established and should not be discarded:

- Cedra Delby's Finger of Dough harvest threshold custom.
- Wealthier Cedra Delby households may use fruit instead.
- The offering is understood as a little life and food for protective spirits.
- Kallioused Lake is a popular young lovers' getaway.
- Elders repeatedly chase young people from secluded bushes and coves.
- The lake is associated with first kisses, moonlit courtship, glowbugs, colorful aquatic plants, little floating lights, and occasional skinny dipping by both youth and adults.
- Bright Sunder in Bloomtide is especially associated with the lake.
- The Little Court and minor traditions should number in the thousands across the Realm.
- Small religious and cultural practices are politically, emotionally, and socially meaningful even when they never enter national records.
