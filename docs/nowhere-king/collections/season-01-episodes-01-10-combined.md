# Episode 1 — Crown Without Court

The first report of the morning concerned what the king had eaten.

Luceran learned this because the second report concerned who had learned it first.

He had broken his fast with black bread, soft cheese, two pears and watered wine in the small solar adjoining his father's old study. He had eaten one pear and half the bread. The cheese had gone untouched. He had sent the wine away after one cup because it tasted sour, although the cupbearer swore it was the same cask served the previous evening.

By the time Luceran crossed the inner gallery for morning prayers, House Damaris knew he had refused the cheese, House Nerezza knew he had returned the wine, someone attached to the Velvet Exchange had apparently decided that the king's appetite was failing, and a Lethayne secretary had asked one of the royal physicians whether grief might make a young man unusually susceptible to suggestion.

The physician had declined to answer.

That refusal had become a report of its own.

Luceran stood in the chapel doorway while Brother Calven Orsanne explained this in the careful tone used by men who wished to deliver bad news without becoming part of it.

"How," Luceran asked, "does House Damaris know what I had for breakfast?"

Calven looked toward the altar as though divine intervention remained possible.

"One of the kitchen clerks has a sister married to a Damaris factor."

"And Nerezza?"

"The wine steward's nephew is employed in the customs office."

"Lethayne?"

"I do not know."

"Excellent. At least one mystery remains in the world."

Calven did not laugh.

Luceran regretted the joke immediately. He had begun regretting jokes before he finished making them. People watched his humor now. They watched whether he smiled too soon after his parents' deaths, whether he smiled too little, whether he prayed long enough, whether he slept, whether he drank, whether he looked tired, whether he looked relieved.

Especially whether he looked relieved.

His father had abdicated eleven weeks before his death.

That fact had transformed grief into evidence.

King Othmar had stepped down after a winter of worsening pain, shaking hands, sleeplessness and the humiliating parade of physicians who had all agreed that the burden of government was killing him while disagreeing about how. Luceran had been crowned while both parents still lived. His mother had stood beside Othmar during the ceremony, one hand beneath his elbow when she thought nobody was looking.

For thirty-seven days afterward, Luceran had been king with parents.

Then his mother sickened.

Then his father worsened.

Then, within nine days of one another, both were dead.

Every faction at court had an explanation.

No faction called it murder where Luceran could hear.

They were more civilized than that.

House Valcere wondered aloud whether the royal physicians had been selected for medical skill or political reliability. House Orsanne regretted that certain alchemical treatments had been permitted. The Glass Choir found traces in a tonic bottle and then spent three days arguing whether the traces were medicinal. A Bellisar cousin remembered that an Ustran merchant had supplied citrus peel used in one of his mother's draughts. Ustran's ambassador responded by producing invoices proving that the peel had been purchased from a Damaris warehouse. Damaris discovered that the warehouse inspector's brother belonged to a Nerezza tax office. Nerezza replied with six years of customs records and an accusation so carefully phrased that it was technically a request for clarification.

Nobody accused anyone.

Everybody accused everyone.

And every explanation ended, somehow, with the young king being advised to trust the person explaining it.

"Your Majesty?" Calven said.

Luceran realized he had been staring at the memorial candles.

Not the seven old candles at Edselsto. These were ordinary wax, newly lit, already shortening.

"What?"

"The prayers."

"Yes."

He entered the chapel.

By luncheon, three separate courtiers would know how long he had knelt.

---

His father had loved the western study because it had only one public door.

Luceran loved it because the windows were too high for anyone in the courtyard to see inside.

He painted there in the afternoon.

Not well.

That had never mattered when he was a prince.

He painted mountains from memory because mountains did not ask him for offices.

The canvas on the easel showed a black ridge beneath a pale winter sky. He had been working on the same line of pines for nearly an hour when the door opened without a knock.

Luceran turned with the brush raised like a dagger.

Montegrain stopped in the doorway.

"If that is how you receive foreign nobility now, I can come back wearing armor."

Luceran lowered the brush.

"You are not foreign nobility."

"The Elbren court will be devastated to hear it."

"You are a parasite I have known since I was twelve."

"Ah. Then diplomacy survives."

Montegrain closed the door behind him.

He was dressed too well for mourning and too badly for formal court, which was exactly how he had dressed when they were boys and precisely why Luceran had liked him. His coat was Elbren blue, his boots muddy, and one cuff had been repaired with thread that did not match.

He looked at the canvas.

"That tree is leaning."

"It is a mountain pine."

"It is trying to escape the painting."

"It has the right idea."

Montegrain's smile vanished.

There were very few people before whom Luceran could watch a smile vanish without wondering who would be told.

"How bad?" Montegrain asked.

Luceran set down the brush.

"I had a pear this morning."

"Monster."

"Damaris knows."

Montegrain stared.

"Already?"

"Before prayers."

"Efficient."

"Nerezza knows I refused the wine. Lethayne wants to know whether grief has made me suggestible."

"Has it?"

"Apparently everyone intends to find out."

Montegrain walked to the sideboard and poured himself water without asking.

Luceran watched him do it and felt, absurdly, grateful.

"My mother used to come in here," Luceran said. "She would sit there and complain that Father's chairs were designed by a man who hated backs."

Montegrain did not say he was sorry.

Everyone else was sorry.

"They were," he said instead. "I sat in one once. Your dynasty has committed crimes."

Luceran laughed.

Then his throat closed.

He sat down hard on the window bench.

Montegrain waited.

"I think they killed them," Luceran said.

There it was.

He had not said it aloud.

Not to the physicians. Not to the chancellor. Not to the court wizard. Not at the funerals. Not even alone.

Montegrain's face did not change quickly enough to be reassuring.

"Who?"

"I don't know."

"That is an important part of an accusation."

"I know."

"Do you have evidence?"

"No."

"Then what do you have?"

Luceran looked at the unfinished mountain.

"Timing."

"Timing is not poison."

"Father abdicates. I become king. Thirty-seven days later Mother is ill. Then Father worsens. Then both are dead. And before their rooms are cold every house in this fucking palace has a theory explaining why one of the other houses did it and why I should place myself in their hands for protection."

Montegrain sat opposite him.

"That proves they are vultures."

"I knew that already."

"It does not prove one of them killed your parents."

"I know."

"Do you?"

Luceran looked up.

Montegrain held his gaze.

This was why he had been admitted.

Not because he was safe. Luceran no longer believed anyone was safe.

Because Montegrain would say the thing that made him angriest if he thought it true.

"Yes," Luceran said finally. "I know."

"Good. Be suspicious. Do not become stupid."

"A stirring speech from Elbren."

"We charge extra for stirring."

A knock sounded.

Both men went silent.

The door opened after permission this time.

The Court Wizard entered carrying a leather folio under one arm and looking irritated with the existence of corridors.

Magister Vael Teren was old enough that nobody knew whether the title Court Wizard described his office, his profession, or simply what everyone had called him for so long that correcting them had become pointless.

He looked at Montegrain.

"Still here?"

"The king enjoys me."

"The king is grieving. His judgment is impaired."

Luceran said, "You see? Everyone agrees."

Vael looked at him.

"On what?"

"That grief makes me easy to manipulate."

"Grief makes everyone easy to manipulate. So does hunger. So does lust. So does fear. So does being twenty-three and wearing a crown. This is not an arcane discovery."

Montegrain lifted his cup.

"I missed him."

Vael dropped the folio on the table.

"Your mother's final alchemical assays. Nothing in them proves poisoning. Nothing in them disproves it either. Half the compounds used by physicians become poisonous if the dose is wrong. The other half become poisonous if the patient is unlucky."

Luceran opened the folio.

"And my father?"

"Dying for months before he abdicated."

"Could someone have hurried it?"

"Yes."

"Could someone have hurried Mother's?"

"Yes."

"Could you tell?"

"Not with certainty."

Luceran shut the folio.

"Wonderful."

Vael sat.

"Certainty is a luxury."

"So I am told by people who then ask me to sign things."

"Do not sign them."

"Avarren says government requires signatures."

"Avarren is correct."

"I hate how often people I dislike are correct."

Montegrain said, "That is government."

For almost an hour, the three of them spoke privately.

Or Luceran believed they did.

They discussed the physicians. They discussed his father's abdication. Vael refused to accuse the Glass Choir. Montegrain refused to accuse Ustran. Luceran accused everyone in rotation and was contradicted by both of them. Eventually the conversation wandered into Elbren horses, a vulgar story about an ambassador's wig, and whether Luceran's mountain pine was anatomically possible.

When Montegrain left, he clasped Luceran's shoulder.

When Vael left after him, he told Luceran to sleep.

Luceran remained in the study until dusk.

He painted three more trees.

By supper, House Valcere knew that Montegrain had remained with him for fifty-three minutes.

By evening prayers, House Lethayne knew Vael had visited afterward carrying a folio.

By midnight, someone had circulated the conclusion that Elbren and the Court Wizard were jointly advising the king on the investigation into his parents' deaths.

There was no investigation.

That did not matter.

---

Mara Kest knew the rumor was wrong because she knew exactly how long the king had been alone.

That was not because she spied on him.

It was because she was assistant housekeeper for the royal apartments, and if the king occupied a room for four hours then somebody had to know whether to send in wood, lamps, water, food, chamber pots, clean cloths, and the boy whose job it was to remove ashes without setting fire to the carpet.

The distinction between service and surveillance had become increasingly theoretical.

"He ate?" Mara asked.

Frederick Doss nodded.

"Half the bread. Pear."

"Later."

"Nothing."

"You offered?"

"Twice."

"And?"

"He told me if I asked a third time he would have me hanged."

Mara looked up sharply.

Frederick grinned.

"Then he said not really."

"Do not repeat that."

"I wasn't going to."

"You just repeated it to me."

"You're you."

Mara stared at him.

Frederick's grin faded.

That was how it happened.

Not spies in black cloaks. Not secret tunnels. A footman told a housekeeper because she was the person who needed to know whether the king had eaten. The housekeeper told a kitchen clerk to prepare something plain. The kitchen clerk told a cook why. The cook complained to a supplier that the king was not eating the elaborate mourning dishes. The supplier belonged to a merchant league. By morning, somebody with money had learned something true.

Then somebody made it useful.

Mara rubbed her forehead.

"No more questions about his meals unless I ask."

"People ask me."

"Who?"

Frederick hesitated.

"People."

"Names."

"Lord Pell's man asked whether His Majesty was drinking. A Bellisar groom asked whether he was sleeping. One of the Nerezza clerks offered me two silvers to tell him whether the king had vomited after breakfast."

Mara's face hardened.

"Did you take it?"

"No."

"Why not?"

Frederick looked offended.

"Because I'm not an idiot."

"That was not my question."

He glanced down the corridor.

"Two silvers isn't enough to become the man who sells the king's vomit."

Mara almost laughed.

Then she realized he had answered honestly.

"How much would be?"

Frederick looked at her.

For a moment, neither of them spoke.

"I don't know," he said.

That answer worried her more than a lie would have.

---

The court used Luceran's exhaustion because courts used everything.

Nobody said, **The king is tired; now is the time to make him give us what we want.**

They were subtler.

House Bellisar requested an audience after supper because the horse levies could not possibly wait until morning. House Nerezza sent a revenue memorandum at dawn because the eastern customs dispute was urgent. The Red Script requested clarification of an oath formula. The River Factors sent a gift of rare fruit accompanied by a petition. House Lethayne sent a widowed cousin to express condolences in person; she was beautiful, intelligent, and very careful not to flirt until Luceran was tired enough to notice that she had not been flirting.

House Edevane sent family.

That was worse.

His cousin Oren Edevane found him outside the council chamber on the fourth day after the funerals and embraced him before Luceran could decide whether he wanted to be embraced.

"You look exhausted."

"Everyone tells me."

"Then you should rest."

"Everyone tells me that too. Usually immediately before asking for something."

Oren smiled sadly.

"I ask nothing."

Luceran waited.

Oren lasted eight seconds.

"Except that you consider appointing someone you trust to manage the daily petitions."

"Someone like you?"

"Someone like family."

"You are like family because you are family."

"Exactly."

Luceran walked away.

Oren followed.

"Luceran."

The use of his name stopped him.

"Your parents are dead. Your father placed the crown on you before he died because he knew this would be difficult. You do not need to prove strength by doing everything yourself."

There was kindness in it.

That was the problem.

Luceran believed Oren loved him.

He also believed Oren wanted the petitions office.

Both things could be true.

The court had become unbearable because both things were always true.

Affection had a faction.

Concern had a patron.

Grief had a constituency.

Even privacy had witnesses.

"No," Luceran said.

Oren's expression tightened.

"No to what?"

"Whatever comes after the kindness."

"That is unfair."

"Yes."

Luceran walked away before fairness could become another negotiation.

He hated himself for that one before he reached the end of the corridor.

Oren might actually have meant the first part.

That was another thing nobody had warned him about when kingship was still theoretical: manipulation did not require false affection. People could love him and still want offices. They could mourn his parents and still calculate what their deaths changed. They could sincerely worry that he was exhausted and sincerely believe their own faction ought to be the one trusted to relieve the burden.

As prince, Luceran had thought politics meant knowing which house hated which other house, remembering who controlled which province, and learning not to insult foreign ambassadors.

He had been good at that sort of politics.

It turned out to be the children's version.

Nobody had explained that kingship meant waking to discover that the person asking whether he had slept might be worried about him, gathering information, seeking intimacy, testing weakness, performing duty, or doing all five at once.

He did not know how to tell the difference.

He was twenty-three years old, unquestionably an adult, legally sovereign, crowned before witnesses from half the continent—and privately aware that men twice his age could make him feel like a boy who had wandered into the wrong room.

That awareness did not make him wiser.

Mostly it made him tired.

Mostly it made him want the room to empty.

---

The decision came two nights later.

Not in council.

Not after a vote.

Not because an archivist discovered a forgotten memorandum.

It came because Luceran tried to take a shit in peace.

The royal privy adjoined his bedchamber behind two doors and a narrow dressing room. It was, by any reasonable definition, private.

When he emerged, Captain Tovan Rhyse was waiting outside the dressing room with the expression of a man who would rather fight cavalry.

"What?"

Rhyse hesitated.

"Your Majesty, there is a rumor that you were ill this evening."

Luceran stared at him.

"I was not."

"I know."

"How do you know?"

Rhyse's face became worse.

Luceran closed his eyes.

"No."

"Your valet asked for hot water. Someone assumed—"

"Hot water."

"Yes."

"My valet asked for hot water."

"Yes."

"And now the court thinks I am ill."

"Part of it."

"Which part?"

"It is difficult to say."

Luceran began laughing.

Rhyse did not.

The laughter kept coming until Luceran had to lean against the wall.

Then, without warning, it became rage.

"Get Avarren."

"Your Majesty—"

"Get Avarren. Get Harrowmere. Get Nerezza. Get Vael. Wake Calven. Wake every miserable bastard who will tell me why I cannot do what I am about to do."

Rhyse went.

Luceran dressed himself.

By the time the council assembled, he had dragged an old map from his father's study and spread it across the table.

Castle Edselsto stood in black ink in the northern mountains.

The room smelled of lamp oil, sleep and irritation.

Avarren arrived first, robe thrown over his nightshirt.

He saw the map.

"No."

Luceran almost admired him.

"I have not spoken."

"Your father used to look at that map when he was angry with us."

"Then perhaps he had the right idea."

Harrowmere entered fastening her sword belt. Nerezza arrived wrapped in a fur-lined coat and carrying no ledgers, which made him look naked. Vael came last and did not apologize.

Luceran waited until the doors closed.

"I am leaving."

Avarren rubbed both hands over his face.

"For the night?"

"The capital."

Nobody spoke.

Luceran put his finger on Edselsto.

"I am done with this place."

"Your Majesty—"

"No. I mean it. I am done waking up inside other people's arrangements. I am done having to ask which cousin appointed the man who lights my fire. I am done with condolences that turn into petitions and concern that turns into appointments and people telling me to rest while sliding papers under my hand."

Avarren said carefully, "You are describing a court."

"I am describing this court."

"There is a distinction?"

"There will be."

Luceran heard how childish that sounded.

He said it anyway.

"We have talked about returning the imperial household here for years. My father talked about it. His father talked about it. Every time someone says Edselsto would give the Crown room to breathe, everyone else explains that the road is poor, the roofs are old, diplomats will complain, merchants will complain, half the court will complain, and it will cost too much."

Nerezza said, "All of those things remain true."

"I don't care."

"You will when I show you the bill."

"Then show it to me in Edselsto."

Avarren leaned forward.

"Your Majesty, moving the imperial court is not the same as changing houses."

"I know."

"Do you? Archives. Seals. Treasury. Kitchens. Horses. Guards. Messengers. Ambassadors. Judges. Clerks. Craftsmen. Servants. Warehouses. Roads—"

"Yes."

"You cannot simply arrive at a ruined castle with a crown and declare it a capital."

"Watch me."

Harrowmere laughed.

Avarren glared at her.

"Do not encourage him."

"I am not. I am enjoying you."

Luceran planted both hands on the map.

"I am not asking whether Edselsto is convenient. I am asking whether it is still standing."

That changed the room.

Vael looked at the map.

"The old steward still sends winter letters."

Avarren nodded reluctantly.

"A caretaker's account reaches the household office twice a year."

"Then someone goes tomorrow."

Nerezza frowned.

"Someone?"

"An emissary. A diplomat. A man with eyes. I do not care what title makes everyone comfortable. He rides to Edselsto with a mason, a carpenter, enough soldiers not to be robbed on the road, and a letter under my seal. He looks at the walls. He looks at the roofs. He asks the steward what is habitable. He asks the village what can be supplied. If there are enough rooms left standing for me, my household, guards and the first work crews, he tells the steward the king is returning and to prepare for us."

Avarren said, "And if there are not?"

"Then the mason tells me what has to be made safe before I sleep there."

"And if it takes a year?"

"Then we begin in tents."

"Luceran."

It was Vael.

Not Your Majesty.

Luceran looked at him.

The old wizard's face was unreadable.

"Are you leaving because you think someone here murdered your parents?"

The room became perfectly still.

So that was how the secret would begin.

Not leaked by a servant.

Spoken by the one man old enough to risk it.

Luceran considered lying.

"Partly."

Avarren's face changed.

Harrowmere swore under her breath.

Nerezza said nothing.

Luceran continued before they could start.

"I have no proof. I am not accusing a house. I am not arresting physicians. I am not beginning a purge. I am not going to let every faction use my suspicion to murder its rivals for me."

Vael nodded once.

"Good."

"But I am also not going to sit in a palace where everyone knows who visits my rooms, how long they stay, whether I eat, whether I sleep, whether I drink, whether I fuck, whether I shit, whether I cry, and then pretend this is merely the natural weather of monarchy."

Avarren opened his mouth.

Luceran cut him off.

"Edselsto has one old steward and half a village."

Avarren stared at him.

"That is not the argument you think it is."

"It means they are not already there."

"Who?"

"All of them."

Luceran swept a hand toward the invisible capital beyond the chamber walls.

"The houses. The guild men. The cousins. The secretaries who know when I sneeze. The widows who arrive with condolences and leave with signatures. At Edselsto I decide who has rooms. I decide who comes through the gate. If an ambassador wants to see me, he can ride there when I summon him. If a lord wants something, he can ask instead of appearing outside my breakfast room."

Avarren's expression softened in the worst possible way.

Pity.

Luceran hated it immediately.

"Majesty," the chancellor said, "a court is not the palace walls."

"I know that."

"I do not think you do."

Luceran's jaw tightened.

Avarren went on.

"You will take guards because you require guards. They have families and patrons. You will take clerks because you require clerks. They have cousins. You will take cooks, grooms, masons, priests, physicians, scribes and merchants. Ambassadors will follow because you are the king. Petitioners will follow because you are the king. Houses will rent rooms in the village because you are the king. Guilds will build roads because you are the king. Wherever you put the Crown, people will build a court around it."

"Then at least it will be mine."

"Courts are never entirely anyone's."

Luceran knew, dimly, that this was probably important.

He was too tired to care.

"Then I will settle for one that does not already know how long I spend on the privy."

Harrowmere looked down at the map.

"The road is defensible."

Avarren turned on her.

"Stop helping."

"It is."

Nerezza said, "It will be ruinously expensive."

"Yes."

"You cannot say yes as though that makes money appear."

"No. But I can decide what money is for."

That silenced him.

Luceran straightened.

He was tired enough that the room moved slightly when he did.

He did not care.

"Send the emissary at dawn. Send masons. Send carpenters. Send soldiers. Send someone from the household who knows what a king actually needs to sleep, eat and hold audience without dying of cold. They are not to write me a treatise. They are to look at the fucking castle and tell me whether it is still there."

Avarren exhaled through his nose.

"And if it is?"

Luceran looked at Edselsto.

"Then I am going home."

---

The man chosen to go was Sir Edran Vey, a minor royal diplomat whose greatest qualification was that no great house could claim him without provoking three others to object.

His second qualification was that he could ride.

His third was that Avarren trusted him to describe a collapsed roof as a collapsed roof rather than an opportunity for constitutional reform.

By dawn, Edran had a sealed royal letter, twelve mounted guards, Master Corren Bale of the Stone and Beam League, carpenter Iven Marr, and Mistress Mara Kest, who had been selected after loudly insisting that no collection of men should be permitted to decide whether a residence was habitable without asking where the water came from, where the bedding would dry, and whether the kitchens could feed more than six people.

Frederick watched them assemble in the lower court.

"You're going?" he asked Mara.

"Apparently."

"To Edselsto?"

"That is where the castle is."

"Is the king moving?"

Mara looked at him.

"The king has decided he is moving. Whether the rest of creation cooperates remains to be seen."

Frederick grinned.

"Will there be extra pay?"

"For me? There had better be."

"For everyone."

"Why?"

"Mountain hardship."

"You invented that."

"It sounds official."

"You have never seen a mountain."

"Exactly. Hardship."

Mara shook her head and mounted badly.

Frederick steadied the stirrup.

"Bring back something expensive."

"I will bring back a list of everything broken."

"Same thing."

The gates opened.

Edran Vey rode north carrying the first direct royal command Castle Edselsto had received in years:

**The King considers his return. Receive our servant. Show him the house entire where it may safely be shown. Prepare those chambers that remain sound. Summon such labor as may be had without coercion. Make ready for masons, carpenters, soldiers and household officers. If the old royal apartments cannot receive us, make ready whatever can. We will not be delayed for ceremony.**

Below it, in Luceran's own hand, was a shorter instruction to the steward.

**Tell me what still stands. Do not flatter me.**

---

By breakfast, the court knew an armed royal party had left by the northern gate.

By midmorning, House Bellisar knew there were twelve guards.

By noon, the River Factors knew a mason had gone.

By afternoon, House Lethayne had learned that Mara Kest was among the party and concluded, correctly, that the question was not military alone.

By supper, three ambassadors had requested clarification of rumors that the imperial court intended to relocate.

Avarren sent all three the same answer.

**His Majesty is considering arrangements for the proper exercise of the Crown. You will be informed of matters affecting your mission when they are settled.**

It was the diplomatic form of go to hell.

Luceran read it twice and approved it without alteration.

Then he returned to the western study.

His painting was still on the easel.

The mountain pine still leaned as if trying to escape.

Montegrain had left a note beneath the palette.

**If you flee to a mountain, I am still visiting. Elbren possesses horses. — M.**

Luceran sat alone for almost an hour.

Nobody entered.

Nobody asked him to eat.

Nobody asked him to sign anything.

He knew perfectly well that someone outside the door was recording how long he remained inside.

For once, he let them.

Soon, if Edselsto still stood, they could come report on him there.

Or perhaps they would not.

Perhaps the road really would be too inconvenient. Perhaps half the useless people who spent their days discovering what he ate would decide the mountain was not worth the trouble. Perhaps he could choose a smaller household, close a gate, refuse a visitor, and spend one entire afternoon painting without learning at supper which house had counted the minutes.

He knew this was not a plan anyone on his council considered sophisticated.

At that moment, sophistication could go fuck itself.

He wanted a door he could close.

He wanted a room that belonged to him before somebody else's grandfather had learned how to listen outside it.

He wanted, with all the stubborn certainty of an exhausted young man who had been king just long enough to believe geography might solve politics, to go home.

He picked up the brush and painted another black pine.

# Episode 2 — Packing The Monarchy

The argument began over lunch.

That mattered because Luceran had discovered that bad news became more irritating when delivered over food he was not permitted to enjoy.

The table in the western solar held cold chicken, bread, mustard, pears, two cups of wine, three unopened petitions, one opened petition he regretted opening, and Master Clavard Clifford standing at his elbow with a ledger.

Clavard was one of the men Luceran had liked before becoming king.

He was intelligent without advertising it, careful with money without worshipping it, and possessed the rare courtly skill of explaining arithmetic without making the listener feel stupid. As prince, Luceran had thought of him as one of the competent adults who made the palace function.

As king, Luceran was learning that competent adults could say astonishingly irritating things with perfect sincerity.

"We cannot assemble the labor your order requires by tomorrow, Majesty."

Hammering sounded outside.

Luceran chewed.

"Say that again."

Clavard glanced down.

"The Crown works office reports its master masons already committed. The Guild of Stone and Beam says its larger crews are contracted through the month. Carpenters are similarly—"

Hammer.

A saw rasped somewhere beyond the shutters.

Then came the heavy clack of dressed stone settling onto dressed stone.

Luceran put down his fork.

"Clavard."

"Majesty?"

"You are an intelligent man."

Clavard hesitated.

Lord Chancellor Aurel Avarren, seated farther down the table, looked up from a letter.

"You are a wonderful courtier."

Clavard became visibly less comfortable.

"You handle my household finances quite handily."

"Thank you, Majesty."

"Now listen."

Luceran raised one finger.

The room went quiet.

Hammer.

Hammer.

Saw.

A workman shouted something across the lane.

Luceran pointed toward the windows.

"For three weeks I have eaten in this room while that damned mansion is being built."

Nobody answered.

"Every day."

Clavard looked toward the shutters.

Luceran's voice rose.

"Lord Edmund Moncler is building that absurd thing a stone's throw from my palace because I am here. He wants a capital residence because the court is here. I have listened to his carpenters sawing through luncheon, his masons hammering through prayers, and his carts blocking the east lane since before my coronation."

Avarren said, very carefully, "Majesty—"

"No. Listen."

Hammer.

Luceran pointed again, as though the sound itself were evidence.

"I can hear the labor you are telling me does not exist."

Clavard inhaled.

"Majesty, I said there is insufficient uncommitted guild labor on the timetable—"

"So there are laborers."

"Yes."

"Carpenters."

"Yes."

"Masons."

"Yes."

"Working right now."

"Yes."

"Across the street."

Clavard closed his ledger.

"Private contracts are not the same as available royal labor."

Luceran stared at him.

That was a perfectly reasonable sentence.

He hated it.

"Fine."

Avarren closed his eyes.

Clavard noticed.

"What?"

Avarren folded his letter.

"I have learned to fear the moment he says 'fine' and stands up."

Luceran stood.

Clavard looked alarmed.

"Majesty?"

Luceran reached for his coat.

"Where are you going?"

"Across the street."

The aide blinked.

"My lord."

Luceran walked toward the door.

"My lord, what are you doing?"

"Finding the labor."

Clavard followed.

So did Avarren.

The page outside the door saw the king walking quickly and fell into step because pages generally assumed a rapidly moving king was going somewhere important.

A household secretary saw the chancellor following and followed the chancellor.

Brother Calven emerged from a side passage, saw the growing procession and asked the fatal question.

"Where are we going?"

"Apparently," Avarren said, "to steal Lord Moncler's house."

Luceran did not turn around.

"I am not stealing the house."

"That is reassuring."

"Only the people building it."

Clavard made a noise.

They reached the great stair.

Captain Tovan Rhyse was below speaking with two palace guards.

Rhyse had known Luceran since the prince was young enough to flee tutors through laundry corridors and old enough to think himself subtle. Friendship had never made Rhyse indulgent. It merely meant he recognized the expression Luceran wore immediately before doing something everybody else would have to organize afterward.

"Your Majesty?"

Luceran descended.

"Good. Come."

Rhyse looked at the growing procession.

"Where?"

"Outside."

The captain's posture changed instantly.

"Majesty, if you intend to leave the palace I need to assemble your escort."

"Do that."

"When?"

"Now."

"How many?"

Luceran reached the vestibule.

"Enough."

Rhyse looked briefly toward the ceiling.

Then he began shouting for men.

Luceran did not wait.

---

The front gate had two ordinary guards posted inside the arch.

Sergeant Bram Tolland had held the gate often enough to know every important carriage crest in the capital and every nobleman who believed gate regulations were written for lesser families. He was thirty-six, married to Nessa, father of two daughters, and had once told Rhyse that the great virtue of gate duty was that kings generally stayed on the correct side of it.

Guardsman Edrik Saye stood opposite him.

Edrik was twenty-four, broad-shouldered, earnest, and still new enough to the royal household that he polished buckles nobody inspected. Half his pay went to his widowed mother and younger brother in the south quarter.

They both saw Luceran approaching.

Neither understood.

Bram stepped forward.

"Your Majesty."

Luceran kept walking.

Bram glanced behind him.

No carriage.

No horse.

No formed escort.

Only an expanding waterfall of courtiers, clerks, pages and advisers, with Rhyse trying to create royal security while walking.

"Majesty," Rhyse called, "wait."

Luceran reached the gate.

Bram tried again.

"Your Majesty, is there a destination?"

"Yes."

"Shall I send for a carriage?"

"No."

"A horse?"

"No."

Luceran walked beneath the arch.

Bram looked at Edrik.

Edrik looked at Bram.

Rhyse arrived three steps too late.

"You two. With him."

Bram blinked.

"Captain?"

"Now."

Luceran heard this, turned, pointed at both men and snapped his fingers.

"You. You. You're my escort. Come."

Then he kept walking.

Bram and Edrik ran.

That was how two gate guards became the king's actual personal escort.

Not by ceremony.

Not by noble recommendation.

Not through the Royal Companions Office two floors below, where the men officially appointed to guard the king were at that exact moment flicking ivory counters across a polished table into little silver cups and wagering on whose shot had touched the rim.

The Royal Companions were sons, nephews, clients and inconvenient younger brothers of respectable houses. Their official duty was to remain immediately available whenever the sovereign moved beyond secured private rooms.

Their practical duties had gradually expanded to include wearing extremely good coats, knowing which noble daughters were watching, collecting palace gossip for family correspondence, and being somewhere else whenever standing in rain might be required.

None of them knew Luceran had left the palace.

None of them knew he had left the royal grounds.

They would not discover either fact until he returned.

---

The palace square gave way to the east lane.

Luceran crossed without waiting for traffic.

Bram Tolland moved ahead of him and nearly collided with a cabbage cart.

Edrik Saye fell in on Luceran's left and began watching windows because he had been trained to watch windows whenever someone important behaved unpredictably.

Behind them came Avarren, Clavard Clifford, Brother Calven, Rhyse, four more guards Rhyse had managed to collect, two secretaries, the page, and Marshal Dame Ysolde Harrowmere, who joined after seeing the procession from an upper gallery and deciding she would regret missing whatever this was.

Montegrain caught them halfway across the square.

"What happened?"

"The king heard a hammer," Avarren said.

Montegrain looked at Luceran.

"That sounds dangerous."

"It was illuminating."

The hammering grew louder.

Lord Edmund Moncler's new capital mansion rose directly beyond the east lane, four stories of pale stone, scaffolding, half-set windows, stacked timber, pulleys, lime barrels and men who had possessed ordinary lives until approximately one minute ago.

Luceran stopped outside the site.

The foreman saw him.

The foreman's face emptied.

Luceran pointed.

"You."

The foreman removed his cap.

"Majesty."

"Come here."

He came.

"Name."

"Master Olan Crewe, Majesty."

"You are the foreman?"

"Yes."

"You have carpenters."

Olan looked toward the scaffolds.

"Yes, Majesty."

"How many?"

"On site?"

"No, hidden in the sewers. Yes, on site."

Avarren murmured, "Majesty."

Olan swallowed.

"Twenty-two carpenters and joiners today. More if you count apprentices."

Luceran looked at Clavard.

Clavard stared at the ground.

"You have masons."

"Yes, Majesty."

"How many?"

"Fifteen dressed-stone men, six foundation men, four mortar hands—"

"Fifteen is a goodly number."

Olan did not know what answer was expected.

"Yes, Majesty."

"Assemble them."

The foreman froze.

"Majesty?"

"Your carpenters. Your masons. Assemble them here."

Olan looked at Avarren.

Avarren offered the expression of a man who could provide sympathy but no rescue.

"Now?"

"Now."

Olan shouted.

Work stopped.

Men climbed down from scaffolds.

Masons laid aside hammers.

Carpenters emerged carrying saws, planes and mallets.

Apprentices crowded behind them.

Luceran waited until perhaps forty men stood in the yard.

For the first time in weeks, Moncler's construction site became quiet.

That pleased him more than it should have.

"Good."

He looked over the workers.

"You have all been building this mansion for some time."

Nobody answered.

One man nodded.

"The work is good. You are obviously skilled."

That earned several uncertain glances toward the half-finished facade.

"I require skilled carpenters and masons."

Olan Crewe's face changed.

He understood first.

Luceran raised his voice.

"Tomorrow morning, we leave for Castle Edselsto."

The workers looked at one another.

"You," Luceran said, sweeping one hand across them, "are coming with me."

Somebody in the back said, "What?"

Luceran heard him.

"To Edselsto. The ancestral royal castle is to be restored. Roofs, walls, floors, doors, windows, stairs, whatever remains to be done. You are now in royal service and part of the royal entourage."

A carpenter near the front lifted one hand halfway and seemed to regret it.

Luceran pointed.

"Yes?"

"Majesty, tomorrow?"

"Yes."

"My wife—"

"Tell her."

Another worker said, "My lord, we are contracted here."

"The Crown will settle Lord Moncler's contract."

Avarren turned slowly toward Luceran.

Clavard whispered, "Will it?"

Luceran heard him.

"Yes."

Clavard rubbed his eyes.

Olan Crewe stepped forward.

"Majesty, I must say—"

"You may say it."

"We cannot simply abandon an active build."

Luceran looked back toward the palace.

Then at Moncler's mansion.

Then at the men.

"You can."

Olan opened his mouth.

Luceran continued.

"And you will not be cheated for it. Royal field wages. Travel provision. Your families receive household allowance while you are away. If a man has an existing debt or penalty tied to this work, the Crown settles it before departure."

Clavard looked as though he could already hear Nerezza screaming.

A murmur moved through the workers.

That changed the arithmetic.

Jory Fell stood beside a half-finished casement frame he had carried down without thinking.

Derrin Voss still held a stone hammer.

Yesterday neither man had expected to know the king's voice.

Now the king was telling them they would rebuild a royal castle.

Jory raised his hand.

"What if a man cannot go, Majesty?"

The question quieted the yard.

Luceran frowned.

"Cannot?"

"Family. Illness. Something like that."

Luceran had not thought that far.

Avarren watched him.

Luceran was still angry.

He also knew what it was like to have another person's plans dropped onto his life and called duty.

"Then he speaks to Master Crewe and my clerk before sunset. I am not taking sick men away from their children."

Avarren's shoulders relaxed slightly.

Luceran pointed at the rest.

"But if your objection is that Lord Moncler's windows will be late, the windows will be late."

Someone laughed.

Then several others did.

Luceran smiled.

"North gate before dawn. Tools. Warm clothes. What you can carry. The Crown handles wagons."

Clavard whispered, "The Crown does not currently have enough wagons."

Luceran turned.

"Find them."

Clavard stared.

Luceran smiled pleasantly.

"You are an intelligent man."

Montegrain covered his mouth.

---

Rhyse did not laugh.

He was watching Bram Tolland and Edrik Saye.

Both men had adapted without instruction.

Bram had placed himself between Luceran and the open street.

Edrik was watching the scaffolds and upper windows.

Rhyse pointed at them.

"You two remain with His Majesty."

Bram said, "Until we return?"

"No."

Edrik looked over.

"Captain?"

"From now on."

Both men became very still.

Rhyse stepped closer.

"If the king is in a room, one of you knows every door."

Bram nodded.

"The other stays close enough to see him."

Edrik nodded.

"If he moves, you move."

Bram glanced at Luceran.

Luceran was asking Olan Crewe whether lime could be transported wet.

Rhyse lowered his voice.

"I will not again learn that the King of Embar-Zara has left his own palace because a gate guard sends a boy running after me."

"Understood," Bram said.

"Yes, Captain," Edrik said.

Rhyse looked between them.

"Do not lose him."

Bram watched Luceran wander toward a stack of stone without telling anybody.

"I am beginning to understand the assignment."

---

The effects of the king's visit spread faster than the king himself.

Lord Edmund Moncler learned that his building crew had been recruited while standing in his bath.

The messenger had to repeat it.

"His Majesty did what?"

"Entered the site, my lord."

"Why?"

"He required masons."

Moncler stared.

"From my house."

"Yes, my lord."

"From my scaffolding."

"Yes."

"How many?"

The messenger hesitated.

"Potentially all of them."

Moncler stood.

Water ran off him.

"Get me a robe."

His valet obeyed.

"And my carriage."

"To the palace?"

Moncler stopped.

He imagined arriving at the palace to demand that a newly bereaved king return forty tradesmen because his west gallery needed windows.

"No."

The valet waited.

Moncler swore.

"Send to the guild."

"What shall I say?"

"Say the Crown has taken contracted labor directly off a private work."

"Taken?"

Moncler considered.

"Recruited."

The valet nodded.

"And tell my steward to cover the open masonry before it rains."

By the time Moncler dressed, the story had changed twice.

The king had personally conscripted fifty men.

The king had threatened to hang a guild master.

The king had seized Moncler's entire estate.

None of those things had happened.

Moncler still had no reliable workforce.

---

Jory Fell went home before midday.

His wife Ellyn was mending a sleeve while their eldest daughter copied letters at the table.

Jory entered carrying a purse.

Ellyn looked up.

"You are early."

"The king came to the site."

She waited.

Jory placed the purse down.

"The king."

"Yes."

"Our king."

"There is only one."

"What did you do?"

"Nothing."

"Jory."

"He recruited us."

"For what?"

"Edselsto."

Their daughter stopped writing.

"Where is that?"

"North."

"How far?"

"Far enough."

"When?"

"Tomorrow morning."

Ellyn stood.

"No."

"He says we are going."

"Then tell him no."

Jory looked at her.

"You tell him."

She opened her mouth.

Closed it.

Jory pushed the purse toward her.

"The Crown pays field wage. Family allowance. More if Clifford approves the advance."

She looked inside.

Her expression changed.

Not greed.

Arithmetic.

Rent.

Winter coal.

Shoes.

Their son's apprenticeship fee.

The debt to her brother.

The roof patch they had delayed.

"How long?"

"I don't know."

"How dangerous?"

"I don't know."

"Where will you sleep?"

"I don't know."

"Will you come home?"

He looked down.

"I don't know."

There it was.

The other half of royal fortune.

Ellyn sat.

Their daughter whispered, "Are we rich?"

"No," both parents said.

A moment later Ellyn started laughing.

Jory stared.

"What?"

"You were fitting Lord Moncler's windows this morning."

"I know."

"Tomorrow you are rebuilding the king's ancestral castle."

"I know."

"That is the stupidest thing I have ever heard."

"I know."

He started laughing too.

Then neither of them did.

---

Derrin Voss had no wife.

He had a mother.

This was worse.

She listened in complete silence while he explained that the king had walked onto the site and declared the crew royal.

When he finished, she slapped him.

Derrin held his cheek.

"What was that for?"

"Standing close enough to a king to be noticed."

"I was working."

"Work farther away."

"Mother."

She took the advance.

Counted it.

Counted it again.

"This is too much."

"It is royal field pay."

"Why?"

"Because His Majesty is angry."

"Never take money set by an angry king."

"That is not a proverb."

"It is now."

His sister leaned through the doorway.

"Are you really going to Edselsto?"

"Tomorrow."

"Can you take Tavin?"

"No."

"He needs a trade."

"I am not taking your son to a ruined mountain castle because the king had a tantrum about carpenters."

His mother pointed at him.

"That. Remember you said that. Never say it near anyone important."

---

By early afternoon, the Guild of Stone and Beam discovered that skilled labor could, in fact, be found.

Three guild officers arrived at the palace carrying lists.

Crews.

Names.

Rates.

Existing obligations which apparently could be rearranged after all.

The Master of Royal Works, who had spent the morning explaining why no properly chartered team could depart before the following week, now stood before Avarren insisting that unregistered royal recruitment threatened the integrity of Crown construction.

Avarren let him finish.

Then asked:

"How many masons can you provide by dawn?"

The master hesitated.

"Twenty-four."

"Carpenters?"

"Seventeen."

"Roofers?"

"Eight."

Avarren looked at the clerk beside him.

"Write that down."

"With proper royal contracts."

"Of course."

"And guild rates."

"Discuss them with Nerezza."

The master paled.

Avarren almost smiled.

"His Majesty appears to have found the remaining labor himself."

---

Luceran returned to the palace pleased with himself.

This was unfortunate.

Montegrain walked beside him.

"You understand that you did not solve the problem."

"I found carpenters."

"You found a construction site."

"Which contained carpenters."

"You also made every guild in the city terrified you will continue walking around."

"Good."

"That is one interpretation."

They reached the gate.

Bram and Edrik entered with them.

Rhyse stopped.

He looked toward the stairwell leading down to the Royal Companions' barracks.

Then at the two ordinary guards who had spent the last hour actually guarding the king.

Something in his face became very calm.

Montegrain saw it.

"Oh," he said.

Luceran looked over.

"What?"

Rhyse said, "Majesty, would you come downstairs with me?"

"Why?"

"I believe I have discovered another labor surplus."

---

The Royal Companions discovered the king had left when the king returned.

Nine of the fourteen men assigned to immediate royal escort were in the lower barracks.

Four were gathered around the gaming table.

Three more had chairs pulled close.

Two were arguing over whether an ivory counter had struck the rim of a silver cup before falling in.

A bottle of wine stood open.

Nobody was wearing a sword belt.

Rhyse entered first.

The room went silent.

Lord Pell's youngest nephew stood.

"Captain."

Rhyse looked at the table.

Then at the men.

Then back at the table.

"Where is the king?"

Nobody answered.

One of them finally said, "In the palace, Captain."

Luceran stepped into the doorway.

"No."

Every face changed.

Rhyse did not turn around.

"His Majesty crossed the outer gate, crossed the public square, entered Lord Edmund Moncler's construction site, addressed approximately forty tradesmen, and returned."

Silence.

"You did not know he left."

No answer.

"You did not know he left the building."

A younger companion said, "Captain, our rotation—"

Rhyse looked at him.

The explanation died.

Another tried.

"My lord father was told this appointment—"

Rhyse's voice sharpened.

"Your appointment was to the king."

That silenced the room.

"Not to your father. Not to House Pell. Not to Valcere. Not to whichever aunt, uncle, creditor or patron secured you a handsome coat and a place close enough to hear royal conversation. To the king."

One man looked at Luceran.

"Majesty, surely this is—"

"No," Luceran said.

He surprised himself with how easy it came.

The man stopped.

Luceran looked across the room.

These men had been assigned to him.

Some he knew by name.

Some had danced at the coronation.

At least three had privately offered condolences after his parents died.

Several almost certainly sent letters home about who visited him, what he said, whether he looked tired, who he trusted.

He had resented them for that.

Now Rhyse had given him something cleaner than resentment.

Failure.

Gross, obvious, professional failure.

Rhyse said, "Nine men absent from immediate escort duty without authorization. Five additional appointments will be reviewed individually. Those genuinely assigned to messages, gate rotations or other lawful posts will retain service pending my judgment."

Lord Pell's nephew went pale.

"Captain—"

"You nine are dismissed from the Royal Companions effective immediately for gross negligence of duty."

The room exploded.

"My father—"

"House Valcere will—"

"You cannot—"

"My commission bears—"

Rhyse raised his voice.

"I can."

Silence fell by force.

"I am Captain of the Royal Guard. The king left the palace, left the grounds, walked into the public city and returned before his appointed close escort discovered he was gone."

Nobody had a useful answer to that.

Rhyse pointed toward the door.

"If your patrons wish to argue that this demonstrates satisfactory performance, I invite them to do so in writing."

Montegrain turned his face away.

Luceran knew that expression.

He was trying not to laugh.

The dismissed companions began removing badges.

One did so angrily.

One looked humiliated.

One looked genuinely frightened of going home.

That mattered.

They were not all villains.

Some were merely spoiled.

Some were politically placed.

Some had been trained to believe the post was ceremonial because everyone before them had treated it that way.

Their negligence was still real.

Rhyse turned to Bram and Edrik.

Both men had remained by the door because nobody had told them to leave.

"Sergeant Tolland."

"Captain."

"Guardsman Saye."

"Captain."

"You followed the king when ordered?"

"Yes, Captain."

"You remained with him?"

"Yes."

"You kept him in sight?"

Bram glanced at Luceran.

"With effort."

Montegrain lost the fight and laughed.

Rhyse ignored him.

"Effective now, both of you are assigned to the King's Close Escort."

Edrik stared.

Bram said, "Captain?"

"You heard me."

Bram looked at the silk-coated noblemen packing their belongings.

Then at his own gate-duty uniform.

"This seems irregular."

Rhyse's mouth twitched.

"Today has been irregular."

Luceran stepped closer.

He looked at the two guards who had become his escorts because they had happened to be standing where duty required them when he did something stupid.

"You two."

Both straightened.

Luceran pointed at himself.

"If I leave a room—"

"We go with you," Bram said.

Luceran blinked.

Edrik added, "Immediately, Majesty."

Rhyse nodded.

"Good. We are learning."

Luceran looked at the dismissed Companions.

Then back at Bram and Edrik.

For the first time since deciding to leave the capital, he felt that something inside the palace had actually changed rather than merely been promised.

Not because he had planned it.

Because he had walked out a gate.

Outside, somewhere across the lane, Moncler's hammers had started again with fewer hands.

Inside, nine noble households were about to receive very unpleasant news.

Rhyse began collecting the dismissed badges.

That was where the old royal escort ended.

# Episode 3 — The Road North

At eleven minutes before midnight, the palace had run out of wagons.

It had not run out of things.

Things were everywhere.

Chests stood open beneath the western arcade. Rolled carpets leaned against pillars. Two clerks argued over a locked iron dispatch box while a third sat on it to prevent a porter from taking it away. Kitchen boys carried sacks of flour toward the courtyard and returned carrying the same sacks back because somebody had decided flour belonged in the second train, not the first. A physician had commandeered half a cart for medicines and was defending it with the expression of a man prepared to diagnose anyone who objected with a broken nose.

The horses knew something terrible was happening and had elected to contribute by shitting everywhere.

Master Clavard Clifford stood in the middle of it with three ledgers, two exhausted scribes, and no remaining patience.

"Whose wagon is that?"

A carter looked behind him.

"Mine."

"It was yours. Whose warrant?"

"Red seal."

"There are four red seals in use tonight."

"Then one of those."

Clavard closed his eyes.

"Wonderful."

The carter waited.

"Do I load the chairs?"

"No."

"They told me chairs."

"Who?"

The carter pointed into the palace.

That narrowed the matter to several hundred people.

Clavard looked at the chairs. They were carved walnut, upholstered in blue velvet, and entirely capable of remaining in the capital without threatening the continuity of government.

"Unload them."

A household clerk appeared at his shoulder.

"Master Clifford, the Keeper of the Wardrobes says Her late Majesty's winter trunks must travel in the first train."

Clavard looked at him.

"Her late Majesty is dead."

The clerk's face tightened.

Clavard regretted the sentence immediately.

"I know," he said, quieter. "Second train. Seal the trunks. Put them under guard. Nothing is to be discarded."

"Yes, master."

"And find out who ordered chairs."

The clerk disappeared.

Across the courtyard, a man began shouting about oats.

Clavard opened the first ledger again.

The king had said dawn.

Dawn, Clavard was learning, was not a time of day. It was a weapon.

---

Lord Edmund Moncler arrived at the palace shortly after midnight wearing an excellent coat over clothing that had plainly been put on in anger.

He did not ask to see the king.

That was the first sensible decision anyone had made in an hour.

He asked for Clavard.

"My lord."

"Master Clifford."

They regarded one another across a writing table that had been dragged into a side gallery because every proper office between there and the treasury was full of crates.

Moncler placed a folded contract on the table.

"Forty-one men."

"Thirty-eight have reported. Three claimed hardship."

"Forty-one contracted."

"Yes."

"Two months of dressed stone already purchased. Timber cut to measurements for work that will now stop. Lime mixed. Scaffold hire. Crane hire. Lodging for six country masons. Penalties if the west roof is not enclosed before the rains."

Clavard nodded.

"I have not disputed your loss."

"His Majesty took my workmen out of my house."

"Your unfinished house."

"That distinction is not as comforting as you seem to think."

Clavard almost smiled.

Almost.

Moncler leaned forward.

"I will not be made the villain because the king heard a hammer and discovered economics."

"I don't intend to make you one."

That stopped him.

Clavard turned the contract around.

"Your direct losses will be assessed. Wages already advanced by you against unfinished work will be credited. Perishable materials reasonably lost because of the interruption will be compensated. Existing penalties caused solely by Crown recruitment will be suspended where the Crown has authority to suspend them."

Moncler studied him.

"And where it does not?"

"Bring me the claims."

"You are offering full compensation?"

"I am offering an accounting. Those are not the same thing."

Moncler sat back.

There was the man he knew.

"And my labor?"

"Gone."

"All of it?"

"Most."

"For how long?"

Clavard rubbed the bridge of his nose.

"I do not know."

"Then you cannot calculate my loss."

"Correct."

"This is absurd."

"Yes."

Moncler had prepared himself for denial, hauteur, perhaps a lecture about royal necessity. Agreement left him with nowhere satisfying to put his anger.

"I want that in writing."

Clavard gestured toward one of the scribes.

"So do I."

The scribe began copying.

Moncler looked through the gallery arch toward the courtyard.

"Is he truly leaving at dawn?"

"Yes."

"Why?"

Clavard looked at the wagons.

"Because he said he would."

Moncler gave a short laugh without humor.

"That is going to become expensive."

"It already has."

---

Two floors below the gallery, Captain Tovan Rhyse was being told that he had insulted House Pell.

The speaker was Lord Cassian Pell, uncle to one of the dismissed Royal Companions and a man with the unfortunate gift of sounding reasonable while furious.

"My nephew's failure is not in dispute."

Rhyse folded his arms.

"Good."

"Do not enjoy this too much, Tovan."

"I haven't slept. Enjoyment is beyond me."

Cassian ignored that.

"My objection is to what follows. You dismissed nine men in one stroke and replaced their function with two gate guards."

"I replaced nobody's function with two gate guards. I assigned two men to stay close to the king because they demonstrated they could do it."

"Sergeant Tolland has never served close escort."

"He has now."

"For an hour."

"During which he knew where the king was."

Cassian's jaw tightened.

Rhyse let the silence sit.

The facts were vulgar things. They had no respect for pedigree.

"You know what this looks like," Cassian said.

"Yes."

"Do you? Nine noble appointments removed immediately after the king announces he is abandoning the capital. Men whose families have voices in half the provincial courts."

"Nine guards dismissed after losing the king."

"The king walked out."

"Kings do that."

"Without notice."

"Enemies are famous for providing notice."

Cassian exhaled through his nose.

"And the other five?"

"Two were on authorized message duty. One was at the north gate because I put him there. One was sick and had reported it. One was in the armory collecting equipment for the departure. They keep their commissions pending review."

Cassian stared at him.

That was worse politically, because it was fair.

"My nephew will appeal."

"He may."

"To the king."

"He may."

"And if the king restores him?"

Rhyse shrugged.

"Then I will put him somewhere he can learn to count sovereigns."

Cassian laughed despite himself.

It lasted only a moment.

"You have made enemies tonight."

"No. I have identified paperwork attached to enemies I already had."

"That sounds like Avarren talking."

"Don't insult me."

Cassian turned to leave, then stopped.

"Tovan."

"What?"

"My nephew is a fool. He is not a traitor."

Rhyse's expression changed slightly.

"I know."

"I intend to."

Cassian left.

"Captain."

"Courier from Edselsto."

Rhyse straightened.

"Alive?"

"Take him to the king."

Bram Tolland's wife did not slap him.

He considered this a promising beginning.

Nessa stood in their kitchen with both hands on the table and looked at the close-escort baldric he had been issued less than an hour earlier.

Their daughters had been sent back to bed and were, judging by the silence above, listening from the stairs.

"Say it again."

"The captain assigned me to the king."

"Why?"

"Because the king left."

"Left what?"

"The palace."

"And?"

"I followed him."

Nessa stared.

"That is the reason?"

"Apparently it distinguished me."

Her mouth twitched.

Bram put a small purse on the table.

"There will be an allowance while I'm away. Rhyse says close duty carries extra pay."

She did not touch it.

"How long?"

"I don't know."

"Where will you sleep?"

"I don't know."

"How often will you come home?"

"I don't know."

"You have been promoted into knowing considerably less than you did this morning."

"That is also my assessment."

Nessa finally took the purse.

"Do you want it?"

Bram looked at the baldric.

That was harder.

"I want the pay."

"Bram."

"I want Rhyse to have men near the king who think guarding him is work instead of jewelry."

She waited.

"And?"

He sighed.

"And when he pointed at me, I went."

Nessa knew him well enough not to ask which part mattered most.

She came around the table and fixed the strap where he had buckled it twisted.

"Then don't become jewelry."

"I will strive against it."

From the stairs came a small voice.

"Does the king run away a lot?"

Bram looked toward the darkness.

"Go to bed."

"Does he?"

Nessa covered her mouth.

Bram considered the question.

"Apparently."

Edrik Saye's mother did slap him.

Not hard.

It was more of an editorial comment.

"Ow."

"You are going where?"

"Edselsto."

"With whom?"

"The king."

She slapped him again.

"Stop that."

"Stop saying foolish things."

His younger brother Len was laughing into his sleeve.

Edrik pointed at him.

"You are paying for coal while I'm gone."

Len stopped laughing.

Their mother looked at the new badge.

"Did you ask for this?"

"No."

"Did you do something heroic?"

"I walked quickly."

Len laughed again.

Edrik packed two shirts, wool stockings, a whetstone, sewing kit, spare bootlaces and the good gloves his mother had bought him the previous winter.

She removed the gloves and replaced them with an older pair.

"Those are for church."

"I am guarding the king."

"Is he a church?"

"No."

"Then old gloves."

He did not argue.

Before he left, she caught his sleeve.

"Listen to your sergeant."

"I do."

"Listen to your captain."

"I do."

She looked at him until he understood there was another instruction coming.

"And if the king tells you to do something stupid?"

Edrik thought of Luceran stepping into traffic.

"Stay close enough to stop somebody else killing him while he does it."

His mother nodded.

"Good."

Jory Fell did not sleep.

Neither did Ellyn.

They packed his tools on the floor because the tool chest was too heavy to carry full and because every object became an argument about what counted as necessary.

"Two planes."

"Three."

"You have two hands."

"They do different things."

"Then grow another hand."

Jory wrapped the third plane.

Their daughter had fallen asleep at the table with her head on her arms.

Ellyn watched him tie the bundle.

"You can still claim hardship."

"For what?"

"Us."

He looked at her.

"We are not hardship."

"I didn't mean—"

"I know."

She laughed.

"Come home."

"I intend to."

The courier from Edselsto arrived in Luceran's private chamber while the king was arguing with Avarren about books.

"The law library travels."

"A selection travels."

"All of it."

"Majesty, unless you intend to eat precedents, we need the wagons for food."

A knock interrupted them.

Rhyse entered with a road-stained soldier whose boots were grey to the ankle.

Luceran forgot the books.

"From Edran?"

The courier bowed and held out a sealed packet.

"Yes, Majesty. Sir Edran Vey sends report and asks that I say the road above Kaelmont's lower toll is passable for wagons if taken slowly. One culvert is damaged. He has men marking the edge."

Luceran broke the seal.

Edran's handwriting was compact and ugly.

The report was not romantic.

That was reassuring.

The lower stable roof held. The upper stable did not. Two kitchens could be made usable; one chimney needed clearing before fire. The main well produced water after debris was hauled from the housing, but Edran wanted it boiled until Mara Kest was satisfied with the source. The great hall was dry except along the eastern wall. Several chambers in the inhabited wing were sound enough for bedding. The west roof leaked badly. The south gallery was closed because the floor had failed in two places. Rats were abundant. Firewood was available. The village could provide eggs, goats, rough bread, some preserved meat, labor, and more opinions than Edran had requested.

The elderly steward was alive.

His name was Orven Dast.

He had received the royal seal, looked at Edran for a long time, and said, according to the report, *About bloody time.*

Luceran read that line twice.

Avarren leaned over.

"Good steward?"

"Apparently."

"Castle?"

Luceran continued.

Several interior doors were swollen shut. Others had been locked long enough that nobody currently present had keys. Dast insisted some family rooms were not to be forced before the king arrived because they contained dynastic property. Edran had respected the instruction because nothing behind them was required for immediate habitation.

No army occupied the castle.

No rival banner flew above it.

No obvious ambush waited on the approach.

It was, in short, a ruin with a stubborn old man in it.

Luceran smiled.

Rhyse noticed.

"What?"

"It exists."

Avarren looked at the report.

"That was not seriously in doubt."

"Not the point."

Luceran handed it over.

For days Edselsto had been an argument. A name. A childhood painting. A place used by older men to explain why something could not be done quickly.

Now there were rats.

Rats were excellent.

Rats were real.

"We leave at dawn."

Avarren read the line about the culvert.

"We were already leaving at dawn."

"Now we know where we're going."

Rhyse said, "We knew where we were going."

Luceran looked between them.

"You are both determined to ruin this for me."

"Yes," said Avarren.

"Professionally," said Rhyse.

Dawn found the old capital awake and furious.

The first train occupied the avenue from the palace gate to the lower market.

There were fewer wagons than Luceran had imagined and more than Clavard had believed possible.

The Carters' Union had produced them at prices that made Clavard briefly religious.

House Damaris supplied two grain carts after receiving a sealed assurance that the loads would be paid at emergency carriage rates rather than requisitioned. House Bellisar loaned remount horses with a written condition that royal grooms, not household amateurs, handle them. House Nerezza sent a revenue clerk to witness every emergency expenditure and thereby made itself hated by everyone while preventing at least three obvious attempts at theft.

The Royal Kitchens Compact had packed bread, hard cheese, smoked meat, dried peas, salt, onions, ale, vinegar, and enough pots to start a small war over cookware.

The physicians lost half their wagon and won it back.

The chairs remained behind.

Clavard considered this his greatest victory.

Luceran came through the gate on horseback.

Bram rode to his right.

Edrik rode to his left.

Neither looked ornamental.

That, more than the new badges, was noticed.

So were the absences.

Windows along the avenue held faces. Noble servants stood beside merchants. Apprentices had climbed onto roofs. Children sat on walls. Men who had been dismissed from the Royal Companions did not appear in uniform, but their families' colors appeared often enough among the watchers.

Luceran felt the attention like weather.

Montegrain brought his horse alongside.

"Still think you are escaping politics?"

Luceran looked at him.

"I am escaping these politics."

"Ah."

"What?"

"Nothing. I look forward to meeting the new politics."

Luceran smiled despite himself.

Behind them, Olan Crewe shouted at a wagon driver who had placed chisels beneath bedding.

Jory Fell walked beside the tool cart until Ellyn pushed through the crowd and caught his hand.

The column slowed around them.

Jory looked mortified.

Ellyn did not care.

She kissed him hard enough to earn cheers from three carpenters and a scandalized noise from somebody's aunt.

"Come home," she said.

"Yes."

"Write."

"Yes."

"Don't let them pay you in promises."

Jory glanced toward Clavard.

"That one I think I can manage."

Derrin Voss's mother did not push through the crowd.

She stood on a step and watched him go with both hands wrapped around the purse containing his advance.

When he looked back, she raised one finger at him.

Derrin knew exactly what it meant.

Do not be stupid near important people.

He raised one finger in return.

His sister laughed.

Rhyse rode down the line once, then again.

The king had an escort now.

The convoy had guards.

Those were different problems.

At the rear gate of the palace, Lord Cassian Pell watched from beneath an awning.

His nephew stood beside him in ordinary clothes.

"You should have been there," Cassian said.

The young man looked at the column.

"I know."

It was the first useful thing he had said about the matter.

---

The capital did not end cleanly.

It thinned.

Stone houses became timber. Shops became yards. Yards became gardens. Gardens became fields wet with morning mist. The road widened where carts had worn the verges down and narrowed again where old walls refused to move for newer traffic.

Luceran had traveled royal roads all his life.

He had rarely traveled them at the speed of a loaded wagon.

This was educational.

By midmorning, one wheel had cracked.

By noon, a draft horse had thrown a shoe.

By early afternoon, the column had stretched so far that Luceran could not see the rear from the front.

He learned that a royal convoy did not move at the speed of the king.

It moved at the speed of the slowest necessary thing.

Today that thing was a wagon carrying archive chests and vinegar.

"Leave it," Luceran said.

Clavard stared at him.

"Which?"

"The wagon. Transfer the important things."

"Define important."

Luceran opened his mouth.

Clavard waited.

The king looked at the chests.

Royal correspondence. Tax rolls. Household warrants. Court seals. Property records. Petitions. Some could be replaced. Some could not. The vinegar was for food preservation and cleaning. The wagon itself belonged to a carter who expected it returned.

"Fine," Luceran said.

Clavard pointed at him.

"Do not walk anywhere."

Montegrain laughed from his horse.

Bram coughed into his fist.

Luceran dismounted.

They repaired the wheel.

It took forty-three minutes.

Nobody died.

The kingdom survived.

Luceran hated how instructive this was.

---

At the damaged culvert below Kaelmont's lower toll, the road narrowed to a single safe wheel track.

House Kaelmont's men had already set stakes along the broken edge.

A toll reeve waited beside them with three laborers and the expression of a man expecting to be blamed for geology.

Luceran looked down into the washout.

"How long?"

"To repair properly? Three days if stone arrives. Five if it rains."

"To get us across?"

"You can cross now. Slowly. One wagon at a time. No passing. Keep riders off the outer edge."

Luceran looked at the length of the column behind him.

"How long?"

The reeve considered.

"All of you?"

"Yes."

"Hours."

Luceran inhaled.

The reeve added, "Majesty, the road does not know you are king."

Silence.

Bram looked very carefully at the horizon.

Edrik's face became a study in duty.

Luceran stared at the reeve.

Then he laughed.

"Apparently nobody told it."

The reeve relaxed by a fraction.

"No, Majesty."

"Can we help?"

That surprised him.

"With the crossing? Yes. Men at the wheels. Keep the teams straight."

Olan Crewe was already climbing down from the tool wagon.

"Jory! Derrin!"

The workers came.

So did soldiers.

So did carters who had no interest in watching their livelihoods roll into a ditch.

The crossing took two hours and nine minutes.

The last wagon cleared before the first drops of rain.

The reeve watched it go and made a note in his toll book that the king had passed north with more wagons than sense.

He later crossed out the last three words.

Not because they were false.

Because toll books survived people.

---

The rain followed them into the hills.

It was not dramatic rain.

It was worse.

It was steady, cold, and competent.

Cloaks darkened. Wheels hissed. Horses lowered their heads. The road became a long brown argument.

Luceran's enthusiasm survived longer than anyone expected.

Then water ran down the back of his neck.

Montegrain saw it happen.

"Majesty?"

"Don't."

"I said nothing."

"You were about to."

"I was going to ask whether Edselsto has weather."

Luceran pulled his hood tighter.

"You may return to the capital."

"And miss the new politics? Never."

Bram rode ahead to check a bend.

Edrik stayed close.

The pattern had formed without ceremony.

When Bram returned, Edrik moved outward. When the road narrowed, one watched the king and one watched everyone else. At halts they argued quietly about doors that did not exist yet, because Rhyse's instruction had been clear and both men were taking it literally.

Luceran noticed.

He also noticed that neither asked him whether he wanted them there.

Oddly, that made their presence easier.

They were not pretending intimacy.

They were working.

---

Near evening the rain weakened.

The road climbed through darker country.

Pines replaced broad fields. Exposed roots gripped black stone beside the road. Farms became smaller and farther apart. Smoke rose from valleys rather than chimneys beside the highway.

The convoy rounded a long shoulder of rock.

Then everyone at the front stopped.

Luceran saw the mountain first.

Not because he was king.

Because he happened to be looking up.

It rose beyond the next valley, dark against the clearing western sky. Basalt and granite broke through the trees in long black shelves. Pines crowded its lower slopes. The road could be seen in pieces, climbing toward it and vanishing again.

Castle Edselsto stood above them.

From this distance it was less a palace than a wound in the skyline.

Towers rose unevenly from the ridge. One roofline sagged. Another caught the last pale light. The outer walls followed the mountain instead of correcting it, stepping across black stone as though the fortress had grown where the rock permitted.

No banners were visible at this distance.

No windows shone.

No miracle announced the king's return.

It was simply there.

Old.

Large.

Inconvenient.

Real.

Luceran reined in.

Behind him the convoy slowed by degrees as people realized the front had stopped.

Jory Fell came around the side of a wagon, saw the castle, and said something obscene.

Derrin Voss looked at the amount of visible stone and began laughing.

Olan Crewe did not laugh.

He was estimating scaffolding.

Clavard was estimating money.

Rhyse was estimating walls, approaches, fields of fire, blind ground, and how many men he did not have.

Montegrain looked at Luceran.

Luceran looked only at Edselsto.

He had imagined relief.

What he felt was something stranger.

The capital was behind him.

His parents were still dead.

The houses had not ceased wanting things. The guilds had not ceased counting. The dismissed guards had not ceased having families. Moncler's unfinished mansion had not repaired itself because the king had ridden north. Every wagon behind him contained evidence that politics had packed efficiently.

But Edselsto existed.

For the first time since he had given the order, he could point at the thing he had chosen.

"There," he said.

Nobody needed him to explain what he meant.

The road descended into the valley before beginning its final climb.

At the foot of the mountain, smoke rose from the nearly abandoned village.

Somewhere up there, an elderly steward who had waited through several reigns had apparently decided the king was late.

Luceran smiled.

"Come on."

Bram looked at the failing light.

"Majesty, the road is wet."

Luceran looked at him.

Bram did not move.

Edrik watched the mountain.

"And we don't know the upper road."

Luceran looked from one new escort to the other.

Yesterday, nine noblemen had failed to notice him leave a building.

Today, two ordinary guards were telling him not to charge up a mountain at dusk because it was stupid.

He considered being offended.

Then he looked at the wagons.

"Village first?"

Bram nodded.

"Village first."

Luceran turned his horse toward the descending road.

The convoy followed.

Edselsto remained above them, dark against the evening sky.

Seven candles burned somewhere inside it.

Luceran did not know that.

Not yet.

# Episode 4 — First Night

The village of Edselsto had three streets.

Luceran had expected more.

That was his first disappointment.

His second was that one of the streets had mostly become grass.

The third was that the people waiting in the rain did not look remotely impressed by him.

They stood beneath roofs darkened by weather, outside low stone houses with patched shutters and smoke crawling from crooked chimneys. A cooper, two goat keepers, several children, an old woman with a basket, men carrying axes, women with aprons pulled over their heads, one priest, one boy holding a chicken, and a black dog with the kind of expression normally reserved for tax collectors.

The royal convoy slowed.

Luceran looked up the mountain.

Edselsto Castle stood above the village in black stone and wet pine, too large for the settlement beneath it and too old for everything around it. Towers broke the ridge line at different heights. One appeared sound. Another leaned enough to make Luceran suspicious of perspective. Slate roofs stepped down toward the eastern wall. The western side was mostly shadow and scaffolding that had not existed three days earlier.

A single banner hung above the gate.

Royal black and silver.

It looked absurdly new.

Montegrain rode beside him.

"Home."

Luceran looked at the castle.

"Do not say it like that."

"Like what?"

"Like you expect something to happen."

"I expect you to become wet."

"I am already wet."

"Then the prophecy begins."

Bram Tolland, riding ahead, glanced back.

"Road narrows after the mill, Majesty."

"How badly?"

"Two wagons abreast until the shrine. One after."

Luceran looked at Edrik.

"And if I say we push all of them up tonight?"

Edrik considered.

"I would ask Sergeant Tolland to answer."

"Traitor."

"Learning delegation, Majesty."

Bram heard and smiled without turning.

That pleased Luceran more than it should have.

The convoy entered the village.

No trumpets sounded.

No bells had been arranged.

A girl of perhaps nine saw Luceran looking at her and curtseyed several seconds too late, with so much concentration that she nearly stepped into a drainage ditch.

Luceran nodded to her.

She looked astonished that it worked.

Then an old man stepped into the road.

He wore a dark wool coat that had been repaired so many times it had become an argument between fabrics. His hair was white, his shoulders still broad, and he carried a ring of keys heavy enough to be useful as a weapon.

Sir Edran Vey stood beside him.

Mara Kest stood three paces back under a cloak, looking tired enough to murder civilization.

Luceran dismounted.

The old man did not kneel.

Everyone noticed.

He bowed.

Barely.

"Your Majesty."

Luceran looked at him.

"Orven Dast."

"Unfortunately."

Montegrain coughed.

Luceran almost smiled.

"Sir Edran wrote that you said it was about bloody time."

Orven looked at Edran.

Edran had the decency not to look ashamed.

"I said worse."

"Good."

Orven studied Luceran more openly than any courtier in the capital would have dared.

Not insolently.

Professionally.

As though deciding whether a roof repair had been done properly.

"You look like your mother around the eyes."

Luceran's amusement vanished.

Orven saw it.

He did not apologize.

"She hated this place," he said.

Luceran blinked.

"What?"

"First time she came, rained for six days. Her boots split. She told your father no sane person built a house on a mountain."

Montegrain looked away.

Luceran swallowed.

"That sounds like her."

"It was."

A beat.

Then Orven turned toward the mountain.

"You can mourn later. Horses need cover."

That was how the king of Embar-Zara was welcomed to his ancestral home.

---

The village did not have enough stable space.

The castle did.

This would have been useful if the upper stable roof had not partly failed.

Edran had therefore turned the village green into an argument involving ropes, wagons, feed sacks, three temporary picket lines, two mule teams, and a furious Bellisar groom who insisted the loaned remounts were not to spend the night standing in mud.

Household hierarchy collapsed under rain.

The royal physician carried a crate because nobody else had hands.

A treasury clerk held a lantern for a carpenter.

Brother Calven helped unload onions.

Bram stood at Luceran's shoulder until Rhyse arrived and said, "Go ahead with the first guard file."

Bram nodded.

Edrik remained.

Luceran noticed.

"You are not going?"

"One of us stays where you are."

"Rhyse's rule?"

"Ours now."

Luceran looked at him.

There was something restful about being guarded by men who treated him as a moving object with blood inside it.

No compliments.

No whispered offers.

No questions about how he was sleeping.

Only position.

Orven barked at a wagon driver who had turned too sharply beside the old stone trough.

"Not there."

The driver shouted back, "Why?"

"Because underneath that mud is a drain built before your grandmother's grandmother and if you crack it, the lower kitchen floods."

The driver moved.

Luceran looked at Orven.

"You know every drain?"

"No."

"How many?"

"Enough to resent the ones I don't."

Mara Kest appeared at Luceran's elbow.

"Majesty."

"Mara."

She looked him over the way housekeepers inspected damaged furniture.

"You brought too many people."

Luceran stared.

"That has been said."

"You brought three wagons of things nobody needs tonight."

"Which?"

"Silver service."

Luceran looked toward the convoy.

"I did not order silver service."

"Nobody ever does. It reproduces."

"Can we leave it in the wagon?"

"Already did."

"Then why tell me?"

"So you know the household is still diseased."

Luceran laughed.

Mara did not.

"I am serious."

"I know."

She pointed up the road.

"Kitchen one is usable. Kitchen two will be usable after Corren stops the chimney from trying to kill us. Main well works. We are boiling everything. East sleeping rooms are dry enough. Great hall will take overflow if people accept blankets and dignity loss."

"West wing?"

"No."

"South gallery?"

"No."

"Family rooms?"

Orven, three paces away, turned his head.

Mara noticed.

"Ask him."

Luceran did.

Orven jingled the keys.

"Locked."

"All of them?"

"Most."

"Why?"

"Because nobody lived there."

"That is not an answer."

"It is the reason."

Luceran looked at the castle again.

Rain ran black down its walls.

"Can they be opened?"

"Some."

"And the rest?"

"With axes."

"I brought carpenters."

Orven looked toward the wagons containing half of Lord Moncler's construction site.

"So I heard."

There was no admiration in it.

Only judgment deferred.

---

The upper road began at the old mill.

One wagon at a time.

No argument.

Even Luceran did not argue after seeing it.

The road cut back across the slope through black pines, their roots forcing old paving stones upward in long ridges. Water ran down the wheel tracks. Moss filled the edges. The mountain smelled of wet bark, earth, iron, smoke, and something mineral Luceran could not name.

He rode near the front.

Edrik stayed close.

Bram ranged ahead with Rhyse and a handful of guards.

Behind came workers, household staff, food carts, archive chests, tools, bedding, medicine, treasury coin, and the things monarchy insisted upon even while pretending to travel light.

The village shrank below.

Luceran looked back.

More wagons waited in the dusk.

The first train had not even reached the gate and already Edselsto had traffic.

Montegrain caught his look.

"Your empty mountain seems crowded."

"Go away."

"You brought me."

"That was before you became irritating."

"I was irritating when we met."

"Then I was naïve."

"Progress."

Luceran almost told him to shut up.

Then the gatehouse appeared.

It was not majestic.

It was enormous.

Those were different things.

The outer walls had been built from dark stone fitted so tightly that the joints were difficult to see even under lichen. The gate towers rose square and blunt rather than graceful. Murder holes opened over the passage. Rust stained the chains where an old portcullis mechanism had been exposed for repair.

Scaffolding clung to one side.

Men from Edran's advance party had erected lanterns.

The royal banner hung above all of it like an apology.

Orven stopped beneath the arch.

Luceran rode through.

Nothing happened.

That annoyed him.

He had not expected anything to happen.

He was annoyed anyway.

---

The first courtyard was larger than the palace square in the capital and far less useful.

Grass grew between stones.

A disused fountain stood dry in the center, filled with pine needles and a dead branch.

One side housed the stable complex.

Another had workshops.

The main keep rose beyond, connected to lower halls and later additions built in different eras by people who had apparently disliked one another's sense of proportion.

Master Olan Crewe stopped dead.

Jory Fell nearly walked into him.

"What?"

Olan stared upward.

"That roof."

Jory looked.

"What about it?"

"It is lying."

Derrin Voss came up beside them.

"Roofs do that?"

"This one is pretending it is straight."

Derrin squinted.

"Ah."

Luceran overheard.

"What does that mean?"

Olan did not seem pleased to be asked.

"Means the line is wrong, Majesty."

"I can see that."

"No. The wall is wrong too."

He pointed.

"See the third window from the corner?"

Luceran did not.

He said so.

Olan led him several steps.

"Now."

The upper line sagged just enough to become visible.

Jory said, "Beam could be gone."

Derrin said, "Stone has shifted."

Iven Marr, the carpenter from the advance party, came out of a side door.

"Both."

Olan looked at him.

Iven looked back.

The two carpenters had the immediate hostility of craftsmen who had not yet established which of them was entitled to be right first.

"You went inside?" Olan asked.

"Three days ago."

"And?"

"Bad principal timber. Worse patching. Somebody put decorative casing over rot fifteen years ago and called it maintenance."

Orven said, "Twenty-two."

Iven turned.

"What?"

"Twenty-two years ago."

"You remember?"

"I paid for it."

"Why?"

"Because the Crown sent a man with a letter saying repair funds would arrive the following spring."

Luceran looked at him.

"Did they?"

Orven looked at Luceran.

"No."

That landed.

No accusation.

No performance.

Just twenty-two years.

Luceran looked at the roof again.

His family had owned this place the entire time.

Someone had written letters.

Someone had promised money.

The old man had paid for a rotten beam to be hidden because waiting for the Crown had stopped being practical.

Luceran said, "Replace it properly."

Olan nodded.

Orven said, "With what timber?"

# Episode 6 — The Price Of A Door

The locksmith arrived with eleven locks and nowhere to sleep.

This was, Luceran thought, a remarkably Edselsto way to begin the morning.

The woman standing in the lower courtyard was perhaps forty, broad through the shoulders, grey beginning at both temples, with a leather roll of tools under one arm and a brass badge shaped like a key pinned to her coat. Behind her stood two apprentices, a mule, three narrow crates, and a carter who had already decided the Crown owed him money.

Sera Morcant stood beside the crates with black on both hands.

"You sent for her?" Luceran asked.

Sera shook her head.

"No."

The locksmith bowed.

"Nella Veyr, Guild of Keys, Majesty. Master doorwright, third ward of the capital charter, licensed for treasury boxes, estate strongrooms, prison work, and noble household service."

"Who sent for you?"

"Nobody."

Luceran looked at Avarren.

Avarren looked delighted.

Nella continued.

"Your court bought every serviceable hinge in Barrow Cross yesterday. The cooper there said Edselsto was paying Crown silver for ironwork and had more doors than locks. I had locks."

"You rode here because of a cooper?"

"I rode here because of money. The cooper provided geography."

Sera snorted.

Luceran liked her immediately.

This was becoming dangerous.

"And the eleven locks?"

"What I could bring without stripping my shop. Four warded chamber locks. Three box locks. Two gate locks. One old-pattern mortise. One very good strongroom lock you cannot afford unless your treasurer is frightened."

"My treasurer is always frightened."

"Then I may sell it."

Avarren said, "Majesty, before you buy a door because you enjoy the woman selling it—"

"Lock."

"The distinction strengthens my point."

Luceran looked at Nella.

"Who else wants your work?"

"Everyone with a door."

"That is not helpful."

"It is accurate."

Behind them, a hammer rang from the village forge.

Nella turned toward the sound.

"That yours?"

Sera folded her arms.

"Mine until somebody with a title discovers it."

Nella looked at her hands.

"Morcant shops?"

"Trained there."

"Bellisar shoes?"

"Contracted there."

"Good. I need a striker."

Sera said, "I need iron."

Nella looked at the crates.

"I brought some."

Sera smiled.

Luceran watched an economy happen without him.

That was also new.

By breakfast, the eleven locks had become seventeen arguments.

The archive wanted two.

Mara wanted the pantry secured against rats and people, in that order.

Pell wanted his chimney repaired and had somehow entered the lock discussion anyway.

Miren Edevane's senior clerk wanted a lock on the east solar because the room now contained copied warrants, household credit tallies, and enough correspondence to make theft interesting.

Olan wanted nobody touching any door in the north range until he had checked the frames.

"Reasonable."

"Don't know."

"Not that one."

"Hand-filed. Old. Repaired twice."

"Was old when I came."

Luceran reached for it.

Not quickly.

Not dramatically.

Just enough.

Luceran's attention sharpened.

Avarren stopped reading.

Rhyse looked up.

"Covered."

"Stone."

"Before my time."

"Before my time."

Luceran almost objected.

Then Mara put a bowl in front of him.

"Eat."

He ate.

This, he suspected, was how absolute monarchy actually worked.

The Morcant iron arrived before noon.

Tomas turned to Luceran as though appealing to the Crown against metallurgy.

Luceran raised both hands.

"I do not know iron."

Sera looked at him.

"Good."

Tomas looked less pleased.

"Majesty, House Morcant has supplied royal armories for—"

"Sera knows iron."

"She was trained in our shops."

"Then this should reassure you."

It did not.

Sera took one rejected bar to the forge, heated the end, and worked it under the hammer with one apprentice striking. The edge opened in a thin ugly split.

Tomas stared.

Sera quenched it.

"Four bars go back."

Tomas exhaled through his nose.

"Four bars go back."

Then, because he was competent rather than theatrical, he took out his tally and marked them rejected.

"I want the Crown order for the next three deliveries."

Avarren said, "At what price?"

"Today's price for the first. Two points less for the second if ordered now. Third indexed to charcoal cost."

Sera laughed.

"No."

Tomas turned.

"You are not the paymaster."

"No. I'm the woman who will tell him how much of your iron I can use."

"And I am the man who knows how much iron can reach this mountain before winter."

That quieted her.

Luceran watched them revise each other.

Not enemies.

Not friends.

Necessary.

Avarren asked, "How much?"

Tomas gave the number.

Avarren swore.

Tomas smiled.

"Now we understand one another."

The bargain took an hour.

Morcant received first right to quote on the next two ordinary iron deliveries, not an exclusive contract. The Crown could buy elsewhere if price or quality failed. Sera would inspect incoming forge iron regardless of supplier. Morcant received no automatic right to finished work merely because it supplied stock.

Tomas disliked the last clause.

Sera loved it.

Luceran approved it because it sounded fair.

Avarren wrote it down because fairness, once written beside quantities and dates, became something creditors could remember.

---

Falbrecht charcoal arrived wet.

Not all of it.

Enough.

The charcoal master, a young widow named Maelin Falbrecht who was a cousin rather than a principal of the house, stood in the storage field while Sera split sacks open with a knife.

"Road rain," Maelin said.

"Bad covering," Sera replied.

"Both."

"I don't burn both."

Maelin crouched and pushed her hand into a sack.

She came up black to the wrist.

"Top quarter is spoiled. Lower is dry. Separate it. You pay only dry weight."

Sera looked at her.

"That easy?"

"No. I want the dry sheds when they exist."

"Everyone wants the dry sheds when they exist."

"I brought the fuel that lets you build the hinges that let them close."

Sera grinned.

"There it is."

Luceran, standing nearby with Miren, said, "There what is?"

Sera pointed between the women.

"Politics."

Miren laughed.

Luceran did not.

Maelin bowed to him.

"Majesty, I want a storage covenant. Covered charcoal space, raised floor, no grain beside it, no lamp storage, and Falbrecht first refusal on supplying the forge through winter if our price remains within one twentieth of the lowest sound bid."

"Why first refusal?"

"Because I will build the sheds."

"With whose timber?"

"Mine."

Miren said quietly, "And then the shed becomes the argument."

Maelin heard her.

"Of course it does."

There was no embarrassment.

Luceran looked across the old common.

Two days earlier it had been grass, mud, a broken wall, and goats.

Now there were stacked timbers, marked stone, carts, lime under tarpaulin, barrels, a rope line, a feed pile, three tents, and two boys running messages uphill.

The Crown was not merely repairing Edselsto.

It was making property valuable by standing near it.

"Temporary shed," Luceran said. "On Crown storage ground. Falbrecht builds it. The Crown owns the structure after one year. You get first refusal through winter if price and quality hold."

Maelin considered.

"Two years."

"One."

"Eighteen months."

Luceran looked at Avarren.

Avarren shrugged.

"You are learning."

"I hate when you say that."

"Eighteen months," Luceran said.

Maelin bowed.

"Done."

Sera said, "And dry weight."

Maelin sighed.

"And dry weight."

The charcoal problem became a shed.

The shed became a supply privilege.

The supply privilege became a date in Avarren's ledger.

Luceran was beginning to understand that politics was often simply the name given to a practical decision after enough people depended on it.

---

The lower well had become six feet deeper and twice as argumentative.

Perren Holt stood in it.

Derrin Voss stood above it.

Neither was happy.

"Stop sending stone down," Perren called.

"I'm not sending stone down."

"Then the mountain is."

"Tell it to stop."

"You're the mason."

Luceran leaned over the edge.

"Progress?"

Both men looked up.

Derrin said, "Water's good. Wall isn't."

Perren said, "North side slipped years ago. Somebody packed rubble behind it instead of rebuilding."

Orven, behind Luceran, said, "That sounds familiar."

"Was everything here repaired badly?" Luceran asked.

"No. Some things were not repaired at all."

Derrin pointed toward a line of stone laid beside the well.

"Need dressed pieces. Not palace grade. Good enough to take load."

"Use the stone pile."

"Which stone pile?"

Luceran looked toward the storage field.

There were now three.

He had not authorized three.

A runner arrived breathless.

"Master Crewe says east pile is roof stone, west pile belongs to Kaelmont carters until weighed, middle pile is salvage and Mister Bale says don't touch the long pieces."

Luceran stared at him.

"How old are you?"

"Twelve."

"How do you know more about my kingdom than I do?"

The boy looked terrified.

Orven said, "Answer him."

"I carry the notes, Majesty."

Luceran straightened.

There it was again.

Information lived where work happened.

"What's your name?"

"Tavin Rane."

"Tessa's?"

"Grandson."

"Good. Tell Master Crewe the well gets enough salvage stone to make it safe unless he can show Derrin a reason it cannot."

Tavin nodded and ran.

Derrin watched him go.

"That'll annoy Olan."

"Will I be wrong?"

"No."

"Then he can be annoyed."

Perren shouted from the well.

"Majesty?"

"Yes?"

"If you want water before winter, stop talking and send stone."

Luceran looked at Orven.

"Do they all become like this?"

"Only the useful ones."

---

Nella Veyr began with the treasury.

She refused to install her best lock.

The treasury clerk nearly suffered a religious crisis.

"You brought a strongroom lock."

"Yes."

"This is the Royal Treasury."

"This is a linen room."

"Temporarily."

"Then temporarily use the lock you have."

The clerk appealed to Luceran.

Luceran had made the mistake of attending.

"Why?"

Nella tapped the door frame.

"Soft wood. Split here. Old worm here. Hinge screws are shallow. Your current lock is adequate against servants, curiosity, and a man without tools. My strongroom lock would be adequate against a patient thief, but the door would still be adequate against a boot."

Rhyse nodded.

"Replace door first."

"And frame," Nella said.

Olan, from the corridor, said, "Not today."

The treasury clerk made a sound.

Olan ignored him.

"Kitchen roof. East sleeping brace. Lower camp drainage. Then doors."

"Treasury—"

"Money doesn't rot when rained on. People do."

Luceran looked at the clerk.

"Current lock stays. Two guards."

Rhyse said, "One guard and a sealed tally at shift change. I don't have men to decorate linen."

The clerk looked wounded.

Luceran said, "One guard."

Another precedent.

He could almost hear Avarren writing it down from three rooms away.

Nella moved to the archive.

There she approved the door but condemned the lock.

At the east solar she approved the lock but condemned the hinges.

At the pantry she said the rats had already won.

At Pell's room she fixed the latch in six minutes and charged him personally.

Pell paid.

"Can I claim this from the household?"

Mara said, "No."

"I admire your consistency."

"I don't. It is exhausting."

By afternoon Nella had sold only four of the eleven locks.

Luceran trusted her more because of it.

He noticed that too.

---

Widow's key waited until late afternoon.

Luceran did not forget it.

Neither did Nella.

Orven tried.

They met at the old family passage with Derrin, Corren Bale, Rhyse, Bram, Montegrain, and one lamp too few.

Olan had refused to come because, in his words, "if the wall has stood a hundred years it can stand until I finish keeping people dry."

Luceran had permitted this insolence because Olan was correct.

The passage lay one turn short of the locked family wing. Its walls were dressed dark stone, older than the plastered guest corridors and colder despite the mild afternoon.

Orven stopped beside a stretch of wall between a narrow window and a shallow alcove.

"There."

Luceran looked.

"That is a wall."

"Excellent."

Derrin crouched.

Corren held the lamp lower.

For a while neither mason spoke.

Luceran began to ask.

Montegrain touched his sleeve.

Wait.

Luceran waited.

Derrin traced a vertical line with one dirty finger.

Once he showed it, Luceran could see it.

The stones inside a tall rectangle were fractionally different in size from those around them.

Corren pointed to the bottom.

"Threshold's still under the floor."

Nella knelt and examined the right side.

"Lock pocket was here."

"How can you tell?" Bram asked.

"Because whoever filled it used a separate piece instead of cutting the jamb out."

She held Widow's key against the air where the lock had once been.

It looked absurd.

A key for a wall.

Luceran said, "When?"

Derrin scraped a little mortar from a deep joint and rubbed it between thumb and finger.

Corren did the same.

"Not original," Derrin said.

"No," Corren agreed.

"Lime's finer than the wall bed."

"Stone came from inside the estate. Same family of basalt."

"Tooling is later."

Luceran waited.

"How much later?"

Corren grimaced.

"Could be a century. Could be three."

"That is useless."

"It's honest."

Nella said, "The lock was maintained before the door was sealed. Key's been repaired."

Orven said, "Or the key was used somewhere else before."

Nella nodded.

"Also possible."

Luceran stared at the rectangle.

"Can we open it?"

Rhyse answered first.

"Where does it go?"

Orven said, "Service stair, according to the old steward."

"According to him personally?"

"According to what he told me."

"Where does the stair emerge?"

"Lower family stores. Maybe."

"Maybe?"

"He never opened it either."

Rhyse looked at Luceran.

"Then no."

Luceran felt the old irritation rise.

"Captain—"

"A sealed route beside the family wing, destination uncertain, with no cleared lower exit and no knowledge of structural condition. If we open it today, I have to guard it today."

Derrin added, "And if the fill is carrying anything it wasn't meant to carry, we may open the ceiling too."

Corren nodded.

"Need shoring before removal."

Nella stood.

"And the key is irrelevant until there is a door again."

Montegrain leaned toward Luceran.

"You have assembled an impressive coalition against your curiosity."

Luceran looked at the wall.

He wanted it opened.

Because his father had known this place.

Because his mother had walked these corridors.

Because somebody had taken a functioning door and made it into stone.

Because he was king and the wall was in his house.

All terrible reasons to knock down masonry at dusk.

"Mark it," he said.

Derrin looked up.

"For opening?"

"For inspection. Shore it. Find the lower end if there is one. Search plans and inventories before anyone removes stone."

Rhyse relaxed by an amount only Bram seemed to notice.

"Guard?" Luceran asked.

"No permanent post," Rhyse said. "That would advertise it. Bram, add it to rounds."

Bram nodded.

Orven looked at Luceran.

"Good."

Luceran turned.

"Do not sound surprised."

"I wasn't."

"You were."

"A little."

Luceran sighed.

Nella held out Widow's key.

"Who keeps this?"

Orven reached for it.

Rhyse reached at the same time.

They stopped.

There, Luceran thought.

Another door.

Except this one was a key.

Orven said, "Steward's ring."

Rhyse said, "Security evidence."

Nella said, "Guild would record the pattern."

Derrin said, "Doesn't open anything."

Montegrain said, "I would like it because everyone else does."

Luceran laughed despite himself.

Then he looked at Orven.

"You kept it forty-seven years. Keep it. Nella records it. Rhyse records that Orven has it."

Rhyse considered.

"Acceptable."

Orven put Widow's back on the ring.

The little piece of iron disappeared among the others.

Nothing about the wall changed.

That made it worse.

---

The village forge worked past sunset.

Luceran heard it from the castle.

Hammer.

Pause.

Hammer-hammer.

Pause.

A rhythm made by people who knew what they were doing.

He went down after dinner with Edrik rather than Bram.

The storage common smelled of wet timber, charcoal, horse piss, lime, cook smoke, and iron.

Falbrecht men were already raising the first posts for the charcoal shed under Maelin's supervision. Bellisar farriers had a separate line of horses waiting. A Morcant wagon was being unloaded under Sera's eye. Tavin Rane ran past with a folded note in one hand and half a heel of bread in the other.

Nobody stopped because the king had arrived.

Luceran liked that.

Then realized liking it was itself a privilege.

Sera had Nella's apprentice at the anvil.

"Again," she said.

The boy struck.

"Flat. You're making a hinge, not murdering a coin."

Nella sat on an upturned barrel filing a lock plate.

"He usually works brass."

"Brass forgives him."

"That's why I like brass."

Luceran leaned against the shed post.

"How many doors today?"

Nella did not look up.

"Four locks sold. Nine latches repaired. Three hinges replaced. Two doors condemned. One wall discovered."

"Productive."

"For me. You owe money."

"Avarren owes money."

Edrik coughed.

Luceran looked at him.

"What?"

"Nothing, Majesty."

"Say it."

"I don't think that's how owing works."

Nella laughed.

Luceran pointed at him.

"You are supposed to protect me."

"From knives. Not invoices."

Sera turned the hinge in the coals.

"Nobody can protect you from invoices."

Maelin Falbrecht entered carrying a tally board.

"Majesty. The shed line crosses the old goat path."

Luceran stared at her.

"Of course it does."

"Marrec Voss says move it four paces east."

"Can you?"

"Yes."

"Then move it."

"It costs another half-day."

"Move it."

She nodded.

No argument.

Edrik watched her go.

"That one will remember."

"The half-day?"

"That you made her move it."

Luceran looked at him.

"I also gave her eighteen months of first refusal."

"She'll remember that too."

"Which matters more?"

Edrik thought.

"Depends what happens next."

Luceran hated how often the answer was that.

---

Miren found him on the walk back up.

She had exchanged court silk for a heavy wool cloak and looked more like the cousin who had once smuggled him into a stable than the cadet princess whose clerks now occupied one of the best workrooms in Edselsto.

"You smell like smoke."

"I have a capital."

"You have a forge."

"Same thing today."

They walked beneath the pines.

Edrik kept far enough back to grant privacy and near enough to ruin any illusion of it.

Miren said, "I heard about the wall."

Luceran stopped.

"How?"

"My clerk heard Nella's apprentice asking for old plans."

Luceran stared at her.

Miren lifted both hands.

"There. That face. Keep it."

"What face?"

"The one where you realize information is not treason merely because it moved without your permission."

"It moved very quickly."

"Everything here moves quickly because everyone is sleeping on top of everyone else."

They resumed walking.

"Do you think my father sealed it?"

"No idea."

"Orven says before his time."

"Then probably not your father."

"He could have resealed it."

"He could have painted it pink. You have no evidence of either."

Luceran glanced at her.

"You are terrible at mystery."

"I am excellent at not helping you invent one."

He smiled.

For a little while they walked without talking.

Below them the new camp showed through the trees in pieces: firelight, lanterns, tent cloth, a wagon moving, sparks from the forge.

Miren said, "You like this."

"What?"

"Building."

Luceran looked down.

"Things happen when I decide them here."

"Yes."

"In the capital every decision became twelve objections and three dinners."

"Here it becomes a shed, a contract, a goat path, and somebody's cousin getting work."

"That sounds better."

"It is better."

He looked at her.

She meant it.

Then she added:

"It is also politics."

He groaned.

"You and Avarren should marry."

"I would kill him by Thursday."

"Wednesday."

"You do know me."

That warmth was real.

So was the fact that her clerks had the east solar.

Luceran was beginning to understand that two truths did not cancel each other merely because holding both was inconvenient.

---

Before bed, Rhyse brought him a sheet of paper.

Not a petition.

A door list.

Twenty-three entries.

Luceran stared at it.

"No."

"You need to see three."

"Then why are there twenty-three?"

"Because I need to see twenty-three."

Rhyse pointed.

"East stair latch is repaired. Bellisar feed access begins tomorrow under the agreed hours. Treasury door remains weak; guard procedure compensates. Family-passage infill is added to rounds, no fixed guard."

"Good."

"And this."

A door on the lower west service route had been found barred from the wrong side.

Luceran looked up.

"Meaning?"

"Meaning somebody once wanted it not merely locked but unusable from the castle side."

"Another mystery?"

"Another old door."

Luceran waited.

Rhyse did not embellish.

"Do we open it?"

"Not tonight."

Luceran smiled.

"You are learning."

Rhyse looked offended.

"Majesty."

"Sorry. Apparently that's irritating."

Rhyse left.

Luceran stood alone with the list.

Twenty-three doors.

Four locks bought.

A treasury room that could be kicked open.

A staircase Bellisar could use at certain hours.

A wall with a key.

A barred service door nobody had yet explained.

A charcoal shed that now represented eighteen months of commercial advantage.

Morcant iron on a conditional supply agreement.

A village well consuming stone that might otherwise repair his roof.

Everywhere he looked, Edselsto was becoming more functional.

Everywhere it became more functional, somebody acquired a reason to care how it was governed.

He had thought power meant being able to open doors.

It was beginning to occur to him that power might be deciding which doors remained closed, who held the key, who paid for the hinge, who stood watch outside, and who got to complain when the answer was no.

From somewhere down the mountain came the faint ring of Sera's hammer.

From the family nave came nothing at all.

Seven candles burned there in silence.

All seven.

# Episode 7 — The Family Wing

The family wing opened at nine in the morning because Nella Veyr refused to open it at eight.

Luceran had been standing in the corridor since a quarter past seven.

"It is a lock," he said.

"It is a lock attached to a door," Nella replied.

"You have said this before."

"And yet people continue buying locks without noticing doors."

She was kneeling beside the north-suite entrance with the old escutcheon removed and three tools laid on a cloth. Jory Fell held a lamp. Orven Dast held the key ring. Captain Rhyse held everyone else outside the chalk line Nella had drawn across the corridor.

Montegrain leaned against the wall beside Luceran.

"I admire a woman who can make a king wait with chalk."

"I could cross it."

"You could also lick the hinge. Neither improves your reign."

Luceran ignored him.

Nella pushed a thin probe into the lock, frowned, withdrew it, and looked at Orven.

"This key has not turned here recently."

"No."

"How recently?"

"Before my time."

Luceran sighed.

Orven looked at him.

"You wanted accurate answers."

"I did."

"This is what they sound like."

Nella finally sat back.

"Lock is serviceable. Door is swollen. Upper hinge has dropped. If I turn the key now and you push, the bolt may clear and the door may still split at the top."

Jory nodded.

"I can lift it from below."

"Without levering against the stone?"

"Yes."

"Show me."

Luceran looked at Montegrain.

"Do they know I am here?"

"They appear to have mistaken you for the client."

Jory wedged a broad timber beneath the door with folded leather protecting the old oak. Nella turned the key. Jory lifted. The door gave a sound like a man waking with a bad back.

Then it opened.

Cold air moved into the corridor.

Nothing whispered.

Nothing glowed.

No dead ancestor appeared to explain the monarchy.

Luceran was disappointed again.

He was becoming accustomed to it.

---

The north suite had been abandoned carefully.

That was worse than ruin.

Dust lay over the furniture, but sheets covered the bed and chairs. The shutters had been latched. Two trunks stood against the inner wall. A writing table remained beneath the window with its drawers closed. The hearth had been swept before the room was left.

This was not a room people had fled.

It was a room someone had finished using.

Luceran stood just inside.

"My father slept here?"

Orven remained at the threshold.

"When he came."

"How often?"

"Less after he married. More before. Then twice after you were born."

Luceran turned.

"Twice?"

"That I remember."

"I was never brought."

"No."

Montegrain did not move closer.

Luceran walked to the writing table.

There was a pale rectangle in the dust where something had once sat.

"What was here?"

Orven looked.

"Writing case, perhaps."

"Perhaps?"

"I did not inventory his desk every time he left."

Luceran opened the first drawer.

Empty.

Second.

Empty.

Third contained sealing wax hardened into useless red sticks, two broken quills, a packet of old sand, and a brass token stamped with the royal stag used during his grandfather's reign.

No confession.

No secret history.

His father had apparently been capable of leaving rubbish like anyone else.

Luceran picked up the token.

He remembered his father's hands teaching him to seal a letter without drowning the paper in wax.

Not here.

At the capital.

He had been eleven.

His father had laughed because Luceran had stamped the seal upside down.

The memory arrived with such ordinary force that he sat down.

Montegrain looked toward the window.

Orven looked toward the floor.

Neither asked what was wrong.

Luceran put the token down.

"He hated bad pens."

Montegrain glanced over.

"I remember."

"He used to steal mine and then complain they were bad."

"That also sounds like him."

Luceran smiled once.

Then he noticed the wall.

Three portraits hung there.

His grandfather.

His grandmother.

His father, perhaps twenty years younger than Luceran remembered him, standing beside a black pine with one hand resting on the shoulder of a man Luceran did not know.

The stranger's face had been cut out.

Not damaged by damp.

Cut.

Cleanly.

Luceran stood.

"Orven."

The steward came in.

He looked at the portrait.

His face changed.

Luceran saw recognition.

"Who?"

Orven took longer than Luceran liked.

"Master Halven Sare."

"Who was he?"

"Your father's secretary here. For a time."

"Why is his face cut out?"

"I don't know."

"When?"

"I don't know."

"Did you know it was like this?"

"Yes."

Luceran stared at him.

"And you never thought to mention it?"

Orven looked back.

"To whom?"

That stopped him.

The castle had spent decades without anyone who cared about a damaged family portrait.

Orven continued.

"Sare left service before your father became king. I was told he went south. Never saw him again."

"Told by whom?"

"Your father."

That was the first thing in the room that felt like a door.

---

The archive problem arrived wearing spectacles and carrying three boxes.

Her name was Ysabet Marris, assistant keeper of royal household records, and she was furious that everyone had started searching before she arrived.

"You moved these?"

The question was directed at Miren Edevane's senior clerk.

He looked offended.

"We preserved them."

"That was not my question."

"They were in damp storage."

"Where?"

"South counting room."

"Which shelf?"

"I don't know."

Ysabet closed her eyes.

Luceran watched from the archive doorway.

Miren stood beside him.

"I like her," he whispered.

"You like anyone who scolds someone other than you."

"It is refreshing."

Ysabet opened her eyes.

"If you move a record without recording where it came from, you preserve the paper and damage the evidence."

The clerk stiffened.

"We were saving them from water."

"Good. Next time save the shelf mark too."

Miren said, "She is correct."

Her clerk looked betrayed.

Miren smiled at him.

"Affection is not immunity from being wrong."

Luceran looked at her.

"You practice that sentence?"

"Constantly."

Ysabet bowed when she finally noticed the king.

"Majesty."

"You came from the capital?"

"Yesterday."

"Who sent you?"

"Keeper Avrel."

"Why you?"

"Because I asked."

Luceran blinked.

"Why?"

She looked around the room as though the answer were physically stacked against the walls.

"Because half the household catalog says 'Edselsto copy' or 'Edselsto original' and nobody in the capital could tell me what that meant."

Miren murmured, "There it is."

Luceran had learned enough to recognize the sound of somebody developing an independent reason to remain near him.

"What do you want?"

Ysabet considered the question seriously.

"A dry room, two copyists, authority to stop people moving records without marking them, and access to Orven Dast while he is alive enough to be annoyed by me."

Orven, entering behind them, said, "No."

Ysabet turned.

"Which part?"

"The implication I am nearly dead."

"Then you have time to help."

Orven stared at her.

Luceran looked at Miren.

Miren whispered, "Keep her."

"I heard that," Ysabet said.

"Good," Miren replied.

---

By midday, the family wing had become less mysterious and more complicated.

Two rooms were structurally safe.

One was not.

A nursery had been stripped nearly bare except for a painted wooden horse missing its tail.

A dressing room contained six gowns wrapped in linen, none belonging to Luceran's mother.

A narrow withdrawing room had a cracked mirror, a card table, and seventeen years of dead flies behind the shutters.

The north library contained books.

This delighted Luceran until Ysabet explained that books were not the same thing as records.

"You are determined to ruin everything."

"I am an archivist."

"Assistant keeper."

"For now."

Montegrain laughed.

Ysabet had begun comparing three inventories: an Edselsto household list copied thirty-one years ago, a royal-family papers register from the capital, and a loose bundle of older shelf tallies Orven had produced from a locked cabinet in the steward's office.

She did not read them dramatically.

She counted.

That was somehow more ominous.

Luceran watched her finger move down a page.

"What?"

"Nothing yet."

"You stopped."

"Because the numbering changes."

"Meaning?"

"Meaning I need the next sheet."

She searched the bundle.

It was not there.

Luceran felt his pulse quicken.

Ysabet did not.

She checked again.

Then the other registers.

Then she asked Orven, "Were these bound?"

"Once."

"When did they come apart?"

"Before me."

"Of course."

Luceran said, "What's missing?"

Ysabet held up one sheet.

"This ends at Family Papers, North Cabinet, sequence forty-three. The next surviving sheet begins at fifty-one."

"Seven entries."

"Eight. Forty-four through fifty-one if fifty-one began on the missing leaf's reverse. I need the binding pattern before I promise a number."

Luceran almost laughed.

"You will not even let me have a missing seven?"

"Majesty, if you want mythology, Brother Calven is down the corridor. If you want to know what paper is missing, let me count holes."

Miren covered her mouth.

Luceran pointed at Ysabet.

"Dry room. Two copyists."

"And authority to stop people moving records."

"Within reason."

"Whose reason?"

"Mine."

"Then write it down."

Avarren would have loved her.

Luceran hated that thought because it meant he was beginning to sort people by institutional compatibility.

He was becoming a court.

---

Outside, the lower village rang with hammers.

Jory left the family wing before lunch because a wagon of black pine had arrived wet and Olan needed someone who could tell which pieces might still serve under cover.

Derrin returned to the well.

Nella went down to the forge because Sera had finished two lock plates and wanted them inspected before wasting more iron.

The king's family mystery did not stop construction.

This also annoyed Luceran.

He stood at a north window overlooking the village.

The old common was almost unrecognizable.

Falbrecht's shed had walls now.

Smoke rose from Sera's forge.

A line of workers carried salvaged stone toward the lower well.

Three new tents had appeared beside the cookfires.

Someone had built a rack for tools.

Two goats were eating something they had certainly been forbidden to eat.

Miren joined him.

"You thought everyone would stop because you found a cut portrait."

"No."

"You did."

"A little."

"Your father would have been offended by the goats."

Luceran smiled.

"He hated goats."

"He hated one goat."

"It ate his sash."

"Therefore all goats became enemies of the Crown."

For several moments they watched the workers below.

Luceran said, "Did he ever talk to you about this place?"

Miren's expression changed.

"Not much."

"That means yes."

"It means not much."

He looked at her.

She sighed.

"Once. At my mother's winter table. You were perhaps fourteen. Someone asked why Edselsto was never used for summer court. He said it was too expensive to maintain and too inconvenient to reach."

"That's true."

"Yes."

"Anything else?"

"He changed the subject."

Luceran waited.

"That's all."

He believed her.

That mattered.

Then she added, "Your mother asked him afterward whether he had written to Orven."

Luceran turned fully.

"You heard that?"

"I was beside her."

"What did he say?"

"Yes."

"About what?"

"I don't know."

"Did she?"

"Probably. They were married. That is not the same as saying she knew everything."

Luceran looked back toward the village.

His parents had become dead before he had learned how much of marriage was simply two people having conversations their children never heard.

He had mistaken being loved for being informed.

The realization hurt more than the cut portrait.

---

Ysabet found his father's handwriting at three in the afternoon.

Not a letter.

A correction.

The note appeared in the margin of an old household inventory beside a line reading: **North family cabinet — retained papers, private.**

The original clerk had written a shelf number.

Luceran's father had crossed it out.

Beside it, in the narrow hard hand Luceran knew immediately, he had written:

**Remove from general register. Keep under household seal. No copies. — L.**

Luceran stared.

Nobody spoke.

Ysabet finally said, "I can confirm the hand against three signed letters if you want formal attribution."

"It's his."

"I believe you. I still want comparison before I enter it as confirmed."

Luceran looked at her.

"Do it."

She nodded.

Montegrain stood at the other end of the table.

He did not come closer.

Luceran read the line again.

"When?"

Ysabet pointed to the inventory heading.

"Register compiled twenty-six years ago. The ink of the correction is later, but I cannot date ink by staring at it."

"Before I was born?"

"The register was. The correction could be later."

"Orven?"

The steward shook his head.

"Never saw that note."

"Household seal. What did that mean?"

"Could mean the king's private papers. Could mean the family secretary. Could mean a sealed chest. Depends when."

"Halven Sare?"

Orven looked at the cut portrait in the adjoining room.

"Could."

"Did he keep the seal?"

"For a time."

Luceran felt the story assembling itself too quickly.

His father.

A secretary whose face had been cut from a portrait.

Records deliberately removed.

A sealed doorway.

Widow's key.

Voices in walls.

He could feel his mind making a shape because minds hated loose pieces.

Ysabet interrupted it.

"Majesty."

"What?"

"The note says papers were removed from a general register. It does not say why."

Luceran looked at her.

"I know."

"Good."

"You think I don't."

"I think you are grieving and have seven missing entries, a damaged portrait, and an old door. That is enough material to build a very convincing wrong answer."

Montegrain looked at Ysabet with sudden approval.

Luceran did not enjoy this.

Which was probably why he needed it.

"Find the household-seal register."

Ysabet nodded.

"If it survives."

"Find whether it survives."

"That I can do."

---

The next discovery belonged to Orven.

He made it unwillingly.

They were in the old dressing room because Ysabet wanted to know whether a narrow cabinet built into the wall had ever held papers. Orven said it held gloves.

Ysabet asked how he knew.

"Because I put gloves in it."

"Whose?"

"The queen's."

Luceran stopped.

"My mother used this room?"

Orven looked annoyed with himself.

"Once."

"When?"

"Last time they came."

"They came together?"

"Yes."

"You said my father came twice after I was born."

"Yes."

"Was she here both times?"

"No."

"When was this?"

Orven thought.

"Nine years ago. Maybe ten."

Luceran would have been an adolescent in the capital.

He remembered his parents leaving for what he had been told was an estate circuit.

"How long?"

"Two nights."

"Why?"

"They did not tell me."

"Who came with them?"

"Four guards. Your father's secretary then. One maid for your mother. No court."

"Did they use the family wing?"

"Yes."

"Did they go anywhere else?"

Orven hesitated.

There.

Luceran saw it.

"Where?"

"Your father asked for old keys."

"Which?"

"Several."

"Widow's?"

"I don't remember."

Luceran nearly snapped at him.

Then stopped.

Forty-seven years of keys.

Nine or ten years ago.

A two-night visit that had meant nothing to Orven until the dead man's son arrived demanding chronology.

"What do you remember?"

Orven considered.

"He asked which lower family stores were still accessible. He asked whether the old north stair had shifted. He asked whether anyone had opened the wall beside the family passage."

Silence.

Luceran said, "The infilled doorway."

"Probably."

"And what did you tell him?"

"No."

"Did he try?"

"Not while I was with him."

"My mother?"

Orven looked toward the little cabinet.

"She was angry."

Luceran's throat tightened.

"At him?"

"I assumed so."

"About what?"

"I did not hear enough to know."

"What did you hear?"

Orven was quiet.

"She said, 'Not while he is a child.'"

Luceran did not move.

Montegrain did.

One step closer.

Not touching.

Close enough.

Luceran asked, "He?"

Orven shook his head.

"I assumed you. There were other boys in the world. I don't know."

"What did my father say?"

"Nothing I heard."

"And you never told me."

Orven's expression hardened.

"Your parents had an argument in their own room ten years ago. I was steward, not spy."

Luceran looked away.

That rebuke landed because it was deserved.

He had spent his first week at Edselsto complaining that the old capital knew everything about him.

Now he was angry that Orven had not listened harder at his parents' door.

"You're right," he said.

Orven's face softened by almost nothing.

"Usually."

Montegrain exhaled a laugh.

The room became survivable again.

---

By evening, Ysabet had found the hole.

Not the missing papers.

The hole where the missing papers had once been accounted for.

A household-seal register survived in the capital archive copy, but the Edselsto counterpart did not. The capital register contained a cross-reference written in a clerk's hand:

**Edselsto private packet — N.F. 44–51 — transferred under royal instruction. Receipt filed separately.**

The receipt was not present.

The transfer date was nine years ago.

Luceran sat very still.

Ysabet had already checked the arithmetic twice.

"N.F.?"

"Probably North Family. Possibly North Folio. I will not choose because one is more dramatic."

"Nine years."

"Yes."

"The visit."

"Likely the same year. I have not established the month."

"Transferred where?"

"Not stated here."

"Who signed?"

"The receipt would tell us."

"And the receipt is missing."

"Not necessarily missing. Not present in this sequence."

Luceran laughed without humor.

"You are relentless."

"Words are the work."

He looked at the marginal instruction in his father's hand.

Remove from general register. Keep under household seal. No copies.

Then at the later cross-reference.

Transferred under royal instruction.

Nine years ago.

His parents had come here quietly.

His mother had said, not while he is a child.

Maybe about him.

Maybe not.

His father had asked about old keys and a wall.

Maybe the same wall.

Maybe not.

For the first time, Luceran did not try to make the pieces agree.

"What can we actually say?"

Ysabet answered immediately.

"Your father ordered a set of private family papers removed from the general register. A later register says a packet numbered forty-four through fifty-one was transferred under royal instruction nine years ago. The receipt is not with the material we have. Orven remembers your parents visiting in roughly that period and your father asking about old keys, lower stores, the north stair, and whether the family-passage wall had been opened."

"And my mother."

Ysabet looked at Orven.

He said, "I remember one sentence from an argument."

Ysabet nodded.

"Then that is what we have."

Luceran leaned back.

Montegrain finally sat beside him.

"Enough?"

Luceran looked at him.

"No."

"I know."

"I thought he told me things."

Montegrain considered.

"He did."

"Not this."

"Apparently not."

Luceran stared at the table.

"I don't know whether to be angry."

"You can postpone it."

"Can kings do that?"

"No. Friends can."

Luceran looked at him.

There were a hundred political uses for a man allowed to sit beside a grieving king after dark.

For once, neither of them cared.

That did not make the uses disappear.

---

Before bed, Luceran walked to the family passage.

Bram went with him.

The infilled doorway remained stone.

Chalk marks from Derrin and Corren showed the old opening. A small notation in Nella's hand marked the probable lock position. Nothing had been broken.

Luceran touched the later masonry.

His father had stood somewhere near here nine years ago.

Perhaps.

That word had become the most important word in Edselsto.

Bram said, "Majesty?"

"Yes?"

"Captain says no breach until Master Crewe clears shoring."

Luceran smiled faintly.

"You thought I might?"

"I thought you might think about it."

"I am thinking about it."

"Then my work is excellent."

Luceran laughed.

He took his hand away.

"Not tonight."

"Good."

They walked back toward the inhabited wing.

At the junction, Brother Calven emerged from the nave carrying a snuffer and fresh wax.

Luceran stopped.

"All seven?"

Calven looked back through the open door.

"All seven."

Luceran could see them from where he stood.

Seven flames.

Steady.

Meaningless, as far as he knew.

Old family custom.

He found himself counting anyway.

One.

Two.

Three.

Four.

Five.

Six.

Seven.

Then he went to bed.

# Episode 8 — The First Court

The first formal court at Edselsto began with a bucket.

Luceran stood beneath the royal canopy while Mara Kest moved it three feet to the left.

He watched her.

She watched the ceiling.

A drop of water struck the flagstones where his right shoulder had been.

"No," Mara said.

Luceran looked up.

"It isn't raining."

"It rained yesterday."

"The roof remembers?"

"The roof leaks slowly."

Montegrain, already dressed for court in black velvet that had somehow survived the journey without becoming construction material, leaned toward Luceran.

"A useful ancestral quality."

"Do not encourage the roof."

Master Olan Crewe stood on a scaffold at the far end of the great hall and shouted down, "Bucket stays until we open that bay!"

Avarren closed his eyes.

"Must he work during audience?"

Olan looked down.

"Must the roof leak during audience?"

Luceran said, "He wins."

Avarren opened his eyes.

"Majesty, this is the first formal reception of your court at Edselsto."

"Yes."

"There is scaffolding behind the throne."

"Yes."

"There are carpenters above the visiting benches."

"Only above the north benches," Mara said.

Avarren looked at her.

"That does not improve the sentence."

Mara held a piece of chalk in one hand and a folded room list in the other.

"Then seat people south."

"Those places are already assigned."

"Then move them."

Avarren stared at her as though she had proposed moving the mountain.

Luceran had begun to understand that this was what court actually was.

Not decrees.

Not speeches.

Not even people.

It was the place where a leaking roof met a seating chart and somebody had to decide which one possessed precedence.

"Move them," he said.

Avarren sighed.

"Yes, Majesty."

The court moved three benches south.

By noon, this would be interpreted as policy.

---

The great hall had been cleaned.

It had not been restored.

That distinction became more obvious the more people tried to dress it as though it had been.

Old banners had been beaten free of dust and rehung between newer royal colors. Two boarded windows had been covered with dark cloth. The dry fountain from the courtyard had surrendered three stone basins to hold pine branches because nobody had enough flowers. Servants had scrubbed the floor until the old repairs became more visible rather than less.

The throne was not the throne.

The actual Edselsto throne, if one existed, remained somewhere in a part of the castle Orven had not yet declared safe.

So a high-backed chair from the west solar had been placed on a low timber platform built the previous afternoon by Jory Fell and two other carpenters.

Jory had refused to make it higher.

"Why?" Luceran had asked.

"Because the floor dips."

"It looks level."

"That is because I made the platform level."

"Can you make it one step higher?"

"Yes."

"Then why won't you?"

"Because then I need another brace underneath and Olan has the dry oak."

Luceran had considered ordering the dry oak.

Then he remembered how quickly practical decisions had become favors and asked what the oak was for.

"East sleeping roof."

"Keep the roof."

Jory had nodded.

That was how the throne of Embar-Zara came to be one step shorter than Avarren wanted.

By eleven, people had already noticed.

---

The first arrivals were people Luceran knew.

That made everything easier.

It also made everything worse.

Lady Miren Edevane came through the west door because her clerks worked near the archives and already knew the route. She wore dark blue, carried no ceremonial gift, and stopped beside Luceran before the herald had finished deciding whether she was supposed to be announced.

"You moved the benches."

"The roof moved them."

"The roof favors Pell."

Luceran looked.

House Pell's assigned places were now closer to the central aisle.

"Oh, for—"

Miren smiled.

"Good morning."

"Can I move them back?"

"You can. Then everyone will know you noticed."

"I hate you."

"No, you don't."

"Temporarily."

"That is how court works."

She took her place.

Lord Montegrain had no assigned place because everyone had assumed he would stand where he pleased.

He did.

Captain Tovan Rhyse stood to the right of the dais where he could see the main doors, east stair, and service entrance. Bram Tolland held the near corridor. Edrik Saye was farther back where Luceran could not always see him.

That was deliberate.

House Bellisar's stable officers entered through the east route because their morning access arrangement already allowed it.

Avarren saw them.

So did everyone else.

"They didn't wait," he murmured.

Rhyse answered without looking away from the hall.

"They were inspected at the east post."

"That is not what I meant."

"I know."

The Bellisar men had not broken protocol.

They had simply possessed a protocol nobody else possessed yet.

Luceran understood the difference now.

He wished he did not.

---

The later houses arrived with gifts.

That was when the court became a court.

House Kaelmont brought a road map.

Not a ceremonial map.

A working one.

It was carried rolled around a wooden staff by two mud-spattered surveyors who looked deeply unhappy about being made to wear clean collars.

Lady Ardel Kaelmont followed them.

Luceran knew her by reputation before he knew her face.

House Kaelmont controlled mountain roads, bridge tolls, quarry rights, and enough fortified passes that every monarch eventually discovered geography had a surname.

Ardel was perhaps ten years older than Luceran, broad-shouldered, brown-haired, and dressed in white and slate gray without a jewel visible anywhere except a small silver clasp at her throat.

She bowed correctly.

Not deeply.

"Majesty."

"Lady Ardel."

"My house offers you the useful parts of a mountain."

Montegrain murmured, "Keep her."

Miren, on his other side, said, "You say that about everyone competent."

"I have standards."

Ardel heard both of them.

Her mouth moved slightly.

Luceran gestured toward the map.

"Show me."

Avarren whispered, "After petitions."

Luceran looked at him.

Avarren looked toward the waiting hall.

There were already forty people watching.

Luceran sat back down.

"After petitions."

Ardel bowed again.

"Sensible."

She turned toward the benches.

Then stopped.

Her eyes moved across the hall.

Pell near the aisle.

Edevane near the archives door.

Bellisar people already inside from the east stair.

Morcant representatives standing near Sera's forge clerk because they had arrived together from the lower village.

A Falbrecht factor speaking quietly with the quartermaster about charcoal deliveries.

Montegrain beside the dais.

Miren close enough to speak without raising her voice.

Ardel looked back at Luceran.

Not accusingly.

Interested.

He knew that look now too.

She had just seen the court he had thought he had not yet built.

---

The first petition concerned beds.

The second concerned flour.

The third concerned horse dung.

Avarren had arranged the order by dignity.

Mara had rearranged it by urgency.

Mara won because the dung was blocking a drainage channel.

Master Pellin Voss of the household stores stood before the dais holding a tally board.

"If the east stable line remains at present strength, Majesty, the lower cart road needs clearing twice daily. We have nowhere permanent for manure and bedding."

Luceran looked at Mara.

"Field?"

"Which field?"

"The one below the old common."

A voice from the waiting benches said, "Belongs to three families."

Everyone turned.

Tessa Rane, representing the Village Council, had not been seated with the nobles.

She had also not asked permission to speak.

Avarren looked pained.

Luceran said, "Three?"

"Rane, Dorn, and Henn strips."

"Can we rent it?"

"You can ask."

Luceran almost said *I am asking you*.

He stopped.

Tessa saw him stop.

That mattered more than either of them said.

"Ask them," he said. "Until then, use the old quarry spoil ground if it won't foul the lower well."

Derrin Voss, present because the well repair had become relevant to half the day's petitions, shook his head.

"Too close downhill."

"Where then?"

Tessa said, "North pasture edge."

Pellin said, "Too far for daily carts."

"Then don't stable so many horses uphill."

House Bellisar's representative inhaled.

Luceran looked at him.

The man closed his mouth.

Luceran almost laughed.

Instead he said, "Half the visiting remounts stay below the village after today. Bellisar can organize the line because they already have the grooms. Crown pays fodder at village rate, not capital rate. Tessa, have the council mark ground that can be used without taking planted strips."

"Temporary?"

"Temporary."

"Write it."

Miren coughed.

Luceran looked at her.

She smiled sweetly.

Ysabet Marris was apparently contagious.

"Write it," Luceran said.

A clerk did.

The court had now produced its first manure ordinance.

Montegrain looked solemn.

"History."

Luceran kicked his boot beneath the chair.

---

Lord Edmund Moncler did not come to Edselsto.

His factor did.

Master Gerant Vale wore excellent wool and the expression of a man who had traveled too far to be laughed at.

Luceran recognized the sealed packet in his hand before the herald finished announcing him.

The great hall became quieter.

Everyone knew the story.

The young king had walked out of the capital, crossed the street, entered Moncler's construction site, and taken the workforce.

It had already become funnier in retelling than it had been for anyone required to pay for it.

Vale bowed.

"Majesty. Lord Moncler sends his respect."

"And his bill."

A few people laughed.

Vale did not.

Luceran regretted the joke immediately.

Good.

That meant he was learning.

"His claim," Vale corrected.

"His claim."

Avarren accepted the packet and passed it to Clavard Clifford, who had been waiting for exactly this moment with the expression of a man finally allowed to use numbers as weapons.

Vale continued.

"My lord does not dispute the Crown's authority to requisition labor in urgent royal works. He disputes the proposition that urgency abolishes compensation for interrupted private contracts, wasted deliveries, idle subcontractors, and penalties already incurred because men under agreement were removed without notice."

No one laughed now.

Luceran looked toward Olan.

The foreman stood at the side of the hall in clean work clothes he clearly hated.

"Master Crewe."

Olan straightened.

"Majesty."

"Were you under contract?"

"Yes."

"Did Moncler lose money when we left?"

"Yes."

Vale looked surprised.

Olan looked irritated that surprise was possible.

"We were framing the west roof. Timber was cut. Stone deliveries booked. Scaffold hired. You can't stop that without cost."

Luceran looked at Clavard.

"How much?"

"We do not know yet."

Vale said, "The claim states—"

Clavard lifted one finger.

"The claim states what Lord Moncler requests. His accounts will state what happened."

Vale smiled for the first time.

"Good."

Luceran noticed that too.

Moncler had not sent a man expecting automatic payment.

He had sent a man expecting to be taken seriously.

Luceran leaned back.

"Then take it seriously."

Clavard nodded.

Luceran continued, "Documented direct loss from the requisition is payable by the Crown. No invented dignity charges. No punishment for making the claim. No assumption that every penalty in that packet is ours until the accounts are checked."

Vale bowed.

"That is all my lord asks."

"I doubt that."

This time Vale laughed.

"It is all he asks today."

There it was.

Court.

Luceran could feel it happening.

"Master Crewe," he said, "you and the workers remain Crown-employed at the agreed royal wage unless you choose to leave when your present term ends. Nobody is to tell me later that compensation to Moncler purchased the men themselves."

Olan's expression changed.

So did several guild representatives'.

Avarren looked sharply at Luceran.

Vale said, "My lord will not object to that language."

"Good."

The clerk wrote.

The joke about stealing carpenters had just become a rule about what the Crown had actually stolen.

Not men.

Time.

Contracts.

Money.

That was more expensive.

---

The Royal Companion problem came immediately afterward.

Luceran suspected Avarren had arranged this deliberately.

Avarren denied it with his face.

That was not evidence.

Sir Caldus Pell stepped forward on behalf of two dismissed Companions related through House Pell's impossible family web.

Luceran liked Caldus.

That was inconvenient.

Caldus had taught him cards when he was sixteen and had once allowed him to win for exactly three hands before taking every coin back.

He bowed.

"Majesty. I am not asking you to restore men who failed their watch."

Luceran relaxed slightly.

"Good."

"I am asking what happened to their commissions."

Luceran stopped relaxing.

Rhyse's attention shifted.

Caldus continued.

"They were appointed under sealed household commission. Captain Rhyse removed them from close duty for cause. Correctly, in my view. But removal from duty and termination of commission are not necessarily the same act. Their families now do not know whether they are dismissed officers, suspended officers, or men awaiting review."

Luceran looked at Rhyse.

Rhyse said, "He's right."

The hall changed.

Not dramatically.

People simply listened harder.

Luceran said, "You dismissed them."

"From the post. I had authority to remove them from immediate duty. Their commissions were issued by the Crown."

"You told me this?"

"I told Clavard."

Clavard said, "He did."

Luceran looked at Clavard.

"Did you tell me?"

"You were leaving the capital at dawn."

"That is not an answer."

"No. I did not."

Avarren said quietly, "And now everyone knows the distinction."

Luceran wanted to be angry.

Instead he looked at Caldus.

"What do they want?"

"One wants formal discharge with arrears through the date of removal. One wants review and the chance to argue he was assigned to barracks duty rather than close escort when you left the palace. I think he will lose. He should still be heard."

That was worse than a demand for favoritism.

It was reasonable.

Luceran looked at Rhyse.

"Can you review them?"

"Not alone. I was the officer who removed them."

Avarren nodded.

"Three-person household board. One guard officer not in Rhyse's direct chain for the incident, one household legal officer, one Crown representative. Written findings."

Luceran grimaced.

"That sounds like something that will take a month."

"Then order a week."

"Can it be done in a week?"

"If you want anyone to sleep, ten days."

Luceran looked at Caldus.

"Ten days. Arrears continue until formal status is determined. No restoration to close escort during review."

Caldus bowed.

"Fair."

Rhyse said, "Yes."

Luceran looked at him.

"You don't mind?"

"Why would I?"

"They're reviewing your decision."

"They should."

Luceran sat with that.

He had fled a capital where every review felt like sabotage.

Here was his friend, the captain of his guard, asking to have his own decision examined because authority was cleaner when somebody else could say whether he had used it properly.

Luceran had not expected that lesson from Rhyse.

He suspected Rhyse had not intended it as one.

Those were usually the lessons that stayed.

---

At midday, the court stopped because the kitchen could not feed it.

Not symbolically.

Literally.

Mara walked to the dais while a grain factor was explaining river prices and said, "Enough."

Avarren whispered, "Mara."

"We have one hundred and seventy-three additional mouths inside the upper gate."

The grain factor stopped talking.

Mara continued, "We prepared for one hundred and twenty. The second oven cracked this morning. The village bakers can provide another forty loaves by evening if we stop buying every loaf before villagers get to market."

Luceran looked at the hall.

One hundred and seventy-three people had arrived to participate in government.

None of them had arrived believing lunch was politics.

"What do we have?"

"Stew. Bread. Cheese. Apples. Enough if portions are sensible. Not enough for a formal table."

Luceran looked at Avarren.

"Sensible portions."

Avarren stared at the visiting houses.

"Majesty—"

Lady Ardel Kaelmont called from the benches, "I have eaten worse on better roads."

Several people laughed.

Miren said, "There. Kaelmont has established the luncheon standard."

Ardel looked at her.

"Send me the bill."

Luceran laughed.

The hall relaxed.

That was how the first formal luncheon at Edselsto became bowls of stew carried to benches while masons continued working behind a cloth screen.

Luceran loved it.

That was dangerous too.

He loved that the people around him could laugh when things failed.

He loved that Ardel ate the same stew as a copyist.

He loved that Miren stole an apple from Montegrain and he accused her of treason.

He loved that Rhyse ate standing beside a door because he refused to surrender the sightline.

He loved that Sera Morcant arrived late with soot still under one thumbnail and nobody asked her to hide it.

This was what he had wanted.

A court that worked.

A court that breathed.

A court that did not feel like a room full of knives.

He did not yet understand that knives were not required for politics.

Friendship was enough.

---

Lady Ardel found him beside the road map after lunch.

The map covered most of an old dining table.

It showed Edselsto's mountain roads in black ink, old quarry tracks in red, bridges in blue, and three sections marked with small white crosses.

"Those?" Luceran asked.

"Failures waiting for weather."

"That sounds encouraging."

"Mountains are patient."

She pointed to the southern approach.

"Your convoy damaged this culvert. Not badly. The next fifty wagons may finish it. This bridge can take loaded carts one at a time. This section needs stone before winter. And this—"

Her finger moved to the switchback below the old mill.

"—is why every factor in your hall is charging you for delay."

Luceran looked at her.

"You want the road contract."

"Of course."

He appreciated the answer.

"Kaelmont controls the road already."

"We control toll rights and maintain two sections under charter. We do not own every stone between here and the capital."

"What do you offer?"

"Survey crews already here. Quarry access at reduced Crown rate for two years. Bridge crews from White Step before first snow. We waive toll on Crown construction stone moving uphill."

"And what do you want?"

"Recognition that improvements we build under existing charter remain Kaelmont-maintained toll works after the emergency period, except the final royal mile."

Luceran stared at the map.

It was a good offer.

That was the problem.

"How much is that worth?"

Ardel smiled.

"Ask someone who dislikes me."

Luceran looked up.

She was still smiling.

Not flirtatiously, exactly.

Not not flirtatiously either.

He had become suspicious of adverbs.

"Who dislikes you?"

"House Damaris when tolls slow grain. House Bellisar when cavalry waits behind carts. Every carter alive."

"Convenient."

"Majesty, if I wanted you ignorant, I would have brought a prettier map."

Luceran laughed.

Then he saw where she was looking.

Not at him.

At the room.

"Say it," he said.

"What?"

"You noticed something when you came in."

Ardel considered denying it.

He saw the consideration.

That pleased him.

"Your court is not blank," she said.

"I know."

"Do you?"

He disliked her immediately.

Then liked her for causing it.

"Explain."

She touched the map staff against the table.

"Edevane has clerks beside your archive. Bellisar has a route through your guard geometry. Pell has rooms close enough to learn who comes and goes. Morcant has first quotation on iron. Falbrecht has winter charcoal preference. Your locksmith came before half the nobility. Your village council can stop you putting dung in a field. Your friend stands beside your chair without an office anyone can petition to obtain."

Luceran's face warmed.

Ardel continued before he could interrupt.

"None of that is necessarily wrong. Most of it is sensible. They came early. They helped. You rewarded usefulness."

"You make that sound like an accusation."

"No. I make it sound like politics."

He stared at her.

She leaned one hip against the table.

"The capital had old winners. Edselsto has new ones. The difference is that you like yours."

That landed harder than Orven's twenty-two-year roof.

Luceran looked across the hall.

Miren speaking with an archivist.

Montegrain arguing cheerfully with a Pell cousin.

Sera discussing hinge iron with Nella Veyr.

A Bellisar groom carrying a message through the east door because he already knew which guard to show it to.

A Falbrecht factor eating the last of the cheese beside the quartermaster.

People he liked.

People who had helped him.

People who had earned things.

Ardel said, "You thought moving here would remove the old map."

"It did."

"Yes."

She tapped the table.

"Then you drew another one."

Luceran looked at her.

"And now Kaelmont wants onto it."

"Naturally."

At least she had the grace not to pretend otherwise.

"No road charter today."

"Good."

He blinked.

"Good?"

"If you granted it because I showed you a map after stew, I would worry about the kingdom."

"You came here to be refused?"

"I came here to be considered before winter."

She rolled the map closed halfway, then stopped.

"Keep it."

"Gift?"

"Working copy. If you spill wine on it, I charge you."

She walked away.

Luceran watched her go.

Montegrain appeared beside him.

"You like her."

"She just explained that I have recreated the thing I ran away from."

"Yes."

"Why would that make me like her?"

"You have peculiar tastes."

Luceran looked at him.

Montegrain took an apple from the table.

"Also she is attractive."

"Go away."

"You say that often."

"One day it will work."

"No."

Luceran hated that he was probably right.

The afternoon audience became faster.

Not because the problems became simpler.

Because Luceran began recognizing their shapes.

A Morcant factor requested that the first-quotation arrangement for ordinary iron include nails.

Sera said no from the side of the hall because nail quality varied by use and she would not let a house contract dictate what she bought for the forge.

Luceran backed her.

Morcant accepted the refusal and asked for written notice when larger structural iron tenders opened.

Granted.

A Falbrecht factor wanted confirmation that charcoal delivered under the winter first-refusal arrangement would be weighed dry.

Granted because that was already the bargain.

A Pell cousin requested the chamber beside his current one for his secretary.

Mara said the ceiling plaster was loose.

Request denied because politics remained subordinate to gravity.

An Edevane clerk asked for another lockable archive cabinet.

Ysabet, who had come upstairs only to deliver a sealed register, said, "Not Edevane. Archive."

The clerk corrected himself.

"Another lockable archive cabinet."

Luceran looked at Ysabet.

"Do you need one?"

"Yes."

"Then archive gets one."

Miren smiled from across the hall.

Her house had lost ownership of a useful thing and gained the useful thing anyway.

That was politics too.

A representative of House Nerezza objected that customs records had been placed two corridors farther from Luceran than Edevane clerks.

Avarren began explaining room safety.

The Nerezza man interrupted.

"I understand the roof, Lord Avarren. I am telling you what the placement communicates."

Luceran looked at him.

The man was not angry.

He was correct.

"What would you have me do?"

"Nothing today."

That answer surprised him.

"Then why raise it?"

"Because six months from now someone will tell you these arrangements became custom because nobody objected. I am objecting."

Luceran sat back.

"Recorded."

The Nerezza man bowed.

"Thank you."

Luceran looked at Avarren.

Avarren's expression said *now you understand*.

Luceran hated that expression.

It was becoming common.

---

By late afternoon, the waiting order had become more important than the petitions.

People who had arrived before noon had watched others enter through doors they had not known existed.

They had watched Miren speak to Luceran between formal matters.

They had watched Montegrain make him laugh.

They had watched Rhyse deny two noble attendants access to the dais corridor while allowing Bram to cross it without challenge.

They had watched Mara interrupt proceedings twice.

They had watched Tessa Rane speak without being announced and receive an answer.

They had watched Olan Crewe send a carpenter across the back of the hall carrying a twelve-foot board while a customs officer was petitioning the Crown.

Nobody needed a chart.

The chart was happening in front of them.

Luceran finally understood why the old capital had seemed to know everything before he did.

People watched.

That was all.

They watched who could interrupt.

Who could approach.

Who waited.

Who did not.

Who got written answers.

Who got jokes.

Who knew which door opened.

Who could make the king change his mind without making him angry.

He had thought secrecy was the problem.

Observation was enough.

---

The last formal matter belonged to the Village Council.

Tessa Rane came forward again, this time with Marrec Voss and Lysa Dorn.

Avarren had given them a proper place on the list after the manure discussion.

Luceran suspected this was either progress or surrender.

"Majesty," Tessa said, "we need tomorrow's arrivals stopped at the lower road until somebody counts beds."

Several nobles reacted as though she had proposed closing the kingdom.

Luceran said, "How many are coming?"

Mara answered.

"At least ninety-six named in messages. Probably more."

"Beds?"

"Thirty-one if nobody objects to sharing rooms."

Montegrain said, "They will."

"Water?"

Derrin answered from the side.

"Lower well isn't finished. Upper well can carry current use if we keep horses off it. Another hundred people and washing becomes the problem before drinking does."

Lysa Dorn said, "Village has rooms."

Tessa looked at her.

"Some."

Lysa continued, "And people will rent them if the Crown stops assuming every empty-looking house is empty."

Luceran winced.

The Henn property problem had apparently acquired friends.

"What do you propose?"

Tessa had come prepared.

Of course she had.

"Lower road receiving line. Every arriving household declares people, horses, carts, and whether they have tents. Village council marks private lodging offered for rent. Crown quartermaster marks camp ground. Nobody takes a house because the roof looks abandoned. Food buyers stop bidding against each other in the market."

A Damaris grain factor said, "Central purchasing?"

Tessa looked at him.

"One Crown buyer for court staples. Private households can buy extras after village morning sale."

The factor frowned.

"That will suppress price."

"It will stop your lord's cook paying twice the normal price for every egg before my neighbors eat breakfast."

The hall became very quiet.

Luceran looked at the grain factor.

The man considered.

"A fixed Crown staple rate will fail if supply costs rise."

Tessa said, "Then review it weekly."

The factor nodded slowly.

"That could work."

Luceran stared at both of them.

They had just negotiated half his food policy while he watched.

He should have been offended.

Instead he felt relief.

"Do that."

Avarren said, "Which part?"

"All of it. Lower-road receiving line. Count people and animals. Village-offered lodging gets recorded before assignment. No occupation of private buildings without agreement or separate royal order. Crown staples through one buyer at a rate reviewed weekly with the council and suppliers. Camp ground marked before tents go up."

Tessa nodded.

The Damaris factor said, "I want our objection to a permanently fixed rate recorded."

"It isn't permanent."

"Then record that too."

Luceran looked at the ceiling.

No water fell on him.

A small mercy.

"Record everything."

Avarren smiled.

"Careful."

"Why?"

"That is how archives happen."

Somewhere below them, Ysabet Marris would have approved.

---

Court ended at sunset.

Nobody left.

That was another lesson.

Formal audience ended.

Politics continued.

People stood in clusters around the hall, courtyard, kitchens, stairs, and covered walkways. Servants carried wine. Workers moved ladders around them. A groom came through looking for a Bellisar officer. Two copyists argued over a dry table. Sera left early because the forge did not care that the king had held court.

Lady Ardel Kaelmont was speaking with the Damaris factor she had named as someone who disliked her.

They were laughing.

Luceran watched them.

"I thought they were rivals."

Miren stood beside him.

"They are."

"They're drinking together."

"Yes."

"I hate politics."

"No, you don't."

He looked at her.

"You keep saying that."

"Because you keep enjoying yourself."

He looked around the hall.

She was right.

He had enjoyed the day.

Even Moncler's claim.

Even Caldus Pell's procedural objection.

Even Ardel telling him he had built a faction without noticing.

Especially that, perhaps, because it meant there was something here worth arguing over.

"I wanted people I could trust," he said.

Miren followed his gaze.

"You have some."

"And apparently that is a political problem."

"Everything is a political problem if enough people care about it."

"That is a terrible philosophy."

"It is not philosophy."

She took his cup, drank from it, and handed it back.

The gesture was old.

Familiar.

Private enough that neither of them thought about it until Luceran noticed Lady Ardel notice.

Miren noticed him noticing.

"There," she said.

"What?"

"Another map."

Luceran groaned.

Miren smiled.

"You wanted a court."

"I wanted a better one."

Her expression softened.

"Then build one."

Not escape one.

Build one.

That was different.

He looked across the unfinished hall.

At the scaffold.

At the bucket.

At the nobles and clerks and workers and villagers and factors and guards who had spent the day making claims on one another.

At Montegrain stealing another apple.

At Rhyse still watching doors.

At Tessa Rane cornering the quartermaster before he could leave.

At Olan climbing back onto the scaffold now that ceremony no longer required him to pretend the roof could wait.

At people.

Difficult, useful, ambitious, funny, observant people.

For the first time since his parents died, Luceran looked at a court and did not want to run away from it.

That was a victory.

It was also, though he did not yet understand the scale of it, the beginning of every problem that would follow.

---

The nave was quiet after midnight.

Luceran did not enter.

Brother Calven did.

He checked the wax because one candle had begun to gutter in a draft from the outer passage.

Orven closed the passage door.

The flame steadied.

Calven counted.

One.

Two.

Three.

Four.

Five.

Six.

Seven.

All burning.

Nothing else happened.

Above them, the new court slept badly in rooms it had already begun to consider its own.

# Episode 9 — The Village Below

# Season One, Episode 09 — The Village Below

The first argument of the morning began over a horse.

It was not, Luceran thought afterward, an especially distinguished horse.

It was a patient brown gelding with one white sock and the exhausted expression of an animal that had spent three days carrying somebody else's political importance uphill. It stood at the lower-road receiving line while two grooms, a Bellisar retainer, a Crown clerk, and Tessa Rane argued over whether it belonged in the village meadow, the temporary stable yard, or nowhere within fifty paces of the lower well.

Luceran arrived in time to hear the Bellisar man say, "My lady's household was told there would be stable accommodation."

Tessa said, "There is."

He pointed uphill.

"That is not stable accommodation."

"It is a rope line, a roof on one end, and ground that drains. At present that makes it one of the better stables in Edselsto."

The clerk tried not to smile.

The Bellisar man saw him.

"You find this amusing?"

"No, sir."

"You do."

"I find Master Venn's handwriting amusing."

The receiving clerk, Master Venn, looked up from his board.

"My handwriting has housed forty-three people since dawn."

"Forty-six," Tessa corrected.

"Three are sleeping in a cart."

"They are housed in the sense that nobody has put them in somebody else's kitchen."

Luceran stopped several paces away and let them continue.

Rhyse stopped with him.

Bram and Edrik did the same.

The king had discovered that appearing in the middle of a working dispute had one predictable effect: everybody stopped solving it and started explaining it to him.

So he waited.

The Bellisar retainer said, "Lady Bellisar will pay."

Tessa's face changed by almost nothing.

"That is not the problem."

"It usually is."

"The problem is that your horses drink."

The man looked at the gelding.

Tessa pointed toward the lower well, where two masons had the capstone off and a timber brace across the mouth.

"That well is still being cleaned and reset. Upper water is for people. The west trough is supplied from the spring line and can carry another twenty horses if nobody washes there. Your household brought thirty-one."

"Twenty-eight."

"Thirty-one."

"Three belong to guests."

"They did not become imaginary because the riders are guests."

The clerk made another mark.

Luceran liked her.

That, he had learned, was dangerous.

He stepped forward.

The Bellisar man bowed immediately.

Tessa did not.

She nodded.

"Your Majesty."

"How many horses can the village take?"

"Today?"

"Yes."

"Without fouling the spring line, ruining somebody's field, or carrying buckets uphill until the servants mutiny?"

"Those seem useful qualifications."

"Fifty-seven more."

The Bellisar man said, "That is absurdly precise."

A voice from behind the well answered him.

"No. Sixty-two if the old tannery channel still runs."

Everyone turned.

A woman climbed out of the drainage cut with mud to both knees and a mattock over one shoulder. She was perhaps forty, perhaps fifty; mountain weather had made the distinction irrelevant. Her hair had been tied back with a strip of blue cloth. A narrow scar ran from her jaw toward her ear.

Tessa said, "Mara Henn."

Luceran looked at her.

The name landed.

The house.

The disputed property.

"Your Majesty."

"You own the house we nearly stole."

"One of them."

Luceran blinked.

Tessa closed her eyes briefly.

Mara added, "We own two."

"Of course you do."

"Possibly three."

Rhyse coughed into his fist.

Luceran looked at him.

Rhyse's face was innocent.

"Explain."

"The tannery channel used to take spring overflow around the south plots. My grandfather blocked half of it when the tannery closed because children kept falling into it. Open it, clear the stone throat, and the west trough can drain without turning the meadow into soup."

The Bellisar retainer asked, "How long?"

"If your people work?"

He frowned.

"If ours?"

"By afternoon."

"If Crown workers do it?"

"Tomorrow."

Luceran laughed before he could stop himself.

Mara looked almost offended.

"That was not a joke."

"I know."

That made it worse.

He said kitchens outranked laundry.

She said everyone at court could eat naked, then.

By the time Luceran heard of it, the two of them had created a schedule.

Kitchen drawing before noon.

Drinking water always.

Laundry in afternoon rotations from the spring cistern once the horse trough was moved to tannery-channel drainage.

Bathing by carried water only until the lower well reopened.

Nobody had asked him.

The system worked.

He ordered it written down.

That was becoming his most useful royal power.

At sunset the receiving line closed with one hundred and seven new people processed.

More than the messages had named.

Twenty-four slept in castle rooms.

Thirty-eight rented village beds or floor space.

Thirty-one went to marked tents.

Fourteen remained attached to wagons or household pavilions because they preferred their own arrangements.

Two households refused the offered terms and continued south to an inn on the road.

No private house had been seized.

Three arguments over boundaries remained unresolved.

The Crown staple buyer had secured bread grain, onions, beans, salt pork, and lamp oil.

Eggs remained a catastrophe.

The village had made more money in one day than in many ordinary weeks.

It had also consumed nearly two days of normal bread supply.

Tessa put both facts in the report.

Luceran read it twice.

Then he walked through the village without announcement.

Bram came.

Edrik came.

Rhyse allowed it because three guards were already ahead pretending not to be guards.

Children were playing between timber piles.

A mason drank with a villager outside the baker's.

Someone had hung laundry from a Crown survey stake.

The forge sounded from uphill.

A woman was arguing over rent.

A man who had sold his spare room looked delighted.

His neighbor looked furious because her landlord had announced an increase.

At the lower well, Mara Henn was still working.

"You are now employed by me," Luceran said.

"Tomorrow."

"Ah."

"Today I am fixing my village."

He looked into the well.

"Will it work?"

"It worked before."

"That is not the same answer."

She looked at him.

"No."

He waited.

"Stone lining on the north side is bad. We can repair it. Water table is lower than Orven remembers. If the court stays this size, you need another source before winter."

"Where?"

"Maybe the old mill spring."

"Maybe?"

"It was buried in a slide when I was a girl."

"Can it be reopened?"

"Probably."

"Whose land?"

Mara smiled.

He had learned.

They found the first grave after dark.

Not a body.

A stone.

A work crew clearing brush from the edge of the proposed overflow camp uncovered a flat marker beneath the roots.

The foreman stopped immediately.

That alone saved them from a worse mistake.

Tessa fetched Mara.

Mara fetched Elder Sovan Henn, who was eighty-two, nearly deaf, and furious that anyone had begun clearing there without asking him.

"It isn't a graveyard," the quartermaster said.

Sovan struck the ground with his stick.

"It was."

The quartermaster looked around.

There were no standing stones.

Sovan pointed into the dark.

"Flood year. Fever after. We buried six families here because the old burial field road washed out."

"What year?"

Sovan told him.

Avarren, summoned from supper, found the year in a chapel death list.

Not the burial locations.

Only the deaths.

The overflow camp moved.

It cost them twelve tent spaces.

Nobody argued after the second marker appeared.

Luceran stood at the edge of the old field while lanterns moved through the grass.

Mara came beside him.

"You didn't know," she said.

"No."

"Neither did half the village."

"Did you?"

"About the graves? Yes."

"Why wasn't it on a map?"

She looked toward the castle.

"Whose map?"

He had no answer.

The mountain above them was black against the last blue of the sky.

Edselsto's windows glowed in scattered clusters where people had finally begun living again.

From below, the castle looked less abandoned than it had a week ago.

The village looked less abandoned too.

That was not the same as healed.

Luceran understood that now.

Repair could injure.

Money could impoverish.

Work could displace.

Loyalty could remember neglect.

A house could be empty and still belong to someone.

A field could look empty and contain the dead.

A road could be forgotten by the Crown and remembered by the woman who milked before dawn.

He had come north because he wanted a place where other people's networks could not reach him.

Instead he had found a kingdom in miniature beneath his own walls.

It had property.

Memory.

Prices.

Graves.

Arguments.

And conditions.

He looked at Mara.

"Tomorrow. After you milk."

She nodded.

"Tomorrow."

Above them, seven candles burned in a nave neither of them could see.

None went out.

# Episode 10 — Guests With Patrons

# Season One, Episode 10 — Guests With Patrons

By the third morning, Master Venn had discovered that a guest could arrive alone with six patrons.

This was not physically possible.

Administratively, it was common.

He stood beneath the lower-road awning with a page from yesterday's receiving register held beside a lodging chit, a kitchen account, and a letter of introduction.

The woman in question was named Selise Varo.

The receiving register called her an independent copyist from Aster Vale.

Her room had been paid for by a factor of House Edevane.

Her cart space had been reserved by a merchant who held Morcant iron contracts.

Her letter of introduction came from a canon attached to the Hall of Veils.

Her supper had been charged to a Pell household kitchen.

"Which one is she?"

Avarren read the four documents.

"A copyist."

"That is not what I asked."

"It may be the only answer she would give."

"Then why are four other people paying for her?"

Avarren looked toward the queue.

"Ask them."

Venn did.

That was how Luceran learned there was a problem.

Not because somebody uncovered a conspiracy.

Because a receiving clerk became annoyed by paperwork.

Selise Varo was thirty-one, ink-stained, tired, and unimpressed by kings before breakfast.

Master Venn had been invited because the problem belonged to him before it belonged to the Crown.

"It is."

"Then I prefer standing."

"No."

"No."

"No."

"The Hall of Veils?"

"No."

"Then why did all of them touch your arrival?"

"Oh."

"You know."

"What did you think the question was?"

"No."

"My mother's sister married an Edevane household clerk twenty years ago. Aunt Leth wrote that rooms here were impossible and asked her husband's office whether anyone had a bed. They paid the first week because she embarrassed them into it."

The cart reservation.

"My brother-in-law works for Tollen Brass and Freight. They have Morcant contracts. One of their carts was coming north half empty."

The Hall letter.

"I copied medical testimony for Canon Serev during the river-fever inquiry. He thought the Crown archive might need trained hands and wrote the introduction."

The Pell kitchen charge.

"That one is embarrassing."

Luceran waited.

"My younger brother is courting the cook's daughter."

Master Venn closed his eyes.

Ysabet laughed.

It transformed her face so completely that Luceran almost forgot she had spent the previous days looking as though every damaged catalogue was a personal insult.

"So," Luceran said, "you are not an Edevane agent."

Selise looked at him.

"I did not say that."

The room went still.

Luceran felt Rhyse shift behind him.

Selise continued.

"I said I am not employed by them."

Avarren asked, "Would you carry information to your aunt?"

"If she asked how I was."

"Would you tell her what work you were doing?"

"Probably."

"Would she tell her husband?"

"Almost certainly."

"Would he tell his office?"

"If he thought it mattered."

Luceran said, "Would you deliberately report archive business?"

"No."

"Would you notice who enters?"

"I am a copyist."

Ysabet said, "That means yes."

Selise looked at her.

"It means everybody notices who enters. Copyists simply have the misfortune of remembering names."

Luceran stared at the papers.

There it was.

Not a spy.

A person.

Which was worse, in a way.

Spies could be arrested.

People had aunts.

"Do you want the archive position?"

"Yes."

Ysabet said, "She passed my hand test."

"You already tested her?"

"I needed copyists."

Ysabet nodded once.

Selise did not move.

Luceran said, "There is something else."

"Yes, Your Majesty."

"If you intend to exclude everyone with a patron, creditor, relative, former employer, lover, guild, temple, school, military acquaintance, landlord, client, or person who once did them a favor, you will have to copy your own records."

"No."

She got the job.

By midday Avarren had turned Master Venn's irritation into a table.

Not a secret table.

That distinction mattered.

They took the previous two days of receiving records and added only relationships already disclosed by the arriving households or visible in Crown transactions: who paid lodging, who provided transport, whose letter introduced them, which kitchen fed them, which household had requested their presence, which guild certified them, and which existing office vouched for their work.

The result was ugly.

Luceran loved it.

Not because it revealed enemies.

Because it revealed that his court was alive.

A mason arrived under Royal Works but slept in a Kaelmont-funded bunkhouse because Kaelmont had supplied the roofing slate.

A stable specialist requested by Rhyse had served Bellisar for twelve years and still had a son in their remount yards.

Two kitchen clerks came through a Pell steward because Pell had a reputation for household accounting.

A locksmith recommended by Nella belonged to the Guild of Keys, owed money to a Morcant lender, and had a sister employed by Nerezza.

A village runner had begun carrying private notes uphill because nobles tipped better than the Crown.

Three servants who appeared in the receiving book under Montegrain's household had cousins among the dismissed Royal Companions.

One of Lady Ardel Kaelmont's surveyors had been trained by a man now employed by Moncler.

"Exactly."

"No."

"Influence?"

"Sometimes."

"Sometimes."

"Sometimes."

Montegrain looked at him.

Edselsto was teaching him that ownership was the wrong model.

People listened in several directions at once.
