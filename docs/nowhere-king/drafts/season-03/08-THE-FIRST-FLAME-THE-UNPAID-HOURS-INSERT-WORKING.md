# Season Three, Episode 08 — The Unpaid Hours

**WORKING INSERT — NOT CANON.** Intended between Luceran's first inspection of the dark candle and the evening nave council in *The First Flame*. This is additional scene material, not a replacement continuity ledger or an independent episode.

The kitchen had become the place where everyone came to ask for something they had not thought to bring.

The first guard wanted a lantern. The second wanted a jug of water for the people waiting outside the family nave. The third wanted to know whether the cook could send two boys to carry benches. He had been told the hall was short of men and had assumed, as people often did, that the kitchen was full of them.

The cook stood beside the newly repaired kettle and regarded him.

"Which two?"

"Whichever you can spare."

"Then I have none."

The guard looked at Tavin, who was setting cups to dry.

"He is here."

"He is working."

Tavin kept his eyes on the cups. Alden Pell had apologized after the business at the door, and Rhyse had made it clear that servants were not playthings for a gentleman's entertainment. Neither act had prevented people from seeing an available boy wherever Tavin happened to stand.

The guard had the decency to look embarrassed.

"I was told the canon needs benches."

"Then the canon may ask the hall steward for benches and the hall steward may send the men whose business is furniture. I can give you the water."

She filled the jug. Tavin took it to the doorway, but when the guard held out a hand for him to follow, he stopped.

"Where is it going?"

"To the nave."

"Who is to receive it?"

The guard named Merel's assistant.

"I'll give it to him at the outer door," Tavin said. "Then I have to come back."

The guard frowned at the condition, not at the boy. He had expected obedience and found himself being asked to account for an ordinary journey.

"Fair enough."

It was a small victory. Tavin did not feel grander for it. He felt, for the first time since the wager, that he might finish the day's work without being asked to stand before a door and prove something.

---

Pell Falbrecht found the halted workers gathered around a brazier they had not been authorized to light.

He did not order it put out. The approach to the lower stair was closed, the air was cold, and the men were waiting for someone to tell them whether they were still employed.

"Vess sent us away," one said.

"From the stair," Pell replied.

"From our work."

The distinction was true and useless to a man who had been promised a day's wage for a day's labor.

Tomas arrived carrying a board of ordinary work assignments. He had come to see whether the men could be moved to the upper yard. The answer was that some could, if their tools were fetched from the barred approach, and the rest could not be put safely to another trade merely because the Crown had paid for their time.

"Write their names," Pell told him.

"They are in the works book."

"Write where they were stopped. If a man is sent away for his own safety, do not let a clerk later call him absent."

Tomas set the board on a dry stone and began.

One of the younger men had bruised his heel against the timber bar when the floor shivered. He could walk, but he did so carefully, and Pell sent him to have the injury seen rather than allowing him to demonstrate that it was nothing. The man objected because a visit to the physician sounded like the beginning of a deduction.

"You will not be charged for obeying an order to leave a dangerous place," Pell said. "If anyone proposes it, bring him to me."

He could promise that much on his own authority. He could not promise that the work would resume tomorrow.

Vess came down from the closed approach with his inked measurements in a leather folder. He had asked for another mason who had not seen the marks. That man was still working at the upper cistern and would come at first light, when the lamps would not distort the line.

"Can you tell them what happened?" Pell asked.

"No."

"Can you tell them whether the wall is safe?"

"Not yet."

Pell looked at the waiting men. An honest answer had cost him nothing to say and would cost the household a day of wages, perhaps more. He disliked both facts, but not enough to send a man down the stair to save face.

"Then put them on the account," he told Tomas.

The brazier burned on. It would need charcoal entered too.

---

Damaris was adding figures when Miren came to her table.

The steward had drawn four columns, though only one contained a sum. Gate watch belonged to the gate. Hall watch belonged to the household. Men kept from the stair belonged to the works. The nave had its own needs, and the canon had not yet asked the Crown to purchase anything for the rite.

"Someone has asked whether the creditor must bear the cost," Damaris said.

"Of the candle?"

"Of the men we put beside it. A more useful question."

Miren read the headings. "And?"

"If Rhyse takes men from the hall for an evening, the hall must account for their hours. If he posts additional men at the gate, Bessa will account for those. We cannot call every frightened guard a gate expense because Jorvan is entitled to see the deductions."

"Nor can we leave the nave unattended."

"No. The arithmetic does not forbid the work. It prevents us from pretending it is free."

A messenger arrived with a request that the sickroom send someone who could speak about the old family candles. Miren read it twice. No name was written, only a suggestion that the household had seen stranger things than a dark wick.

"Jalen did not see it," she said.

"Then he has nothing to attest."

"Someone will say his body gives him special knowledge."

"Does it?"

"Not of a candle."

Miren returned the note with that answer, signed in her own hand. She did not ask Jalen to refuse a summons he had never received. Nor did she turn his private medical record into evidence for a question it could not answer.

Damaris marked a line beneath the columns.

"His Majesty will ask what the inquiry costs."

"And you will tell him?"

"What we know. Then what we owe. The rest can wait for a man who has actually counted it."

Miren watched the steward close the book. She wanted to go to Luceran and put a hand on his shoulder. She also wanted him to understand that her comfort was not the same thing as agreement, and that the dead candle had not made his old trespasses less real.

She could do the first without surrendering the second.

Outside, the fiddler's last tune faded from the yard. The castle had not answered the question everyone was asking. Inside its working rooms, living people had begun answering the smaller questions that could actually be settled.
