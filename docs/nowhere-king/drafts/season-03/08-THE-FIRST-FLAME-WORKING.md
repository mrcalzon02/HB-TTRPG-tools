# Season Three, Episode 08 — The First Flame

The crack was narrower than the mason's thumbnail, which was one reason Vess disliked it.

A wide break gave a man something honest to measure. This one had appeared beside the sealed lower service stair after the rain, and yesterday's marks had been made in charcoal on a wall that had been wet enough to blur them. The workers who had heard the scraping were certain the stone had moved. The men who had not heard it were equally certain the rain had made an old joint complain.

Vess had brought a plumb line, a straight timber and a second man to hold the lantern. Pell Falbrecht had sent two of his own workers, not because either could diagnose ancient foundations, but because they had been the men who barred the passage and would be blamed if someone fell through it.

"Do not pull that timber," Vess told the younger one.

"It's only the bar."

"It is the bar keeping a curious gentleman from walking into a place we have not inspected. That makes it a very useful bar."

He set the plumb line against the outer face. The weight hung a little off the old dressing mark. It might always have hung there. No one had measured this wall in living memory with the precision now being demanded of it.

The lantern bearer moved his hand and the light changed. For an instant Vess thought the line had shifted.

"Hold still."

"I am."

There came a sound too low to belong to a falling tool. It was not loud. It travelled through the soles of their boots, rose into their knees and passed away. Dust lifted from the joint below the sealed stair. The weight at the end of Vess's line touched the stone once, then hung free.

One of Pell's men took a step backward and struck the bar with his heel. The timber held.

"Was that the mountain?" he asked.

Vess had no answer worth giving. He marked the position of the line in ink on a dry slip, wrote the hour as closely as the watch bell allowed, and ordered both men out of the approach. They would leave the bar where it stood. No one would open the descent to discover whether the sound had come from beneath it.

He had been hired to repair a house, not to sacrifice apprentices to its curiosity.

---

The village was enjoying a modest success of its own.

The baker had bought her roof tile with the money from Orlen's meetings. It stood against the wall behind her counter, a good sound piece of fired clay that she showed to everyone who would look. Her husband had promised to put it in place as soon as the weather allowed. She knew better than to call the roof repaired before he climbed it.

Orlen came for bread with his revised quotation in his pocket. He had begun to dislike that quotation. It told the truth about the cost of hauling iron to the upper yard, and the truth made his offer less handsome. Edda Kelm's measurements were still with her own road office. She had given him no permission to improve the road, and he had been careful not to suggest otherwise.

"Are you celebrating?" the baker asked.

"Only that I can now explain why I charge what I charge."

"That is more than most men manage."

He bought a second loaf and left it for the hauler who had lost his morning to a wheel pin. It was not charity. A man whose cart broke on the mountain could ruin a supplier's reputation while both remained entirely innocent.

Up at the castle, the returned kitchen kettle had received its new rivet. The cook tested it with cold water before allowing anyone to set it above a flame. Tavin was the one who carried it back to the scullery. No noble stopped him to ask whether the door liked him.

A paid fiddler played by the receiving yard while workers took their midday bread. The musician had come with a cart from the village and had agreed to two tunes in return for his meal. No royal festival had been declared. The sound of it nevertheless drew people to the windows.

Miren stood at one of those windows with Yara. Below, the baker's husband had come up to deliver a parcel and was arguing amiably with a porter over whether a single tile counted as a building material worth entering in the yard book.

"People are beginning to like living here," Yara said.

"Some of them," Miren answered.

It was not a correction so much as an allowance for the people who had no choice.

---

Canon Merel had counted the candles twice that morning.

He always did. The seven ancestral flames had survived wars, neglected reigns, quarrels over the proper order of prayers, and the practical inconvenience of a nave whose roof still required work. He did not know why the first of them was set slightly apart from the others. A previous canon had called it a matter of ceremonial precedence. Another had insisted the spacing marked an old royal mourning.

Merel had inherited both explanations and trusted neither very much.

His young assistant was cleaning the brass rail when the floor gave a small, unpleasant shiver. A sound followed it, like a great door closing at a distance too far away to judge.

The assistant caught the rail.

"Did you feel that?"

"Yes."

The first flame thinned. Merel watched it draw down to a point of light hardly larger than the head of a pin. There was no gust against his face, and the other six stood straight. He moved one hand toward the candle, stopped, and watched the wick darken.

It did not flare again.

The assistant whispered a prayer. Merel found that he could not remember which prayer ought to come first. He could remember the ordinary ones perfectly. None had been written for a candle which had burned longer than the lives of the men who tended it and had now gone out in front of him.

"Fetch the nave guard," he said. "Then fetch a second witness. Do not touch the candle."

"Shall I light it?"

"No."

The boy looked at the six remaining flames. "What if the others—"

"We do not know what has happened to this one. We shall not pretend to know what to do to the others."

Merel closed the nave doors to casual visitors. He left them unbolted for the living guards and the household officers who had authority to enter. Then he set down the hour, the position of the wick and the names of the two people who had seen the light die.

He had been a priest long enough to know the difference between a sign and an explanation.

---

Rhyse found Vess at the barred stair with dust still on his sleeves.

The captain brought Dain and two living guards. Iven Marrick was already waiting beyond the nearest authorized threshold. He had not crossed the bar or offered to go below it. He had been asked to keep people away from the work, a task he could perform without knowing why the stone had complained.

"Tell me what moved," Rhyse said.

"The line touched the wall," Vess answered. "I can attest to that. I cannot attest that the wall moved. A man can shake a weight without meaning to."

"Did you?"

"I do not believe so."

Dain crouched to inspect the inked mark. The crack beside the sealed descent was not visibly wider than it had been the day before. In the corner, however, a little cone of clean grit had fallen over yesterday's boot prints. Rain could not have washed it there. The stair approach was dry.

One of Pell's men had bruised his heel stepping back against the bar. Vess had already sent him to sit down and have it looked at. He had also stopped the adjoining work gang. The wages of those men would not stop merely because the stones had become interesting.

"We can take down the bar and see whether the stair has settled," Dain said.

"We can," Vess replied. "And then the first man down may learn what the rest of us do not know."

Rhyse studied the sealed stone. He remembered other times when Edselsto had answered questions about old repairs and made itself seem like a particularly obliging steward. He also remembered how often a living hand had found a crack the house had not mentioned.

"I want another mason who has not seen your marks," he said. "Let him take his own measurements before you show him yours. The approach stays closed. Iven, you need not volunteer for an inspection you were never engaged to make."

Iven inclined his head. "I had not intended to."

There was a relief in the answer that Rhyse did not mistake for cowardice.

A runner arrived from the nave, breathless enough that his first attempt at speech produced no words. He carried Merel's sealed note and the names of the witnesses. Rhyse read it once. Then he looked back at the bar, the line, the clean grit.

The coincidence was serious. It was not yet an explanation.

---

Luceran heard the news while the fiddler was still playing in the yard.

He had been reading Orlen's revised prices with Tomas and Damaris. The measured cost was greater than the first estimate; that, at least, was a problem with numbers he could argue about. When Merel's note arrived, he set the quotation aside and read the words in silence.

"One candle," he said.

"One witnessed extinction," Damaris corrected gently. "That is what the canon has reported."

"Do you suppose I cannot count?"

"I suppose that if we speak of what we know, we may have something left to stand on when everyone else begins speaking of what they fear."

Tomas asked whether he should call all the masons off the upper yard. Luceran nearly agreed. It would have been an order everyone could obey, and obedience was an appealing substitute for knowledge.

"Only the approaches Vess has barred," he said instead. "Keep the ordinary repairs where they are safe."

The king went to the nave with Rhyse. The first candle was dark. Its wick was black, and a thin rim of wax stood where the flame had been. Merel had placed no new light beside it. The other six burned without disturbance.

Luceran asked Edselsto what had happened.

The reply came after a pause long enough for him to feel foolish in front of the canon.

"I cannot give you that answer, my king."

It was an answer the house had given before in smaller matters. Here it felt intolerable.

"You remember the old stone."

"I have memories of works and passages. I cannot tell you why this flame has died."

Merel did not dispute the voice. He also did not bow to it as an oracle. "We must keep the wick as it is," he said. "Someone will ask us tomorrow whether we relit it and whether it failed again. We should be able to answer honestly."

Luceran wanted to demand a new light. The desire embarrassed him. It was not the darkness that offended him most, but the sight of a thing he had always assumed would obey its nature refusing to do so.

"Keep the six," he told Merel. "Do not relight the first. Record everyone who has entered since the hour you named."

"Visitors to the nave, sire?"

"Yes. Not suspects. Witnesses, if there are any."

Rhyse heard the distinction and made certain the guards did too.

---

Jalen learned of the candle from a servant who had come to change the water at his bedside.

The servant tried to soften the report. Six remained, he said, and the priests were attending to them. Jalen asked which had died. The servant did not know. It was a better answer than an invented one.

Yara arrived with the physician's ordinary notes and found Jalen watching the window.

"Do they want me in the nave?" he asked.

"Nobody has asked me to bring you."

"They will."

"Perhaps. They have asked a great many people to explain things they did not see."

He turned the page of the book on his lap without reading it. He had already been asked to stand for too many propositions that other people wanted settled: who he had been, what his body proved, which man had consented to what, which foreign house ought to believe which royal letter. He had made himself useful to some of those questions. He would not be made into their answer.

"I did not see a candle die," he said.

"I know."

"And I do not know why it died."

"I know that too."

Miren came after the physician had left. She brought news that the kitchen kettle was finally back in service, an absurdly ordinary thing to say on such an afternoon. Jalen laughed once, not because it was funny but because he had needed to hear of something that could be mended by a rivet.

Miren sat by the window. Yara remained long enough to tell them that the Dols packet had not changed its narrow offer to receive authorized evidence. The road keeper still had not accepted the wall. The dead candle had not issued a foreign judgment, opened a road or authorized Jalen's private medical record to travel.

After Yara went, Miren listened to the faint music from the yard. The fiddler had begun a slower tune. Jalen asked her whether Luceran would be frightened.

"Yes," she said.

"Would he admit it?"

"That I do not know."

She did not offer to make the king brave by forgiving him. She was no longer willing to purchase his peace with her own silence.

---

By evening the nave had become the most crowded room in the castle and the one in which almost nobody dared raise a voice.

Merel wanted the first candle left untouched and the six remaining flames attended by living witnesses. Vess wanted his measurements repeated by an independent mason before anyone moved the sealed stair. A visiting scholar wished to compare the nave's old inscriptions with the family inventories. Damaris wanted to know who would pay the men being kept from work and who would pay for any new guard.

A noble suggested that a rival house had arranged the extinction as a warning. Rhyse asked him for the name of a witness who had seen a hand touch the candle. He had none. The accusation went no farther in the official account, though it would travel far enough in private.

Another insisted the dark wick was divine judgment on the king. Merel answered that a priest could call for prayer without claiming to know which sin heaven had chosen to name.

Luceran listened. He had expected the room to divide between those who trusted Edselsto and those who did not. Instead he found people who disagreed about money, authority, evidence, ritual and the limits of their own trades. None of those disagreements made the others imaginary.

"How many men?" he asked Vess.

"Two masons for the first measures, a third to witness, and the laborers to clear a safe standing place. We will need timber for bracing if the second measurements agree. I will not name the timber before I see where it must bear."

Damaris opened her small book. "The winter linen and minor roof reserve is already spoken for by the hall. We cannot spend it twice."

"Then the upper gallery hangings wait," Luceran said. "No new display cloth until we have paid for the measures and the men kept idle by the barrier. Tomas may draw up the order in daylight. Nothing from the Jorvan gate pledge."

It was not enough money to rebuild a mountain. It was enough to begin without pretending that a royal command made labor free.

Merel asked for the authority to preserve the candle exactly as witnessed. Luceran gave it. Rhyse asked for the right to keep the lower stair closed even against curious noble guests. Luceran gave that too, with a sharpness which would make at least one guest resent him.

The inquiry would have separate books: Vess's measurements, Merel's nave account, Rhyse's access and witness record, Damaris's expenditure. They would be compared, not made identical by decree.

When the room emptied, Luceran remained beside the rail. The six lights gave him enough illumination to see the seventh wick.

He had inherited those flames as he had inherited the crown: not knowing what had been done to keep them burning, only that they had always burned before.

For the first time, the dark space among them looked less like a loss of ceremony than the beginning of a debt nobody had yet learned to price.
