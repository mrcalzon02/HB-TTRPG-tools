# The Morning Nobody Owned

*The Nowhere King — optional Maera village-path scene-study, at the S3E17–S3E18 continuity boundary.*  
**UNPUBLISHED / NONCANONICAL — creator-review candidate — 20 November 2026.** Not an added episode, not inserted into finished S3E17 or S3E18, and not a commission for S3E19.

The first cart came down from the coppice with wet bark stacked higher than the driver's shoulders. Its mule wanted the wider turn beside the gate. The boy at the latch stood back to give it room, then planted both feet when the iron catch refused to rise.

"Lift before you pull," Maera told him.

He did. The gate opened with a complaint like a saw through green wood.

"Will it be fixed before the king comes?"

"Has he said he is coming?"

"The reeve has a paper."

"The reeve has permission to give, not a royal footstep to count."

Maera shifted her bundle of oven brush against her hip. She had cut it that morning because the baker's fire did not wait for court business. If she guided a party uphill, someone else would have to gather it. The household had agreed to reserve her wage and a weather-cancellation portion, but reserved coin was not coin in her hand.

The driver leaned down from his cart. "If they want this road tomorrow, I'll take the mule round the lower field."

"You'll sink there," Maera said.

"I can lose an hour."

"You can lose a wheel. The permission says you keep the turn until your work is done."

"Kings don't much like waiting behind a cart of bark."

"Then somebody should tell them how long bark takes."

The driver gave her a narrow look, then laughed despite himself. He brought the mule forward. The boy held the gate with both hands until the last branch cleared. No royal procession appeared to be delayed by a road that was being used for its ordinary purpose.

Beyond the turn, rainwater slid over the first stones of the uphill path. Maera tested the edge with her staff. Soft ground gave beneath the ferrule. A fine morning at the village door told her nothing about the crossing above the fallen beech.

"Could you take them as far as the bend?" the boy asked.

"I could choose to stop before the bend. I could choose not to start."

"Even if the seal says you must?"

"The seal says I may refuse dangerous weather. The reeve heard it read. So did you."

The boy considered that. "What if they say the road looks dry?"

"Then I will ask whether they have put their boots on it."

She bent to inspect the latch, but did not hammer at the pin or promise a smith's work. It belonged to the village, and opening it properly was not the same as repairing it. She showed the boy where the metal caught and told him to report the trouble to the reeve in his own words, not hers.

When the mule had passed, Maera lifted the brush again. There might be a royal walk on some later daylight; there might be rain enough to cancel it. Neither possibility had bought this morning.

Behind her the gate creaked shut. The baker still needed his fire.
