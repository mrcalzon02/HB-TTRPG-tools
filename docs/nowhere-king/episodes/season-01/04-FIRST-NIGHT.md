# Nowhere King — Season One, Episode Four
## First Night

**Status:** Committed episode prose  
**Season:** One — The Return to Edselsto  
**Episode:** 04 of 32  
**Reveal ceiling:** First lived occupation of Edselsto. Seven candles may be observed with all seven lit. Strange acoustics, drafts, old mechanisms, sealed rooms, and voices may be experienced only in ways that remain plausibly mundane. No sentient-castle proof, containment truth, candle failure, or buried-entity terminology.  
**Primary dramatic purpose:** Turn Edselsto from a political idea into a cold, damaged, inhabited place. Make Luceran's first night about labor, exhaustion, household improvisation, old neglect, and the people who kept the ancestral seat standing without him. Let the first uncanny note enter quietly enough that nobody has earned certainty.

---

The village of Edselsto had three streets.

Luceran had expected more.

That was his first disappointment.

His second was that one of the streets had mostly become grass.

The third was that the people waiting in the rain did not look remotely impressed by him.

They stood beneath roofs darkened by weather, outside low stone houses with patched shutters and smoke crawling from crooked chimneys. A cooper, two goat keepers, several children, an old woman with a basket, men carrying axes, women with aprons pulled over their heads, one priest, one boy holding a chicken, and a black dog with the kind of expression normally reserved for tax collectors.

The royal convoy slowed.

Luceran looked up the mountain.

Edselsto Castle stood above the village in black stone and wet pine, too large for the settlement beneath it and too old for everything around it. Towers broke the ridge line at different heights. One appeared sound. Another leaned enough to make Luceran suspicious of perspective. Slate roofs stepped down toward the eastern wall. The western side was mostly shadow and scaffolding that had not existed three days earlier.

A single banner hung above the gate.

Royal black and silver.

It looked absurdly new.

Montegrain rode beside him.

"Home."

Luceran looked at the castle.

"Do not say it like that."

"Like what?"

"Like you expect something to happen."

"I expect you to become wet."

"I am already wet."

"Then the prophecy begins."

Bram Tolland, riding ahead, glanced back.

"Road narrows after the mill, Majesty."

"How badly?"

"Two wagons abreast until the shrine. One after."

Luceran looked at Edrik.

"And if I say we push all of them up tonight?"

Edrik considered.

"I would ask Sergeant Tolland to answer."

"Traitor."

"Learning delegation, Majesty."

Bram heard and smiled without turning.

That pleased Luceran more than it should have.

The convoy entered the village.

No trumpets sounded.

No bells had been arranged.

A girl of perhaps nine saw Luceran looking at her and curtseyed several seconds too late, with so much concentration that she nearly stepped into a drainage ditch.

Luceran nodded to her.

She looked astonished that it worked.

Then an old man stepped into the road.

He wore a dark wool coat that had been repaired so many times it had become an argument between fabrics. His hair was white, his shoulders still broad, and he carried a ring of keys heavy enough to be useful as a weapon.

Sir Edran Vey stood beside him.

Mara Kest stood three paces back under a cloak, looking tired enough to murder civilization.

Luceran dismounted.

The old man did not kneel.

Everyone noticed.

He bowed.

Barely.

"Your Majesty."

Luceran looked at him.

"Orven Dast."

"Unfortunately."

Montegrain coughed.

Luceran almost smiled.

"Sir Edran wrote that you said it was about bloody time."

Orven looked at Edran.

Edran had the decency not to look ashamed.

"I said worse."

"Good."

Orven studied Luceran more openly than any courtier in the capital would have dared.

Not insolently.

Professionally.

As though deciding whether a roof repair had been done properly.

"You look like your mother around the eyes."

Luceran's amusement vanished.

Orven saw it.

He did not apologize.

"She hated this place," he said.

Luceran blinked.

"What?"

"First time she came, rained for six days. Her boots split. She told your father no sane person built a house on a mountain."

Montegrain looked away.

Luceran swallowed.

"That sounds like her."

"It was."

A beat.

Then Orven turned toward the mountain.

"You can mourn later. Horses need cover."

That was how the king of Embar-Zara was welcomed to his ancestral home.

---

The village did not have enough stable space.

The castle did.

This would have been useful if the upper stable roof had not partly failed.

Edran had therefore turned the village green into an argument involving ropes, wagons, feed sacks, three temporary picket lines, two mule teams, and a furious Bellisar groom who insisted the loaned remounts were not to spend the night standing in mud.

Household hierarchy collapsed under rain.

The royal physician carried a crate because nobody else had hands.

A treasury clerk held a lantern for a carpenter.

Brother Calven helped unload onions.

Bram stood at Luceran's shoulder until Rhyse arrived and said, "Go ahead with the first guard file."

Bram nodded.

Edrik remained.

Luceran noticed.

"You are not going?"

"One of us stays where you are."

"Rhyse's rule?"

"Ours now."

Luceran looked at him.

There was something restful about being guarded by men who treated him as a moving object with blood inside it.

No compliments.

No whispered offers.

No questions about how he was sleeping.

Only position.

Orven barked at a wagon driver who had turned too sharply beside the old stone trough.

"Not there."

The driver shouted back, "Why?"

"Because underneath that mud is a drain built before your grandmother's grandmother and if you crack it, the lower kitchen floods."

The driver moved.

Luceran looked at Orven.

"You know every drain?"

"No."

"How many?"

"Enough to resent the ones I don't."

Mara Kest appeared at Luceran's elbow.

"Majesty."

"Mara."

She looked him over the way housekeepers inspected damaged furniture.

"You brought too many people."

Luceran stared.

"That has been said."

"You brought three wagons of things nobody needs tonight."

"Which?"

"Silver service."

Luceran looked toward the convoy.

"I did not order silver service."

"Nobody ever does. It reproduces."

"Can we leave it in the wagon?"

"Already did."

"Then why tell me?"

"So you know the household is still diseased."

Luceran laughed.

Mara did not.

"I am serious."

"I know."

She pointed up the road.

"Kitchen one is usable. Kitchen two will be usable after Corren stops the chimney from trying to kill us. Main well works. We are boiling everything. East sleeping rooms are dry enough. Great hall will take overflow if people accept blankets and dignity loss."

"West wing?"

"No."

"South gallery?"

"No."

"Family rooms?"

Orven, three paces away, turned his head.

Mara noticed.

"Ask him."

Luceran did.

Orven jingled the keys.

"Locked."

"All of them?"

"Most."

"Why?"

"Because nobody lived there."

"That is not an answer."

"It is the reason."

Luceran looked at the castle again.

Rain ran black down its walls.

"Can they be opened?"

"Some."

"And the rest?"

"With axes."

"I brought carpenters."

Orven looked toward the wagons containing half of Lord Moncler's construction site.

"So I heard."

There was no admiration in it.

Only judgment deferred.

---

The upper road began at the old mill.

One wagon at a time.

No argument.

Even Luceran did not argue after seeing it.

The road cut back across the slope through black pines, their roots forcing old paving stones upward in long ridges. Water ran down the wheel tracks. Moss filled the edges. The mountain smelled of wet bark, earth, iron, smoke, and something mineral Luceran could not name.

He rode near the front.

Edrik stayed close.

Bram ranged ahead with Rhyse and a handful of guards.

Behind came workers, household staff, food carts, archive chests, tools, bedding, medicine, treasury coin, and the things monarchy insisted upon even while pretending to travel light.

The village shrank below.

Luceran looked back.

More wagons waited in the dusk.

The first train had not even reached the gate and already Edselsto had traffic.

Montegrain caught his look.

"Your empty mountain seems crowded."

"Go away."

"You brought me."

"That was before you became irritating."

"I was irritating when we met."

"Then I was naïve."

"Progress."

Luceran almost told him to shut up.

Then the gatehouse appeared.

It was not majestic.

It was enormous.

Those were different things.

The outer walls had been built from dark stone fitted so tightly that the joints were difficult to see even under lichen. The gate towers rose square and blunt rather than graceful. Murder holes opened over the passage. Rust stained the chains where an old portcullis mechanism had been exposed for repair.

Scaffolding clung to one side.

Men from Edran's advance party had erected lanterns.

The royal banner hung above all of it like an apology.

Orven stopped beneath the arch.

Luceran rode through.

Nothing happened.

That annoyed him.

He had not expected anything to happen.

He was annoyed anyway.

---

The first courtyard was larger than the palace square in the capital and far less useful.

Grass grew between stones.

A disused fountain stood dry in the center, filled with pine needles and a dead branch.

One side housed the stable complex.

Another had workshops.

The main keep rose beyond, connected to lower halls and later additions built in different eras by people who had apparently disliked one another's sense of proportion.

Master Olan Crewe stopped dead.

Jory Fell nearly walked into him.

"What?"

Olan stared upward.

"That roof."

Jory looked.

"What about it?"

"It is lying."

Derrin Voss came up beside them.

"Roofs do that?"

"This one is pretending it is straight."

Derrin squinted.

"Ah."

Luceran overheard.

"What does that mean?"

Olan did not seem pleased to be asked.

"Means the line is wrong, Majesty."

"I can see that."

"No. The wall is wrong too."

He pointed.

"See the third window from the corner?"

Luceran did not.

He said so.

Olan led him several steps.

"Now."

The upper line sagged just enough to become visible.

Jory said, "Beam could be gone."

Derrin said, "Stone has shifted."

Iven Marr, the carpenter from the advance party, came out of a side door.

"Both."

Olan looked at him.

Iven looked back.

The two carpenters had the immediate hostility of craftsmen who had not yet established which of them was entitled to be right first.

"You went inside?" Olan asked.

"Three days ago."

"And?"

"Bad principal timber. Worse patching. Somebody put decorative casing over rot fifteen years ago and called it maintenance."

Orven said, "Twenty-two."

Iven turned.

"What?"

"Twenty-two years ago."

"You remember?"

"I paid for it."

"Why?"

"Because the Crown sent a man with a letter saying repair funds would arrive the following spring."

Luceran looked at him.

"Did they?"

Orven looked at Luceran.

"No."

That landed.

No accusation.

No performance.

Just twenty-two years.

Luceran looked at the roof again.

His family had owned this place the entire time.

Someone had written letters.

Someone had promised money.

The old man had paid for a rotten beam to be hidden because waiting for the Crown had stopped being practical.

Luceran said, "Replace it properly."

Olan nodded.

Orven said, "With what timber?"

Luceran turned.

Orven pointed toward the forest.

"Royal forest. Old charter says castle repairs take fallen black pine before live cutting. Half the fallen timber is wet. The saw pit hasn't run properly in twelve years."

Luceran stared.

Orven stared back.

Montegrain murmured, "Home."

Luceran elbowed him.

---

The great hall had once been beautiful.

It remained large.

That was not the same thing.

The eastern wall bore water stains. Two long windows had been boarded from outside. Smoke from newly lit braziers drifted badly until servants opened an upper vent. Old banners hung from rafters, faded nearly colorless.

But it was dry.

Mostly.

That made it valuable.

Mara had turned one side into a sorting floor.

Blankets.

Bedrolls.

Kitchen stores.

Medical chests.

Clerk boxes.

Guard equipment.

A stack of folded tables that had somehow survived the road while one archive chest lost a hinge.

Luceran stood in the center.

Everyone immediately began asking him where things should go.

He hated this within minutes.

"Majesty, treasury chests?"

"Somewhere locked."

"Majesty, Privy Seal?"

"Near me."

"Majesty, physicians?"

"Near everyone."

"Majesty, senior household?"

"I don't know."

"Majesty, Montegrain?"

Luceran looked at Montegrain.

"Roof."

Montegrain smiled.

"Excellent view."

Avarren arrived with mud on his hem and no remaining reverence for architecture.

"We need temporary assignments before dark."

"Then assign them."

"You are the king."

"Today I am wet."

"That does not suspend monarchy."

Luceran pointed at him.

"You have rooms."

"I have no rooms. I have a corridor somebody claims becomes rooms after the third turn."

"Take one."

"Which?"

"I don't care."

Avarren's expression changed.

Luceran recognized it too late.

"Do not."

"Majesty, the first rooms you assign in a new royal seat—"

"No."

"—will be interpreted—"

"No."

"—as precedence."

Luceran looked around the hall.

Workers carrying mud.

Servants carrying bedding.

Guards marking doors.

A physician shouting that a room with mold did not become a sickroom merely because it was near the kitchen.

He put both hands over his face.

"This is exactly what I left."

Montegrain, unhelpfully, said, "No. This has more rain."

Luceran pointed at Mara.

"You."

She stopped.

"Majesty?"

"You know what people actually need."

"Often."

"Assign rooms for tonight."

Mara stared at him.

"By need?"

"Yes."

"Not rank?"

"Yes."

Avarren inhaled.

Luceran looked at him.

"One night."

Avarren said nothing.

Luceran continued.

"Physicians near the great hall. Treasury somewhere lockable. Archives somewhere dry. Guards near entrances. Workers near whichever walls they intend to attack in the morning. Senior council somewhere they can find me without sleeping in my bed."

Mara's mouth twitched.

"And nobles?"

"Dry if possible."

"Which nobles?"

"Any."

Avarren said, "This will become a problem tomorrow."

"Then tomorrow can have it."

Mara nodded.

"Good."

She turned.

"Frederick!"

A footman nearly dropped a blanket.

"Yes?"

"Get chalk."

"For what?"

"We are inventing the court."

Luceran closed his eyes.

Somewhere, Avarren began laughing.

---

The first room assigned to the Royal Treasury had once been a linen closet.

This offended Nerezza's clerk.

The room had one door, no window, dry stone walls, iron hooks, and a lock Orven said had been replaced within living memory.

It was therefore excellent.

The clerk objected anyway.

"The Treasury cannot be housed in a linen closet."

Mara looked at the chests.

"Why?"

"The dignity—"

"Can dignity pick a lock?"

"No."

"Then the money sleeps here."

The physicians received two chambers beside the great hall and complained only about ventilation.

The archives went into an old counting room above the inner kitchen where the floor was level and Orven swore the roof had never leaked.

The Privy Seal received a narrow chamber once used by a hunting secretary.

Clavard got a room containing a desk missing one leg.

He propped it on a ledger chest.

"I have become furniture."

Jory passed carrying planks.

"You have a roof."

Clavard looked at him.

"Excellent point."

Jory kept walking.

That was the first time a carpenter addressed the king's financial aide as though both were simply men trying to survive the same building.

Neither noticed.

Later, both would.

---

The kitchens were worse.

One was usable.

One could become usable.

One smelled like something had died inside the chimney and acquired legal title to it.

Mara put Jory and Iven in the second kitchen because the smoke hood had pulled away from the wall.

Olan objected.

"I need Fell at the sleeping wing."

Mara did not turn around.

"People can sleep cold. They cannot eat smoke."

Olan looked at Jory.

Jory looked at Olan.

Iven looked at both.

"She's right," Jory said.

Olan pointed.

"You are becoming household."

"I was stolen by the king yesterday. I have no identity left."

Derrin laughed from the doorway.

"Carpenter."

"Thank you."

Corren Bale, the advance mason, appeared carrying a bucket of mortar.

"Olan."

"Corren."

They regarded one another.

Mara looked between four craftsmen.

"If this becomes a mating display, do it outside."

Derrin choked.

Jory began laughing so hard he had to put down the brace.

Mara pointed toward the chimney.

"Fix."

They fixed it.

---

Luceran found his room because Bram was standing outside it.

That was already strange.

At the capital there had always been people outside his rooms.

Pages.

Companions.

Ushers.

Petition clerks.

People waiting to be seen.

Bram was doing nothing except watching the corridor.

Luceran stopped.

"This is mine?"

"For tonight."

"Who decided?"

"Mara."

"What was it?"

Orven answered from behind him.

"Your grandfather's west guest chamber."

Luceran turned.

"Guest?"

"You are currently a guest."

Luceran stared.

Orven jingled his keys and kept walking.

Bram looked straight ahead.

Luceran pointed after the steward.

"Does he speak to everyone like that?"

"Don't know, Majesty."

"Helpful."

"I arrived today."

Luceran opened the door.

The room contained a bed, washstand, small hearth, two chairs, one narrow window, a wardrobe with no doors, and three portraits turned face-first against the wall.

Luceran stopped.

"Why are those turned around?"

Bram leaned in without entering.

"Do you want me to look?"

"No."

Luceran walked over.

The nearest portrait showed a woman in dark hunting clothes holding a hawk.

He did not know her.

The second showed a boy in court dress.

The third had been damaged by damp.

Orven appeared again because apparently the castle generated him when questions were asked.

"Storage."

Luceran looked at him.

"These are family portraits."

"Yes."

"Why face the wall?"

"Sun."

"There is no sun."

"There was before the shutters warped."

Luceran looked at the window.

Orven shrugged.

"People move things for reasons that stop being visible later."

That sentence stayed.

Luceran looked at the boy in the portrait again.

"Who is he?"

"Don't know."

"How can you not know?"

"Because I am steward, not a genealogy."

"You have lived here how long?"

"Forty-seven years."

"And you don't know every portrait?"

"No."

Luceran was oddly reassured.

Orven had limits.

Good.

"Where did my father sleep?"

Orven's face changed.

Not much.

"Family wing."

"Which room?"

"North suite."

"Can I see it?"

"Tomorrow."

Luceran stiffened.

"Why tomorrow?"

"Because the corridor is cold, the shutters are bad, the floor before the old nursery needs checking, and you have been awake since before dawn."

"I am king."

"Yes."

"I can go."

"Yes."

Luceran waited.

Orven did not move.

"You're telling me not to."

"I am telling you that if you break your neck tonight, I will have to explain to the realm that the first royal act at Edselsto in forty years was falling through a floor."

Bram looked at the ceiling.

Luceran knew he was hiding something.

"Are you laughing?"

"No, Majesty."

"Good."

Orven turned.

"Tomorrow."

Luceran looked toward the dark corridor beyond.

For once, he let it go.

---

The nave was discovered accidentally.

Not by Luceran.

By Brother Calven, who went looking for a chapel and found a locked oak door beside an old passage lined with cracked devotional tiles.

Orven had the key.

"Still consecrated?" Calven asked.

"Depends who you ask."

"I am asking you."

"I am not a priest."

"That has never stopped anyone before."

Orven opened the door.

The air inside smelled of wax, dust, cold stone, and old smoke.

The nave was small compared with the great hall but more intact than Luceran expected. Seven narrow windows stood along one wall, each glazed in darkened glass. An altar of black stone sat beneath a carved royal crest older than the version used in the capital.

And before the altar, in a wrought iron stand, burned seven candles.

All the same height.

All steady.

Luceran stopped.

"Those were in Edran's report?"

"No," Calven said.

Edran, behind them, frowned.

"I didn't think candles qualified as structural."

Orven said, "They don't."

Calven approached.

"Who lights them?"

Orven.

"Who replaces them?"

Orven.

"How often?"

"When needed."

Calven looked closer.

"They are unusually large."

"They are candles."

Luceran looked at the iron stand.

Seven.

His family had seven in several older ceremonial traditions.

Seven founding companions.

Seven ancestral oaths.

Seven early provinces depending on which schoolmaster wanted to be difficult.

"Meaning?"

Orven shrugged.

"Family tradition."

Calven looked offended.

"You have maintained a seven-candle dynastic rite for forty-seven years without knowing what it means?"

"I have maintained a room with seven candles because the steward before me told me the room was to have seven candles."

Calven stared.

Orven stared back.

"This is how traditions survive priests."

Montegrain laughed.

Calven did not.

Luceran stepped nearer.

The flames moved slightly as the door closed.

One leaned.

Then righted.

Nothing else happened.

He had expected—he did not know what he had expected.

Recognition.

Memory.

Some feeling that this place was his.

Instead there was wax.

Cold stone.

A draft.

He found that disappointing too.

Then comforting.

"Keep them lit," he said.

Orven raised one eyebrow.

"I was planning to."

---

Dinner was bread, onions, hard cheese, stew made from goat, and an argument over whether the king was allowed to eat in the kitchen.

Mara said no because the kitchen was working.

Luceran said that was why he wanted to eat there.

Mara said that was why he could not.

He compromised by eating in the great hall at a trestle table with half the senior household and several workers because nobody had established who belonged where.

This, Avarren informed him, was already political.

Luceran ignored him.

Jory sat three places from Clavard because somebody had moved the benches.

Derrin ate with two guards.

Edrik remained standing until Luceran ordered him to sit.

"I am on duty."

"You can guard me while chewing."

"Can I?"

Luceran looked at Rhyse.

Rhyse said, "Yes."

Edrik sat.

Bram came in halfway through after checking the inner gate.

Nessa would have laughed to see him eating goat stew beneath a royal banner while two dismissed pieces of silver service sat unopened in a wagon below.

Luceran drank watered wine.

For the first time in weeks, nobody handed him a petition during dinner.

He noticed.

Then, because the universe hated him, Avarren said:

"Tomorrow, rooms."

Luceran put down his spoon.

"No."

"Yes."

"Tomorrow, roofs."

"Rooms first."

"Roofs keep weather outside."

"Rooms determine who can reach you."

Luceran looked around.

Workers.

Guards.

Clerks.

His friend.

A steward who did not kneel properly.

A housekeeper who told him where he could eat.

"This is better."

Avarren studied him.

"What is?"

"All of this."

"The goat?"

"No."

"Shame."

"The mess."

Montegrain leaned back.

"You enjoy chaos?"

"I enjoy that nobody here has had twenty years to decide where to stand."

Avarren said nothing.

That silence contained too much.

Luceran narrowed his eyes.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"You disagree."

"I think they began deciding where to stand before you finished speaking at Moncler's construction site."

Luceran looked at Jory.

Jory was arguing quietly with Corren Bale about whether the east lintels were original.

At Edrik.

Eating while still watching the door.

At Mara.

Already reorganizing tomorrow in her head.

At Orven.

Who had sat alone at the far end because nobody knew his rank relative to anyone else.

Luceran looked back at Avarren.

"One night."

Avarren nodded.

"One night."

They both knew that was not how precedent worked.

Only one of them understood it fully.

---

After dinner came fires.

Blankets.

Missing boots.

A cracked wash basin.

Two arguments over beds.

A servant crying quietly because she had never slept outside the capital.

A groom bitten by a horse.

A treasury clerk discovering that rats had no respect for seals.

A physician demanding boiled water.

A guard captain discovering that one inner door could not be barred because the bar had rotted through.

Work continued everywhere.

Luceran walked.

Bram went with him.

Edrik slept for three hours because Rhyse ordered it.

That alone made Luceran realize close escort involved shifts rather than devotion.

Good.

He walked through corridors lit irregularly by oil lamps.

Stone floors changed width without warning.

One stair descended three steps, turned, and descended eleven more.

A wall displayed old spearheads.

Another displayed nothing except pale rectangles where paintings had been removed.

Doors bore different generations of ironwork.

Some hinges were beautiful.

Some were crude replacements.

A corridor narrowed.

Luceran stopped.

"Is this original?"

Bram looked at the wall.

"Majesty, I was assigned here this morning."

"Right."

Orven, from somewhere ahead:

"No."

Luceran almost jumped.

"Do you live in the walls?"

"No."

"Then stop doing that."

Orven came into view carrying a lamp.

"This wing was widened during your great-great-grandmother's reign. The narrow section is older."

"Why leave it narrow?"

"Stone."

"That is not an answer."

"It is most answers."

Luceran sighed.

They reached a junction.

One route led toward the family wing.

A heavy door stood at the far end.

Locked.

Luceran looked.

Orven looked at him.

"Tomorrow."

"I am not going through it."

"Good."

"I am looking."

"Allowed."

Luceran walked closer.

The door was dark oak banded with iron.

No elaborate lock.

No warning.

No ancestral inscription.

Just a door.

He put a hand on it.

Cold.

Bram stood several paces back.

Orven did not move.

Luceran listened.

Nothing.

Then—

Voices.

Faint.

Several.

Too quiet to make out words.

Luceran froze.

Bram's hand went to his sword.

Orven closed his eyes.

There they were again.

Murmuring.

A rise.

A fall.

Then something like laughter.

Luceran looked at Orven.

"What is that?"

Orven opened his eyes.

"Could be servants in the north rooms."

"You said those are closed."

"Some."

Again.

A murmur behind stone.

Bram stepped toward the wall.

"Chimney?"

Orven nodded.

"Old flues run together."

"There is a fire?"

"Three rooms away."

Luceran looked at the door.

"People?"

"Sound."

"That is not the same thing."

"No."

Bram put one ear near the wall.

The sound stopped.

He waited.

Nothing.

Luceran waited too.

Nothing.

Then, from somewhere far above, a shutter slammed.

Bram exhaled.

"Wind."

Orven said, "Probably."

Luceran looked at him.

"Probably?"

Orven lifted the lamp.

"Old houses talk."

Luceran frowned.

"You said nobody else lives here."

"Nobody does."

"What does that mean?"

"It means pipes knock. Flues carry voices. Boards settle. Rats sound like children if you're tired enough. Wind finds holes. Stone gives back footsteps from places you weren't looking."

Luceran kept his hand on the door.

"And if I hear it again?"

"Go to sleep."

Bram looked at Orven.

"That your professional advice?"

"It has served me forty-seven years."

Luceran waited for the voices.

Nothing.

He took his hand away.

"Tomorrow."

Orven nodded.

"Tomorrow."

---

Montegrain was in Luceran's room when he returned.

This should have been impossible.

Bram stopped in the doorway.

Montegrain looked up from the chair.

"Hello."

Bram looked at Luceran.

Luceran looked at Montegrain.

"How did you get in?"

"The door."

Bram's expression became personally offended.

"No one passed me."

"I came before you were posted."

Bram considered this.

Then looked at the wardrobe.

Montegrain followed his eyes.

"I am not hiding in the wardrobe."

"Good."

"I could."

"Please don't."

Luceran shut the door.

Bram remained outside.

Luceran took off his wet coat.

Montegrain looked at him.

"So?"

"So what?"

"Is it everything you hoped?"

Luceran laughed once.

"No."

"Good."

Luceran looked over.

"Good?"

"You would be unbearable if it were."

Luceran sat on the edge of the bed.

The mattress was too hard.

Of course.

"My grandfather's guest room."

"Appropriate."

"I am the king."

"You are also the guest."

"Orven said the same thing."

"I like him."

"Everyone likes people who insult me."

"Efficient social strategy."

Luceran rubbed his face.

For several moments neither spoke.

Then Luceran said:

"I heard voices."

Montegrain's expression changed.

"Where?"

"Family corridor."

"Words?"

"No."

"People?"

"Don't know."

"Did Bram hear them?"

"Yes."

"Orven?"

"Yes."

"What did Orven say?"

"Flues."

Montegrain nodded.

"Reasonable."

"You sound disappointed."

"I promised not to turn your ancestral ruin into a ghost story before breakfast."

Luceran looked at him.

"Thank you."

"After breakfast, no promises."

Luceran lay back without removing his boots.

Montegrain looked at them.

"No."

"What?"

"Boots."

"I am king."

"You are on the bed."

Luceran shut his eyes.

"Go away."

Montegrain stood.

At the door he stopped.

"Luceran."

"What?"

"This was a good decision."

Luceran opened his eyes.

Montegrain was serious.

"You don't know that."

"No."

"Then why say it?"

"Because you needed to leave."

That was not the same thing as saying Edselsto would solve anything.

Luceran understood the difference.

He hated that he understood the difference.

"Good night."

Montegrain left.

Bram opened the door long enough to let him pass, then shut it again.

Luceran lay in the dark.

The room smelled of cold stone, damp wool, old wood, and smoke from a newly lit hearth.

Somewhere distant, a hammer struck once.

Then again.

Workers still repairing something.

Somebody shouted.

A cart moved in the courtyard.

Normal sounds.

Human sounds.

He listened until they thinned.

The castle settled around him.

A pipe knocked.

A board creaked.

Wind pressed against the shutters.

Far away, something murmured.

Luceran opened his eyes.

Silence.

He waited.

Nothing.

He was tired.

That was all.

He told himself that with the confidence of a man who had been king long enough to know how useful explanations could be when one wanted to sleep.

Above the nave, seven candles burned steadily through the night.

All seven.
