# Nowhere King — Season One, Episode Three
## The Road North

**Status:** Committed episode prose  
**Season:** One — The Return to Edselsto  
**Episode:** 03 of 32  
**Reveal ceiling:** Political and material reality. Edselsto may be reported as old, damaged, habitable in part, and architecturally strange; no confirmed supernatural event, candle failure, containment truth, or sentient-castle evidence.  
**Primary dramatic purpose:** Make the consequences of Luceran's haste arrive before the promised dawn departure, then put the new court physically on the road. Convert the escort purge, labor seizure, wagon shortage, family separation, and advance-party uncertainty into durable political and human obligations. End with Edselsto becoming a visible place rather than an idea.

---

At eleven minutes before midnight, the palace had run out of wagons.

It had not run out of things.

Things were everywhere.

Chests stood open beneath the western arcade. Rolled carpets leaned against pillars. Two clerks argued over a locked iron dispatch box while a third sat on it to prevent a porter from taking it away. Kitchen boys carried sacks of flour toward the courtyard and returned carrying the same sacks back because somebody had decided flour belonged in the second train, not the first. A physician had commandeered half a cart for medicines and was defending it with the expression of a man prepared to diagnose anyone who objected with a broken nose.

The horses knew something terrible was happening and had elected to contribute by shitting everywhere.

Master Clavard Clifford stood in the middle of it with three ledgers, two exhausted scribes, and no remaining patience.

"Whose wagon is that?"

A carter looked behind him.

"Mine."

"It was yours. Whose warrant?"

"Red seal."

"There are four red seals in use tonight."

"Then one of those."

Clavard closed his eyes.

"Wonderful."

The carter waited.

"Do I load the chairs?"

"No."

"They told me chairs."

"Who?"

The carter pointed into the palace.

That narrowed the matter to several hundred people.

Clavard looked at the chairs. They were carved walnut, upholstered in blue velvet, and entirely capable of remaining in the capital without threatening the continuity of government.

"Unload them."

A household clerk appeared at his shoulder.

"Master Clifford, the Keeper of the Wardrobes says Her late Majesty's winter trunks must travel in the first train."

Clavard looked at him.

"Her late Majesty is dead."

The clerk's face tightened.

Clavard regretted the sentence immediately.

"I know," he said, quieter. "Second train. Seal the trunks. Put them under guard. Nothing is to be discarded."

"Yes, master."

"And find out who ordered chairs."

The clerk disappeared.

Across the courtyard, a man began shouting about oats.

Clavard opened the first ledger again.

The king had said dawn.

Dawn, Clavard was learning, was not a time of day. It was a weapon.

---

Lord Edmund Moncler arrived at the palace shortly after midnight wearing an excellent coat over clothing that had plainly been put on in anger.

He did not ask to see the king.

That was the first sensible decision anyone had made in an hour.

He asked for Clavard.

"My lord."

"Master Clifford."

They regarded one another across a writing table that had been dragged into a side gallery because every proper office between there and the treasury was full of crates.

Moncler placed a folded contract on the table.

"Forty-one men."

"Thirty-eight have reported. Three claimed hardship."

"Forty-one contracted."

"Yes."

"Two months of dressed stone already purchased. Timber cut to measurements for work that will now stop. Lime mixed. Scaffold hire. Crane hire. Lodging for six country masons. Penalties if the west roof is not enclosed before the rains."

Clavard nodded.

"I have not disputed your loss."

"His Majesty took my workmen out of my house."

"Your unfinished house."

"That distinction is not as comforting as you seem to think."

Clavard almost smiled.

Almost.

Moncler leaned forward.

"I will not be made the villain because the king heard a hammer and discovered economics."

"I don't intend to make you one."

That stopped him.

Clavard turned the contract around.

"Your direct losses will be assessed. Wages already advanced by you against unfinished work will be credited. Perishable materials reasonably lost because of the interruption will be compensated. Existing penalties caused solely by Crown recruitment will be suspended where the Crown has authority to suspend them."

Moncler studied him.

"And where it does not?"

"Bring me the claims."

"You are offering full compensation?"

"I am offering an accounting. Those are not the same thing."

Moncler sat back.

There was the man he knew.

"And my labor?"

"Gone."

"All of it?"

"Most."

"For how long?"

Clavard rubbed the bridge of his nose.

"I do not know."

"Then you cannot calculate my loss."

"Correct."

"This is absurd."

"Yes."

Moncler had prepared himself for denial, hauteur, perhaps a lecture about royal necessity. Agreement left him with nowhere satisfying to put his anger.

"I want that in writing."

Clavard gestured toward one of the scribes.

"So do I."

The scribe began copying.

Moncler looked through the gallery arch toward the courtyard.

"Is he truly leaving at dawn?"

"Yes."

"Why?"

Clavard looked at the wagons.

"Because he said he would."

Moncler gave a short laugh without humor.

"That is going to become expensive."

"It already has."

---

Two floors below the gallery, Captain Tovan Rhyse was being told that he had insulted House Pell.

The speaker was Lord Cassian Pell, uncle to one of the dismissed Royal Companions and a man with the unfortunate gift of sounding reasonable while furious.

"My nephew's failure is not in dispute."

Rhyse folded his arms.

"Good."

"Do not enjoy this too much, Tovan."

"I haven't slept. Enjoyment is beyond me."

Cassian ignored that.

"My objection is to what follows. You dismissed nine men in one stroke and replaced their function with two gate guards."

"I replaced nobody's function with two gate guards. I assigned two men to stay close to the king because they demonstrated they could do it."

"Sergeant Tolland has never served close escort."

"He has now."

"For an hour."

"During which he knew where the king was."

Cassian's jaw tightened.

Rhyse let the silence sit.

The facts were vulgar things. They had no respect for pedigree.

"You know what this looks like," Cassian said.

"Yes."

"Do you? Nine noble appointments removed immediately after the king announces he is abandoning the capital. Men whose families have voices in half the provincial courts."

"Nine guards dismissed after losing the king."

"The king walked out."

"Kings do that."

"Without notice."

"Enemies are famous for providing notice."

Cassian exhaled through his nose.

"And the other five?"

"Two were on authorized message duty. One was at the north gate because I put him there. One was sick and had reported it. One was in the armory collecting equipment for the departure. They keep their commissions pending review."

Cassian stared at him.

That was worse politically, because it was fair.

"My nephew will appeal."

"He may."

"To the king."

"He may."

"And if the king restores him?"

Rhyse shrugged.

"Then I will put him somewhere he can learn to count sovereigns."

Cassian laughed despite himself.

It lasted only a moment.

"You have made enemies tonight."

"No. I have identified paperwork attached to enemies I already had."

"That sounds like Avarren talking."

"Don't insult me."

Cassian turned to leave, then stopped.

"Tovan."

"What?"

"My nephew is a fool. He is not a traitor."

Rhyse's expression changed slightly.

"I know."

"Remember the difference."

"I intend to."

Cassian left.

Rhyse stood alone for three breaths before a lieutenant appeared.

"Captain."

"If this is another uncle, shoot me."

"Courier from Edselsto."

Rhyse straightened.

"Alive?"

"Very much so. Very tired."

"Take him to the king."

---

Bram Tolland's wife did not slap him.

He considered this a promising beginning.

Nessa stood in their kitchen with both hands on the table and looked at the close-escort baldric he had been issued less than an hour earlier.

Their daughters had been sent back to bed and were, judging by the silence above, listening from the stairs.

"Say it again."

"The captain assigned me to the king."

"Why?"

"Because the king left."

"Left what?"

"The palace."

"And?"

"I followed him."

Nessa stared.

"That is the reason?"

"Apparently it distinguished me."

Her mouth twitched.

Bram put a small purse on the table.

"There will be an allowance while I'm away. Rhyse says close duty carries extra pay."

She did not touch it.

"How long?"

"I don't know."

"Where will you sleep?"

"I don't know."

"How often will you come home?"

"I don't know."

"You have been promoted into knowing considerably less than you did this morning."

"That is also my assessment."

Nessa finally took the purse.

"Do you want it?"

Bram looked at the baldric.

That was harder.

"I want the pay."

"Bram."

"I want Rhyse to have men near the king who think guarding him is work instead of jewelry."

She waited.

"And?"

He sighed.

"And when he pointed at me, I went."

Nessa knew him well enough not to ask which part mattered most.

She came around the table and fixed the strap where he had buckled it twisted.

"Then don't become jewelry."

"I will strive against it."

From the stairs came a small voice.

"Does the king run away a lot?"

Bram looked toward the darkness.

"Go to bed."

"Does he?"

Nessa covered her mouth.

Bram considered the question.

"Apparently."

---

Edrik Saye's mother did slap him.

Not hard.

It was more of an editorial comment.

"Ow."

"You are going where?"

"Edselsto."

"With whom?"

"The king."

She slapped him again.

"Stop that."

"Stop saying foolish things."

His younger brother Len was laughing into his sleeve.

Edrik pointed at him.

"You are paying for coal while I'm gone."

Len stopped laughing.

Their mother looked at the new badge.

"Did you ask for this?"

"No."

"Did you do something heroic?"

"I walked quickly."

Len laughed again.

Edrik packed two shirts, wool stockings, a whetstone, sewing kit, spare bootlaces and the good gloves his mother had bought him the previous winter.

She removed the gloves and replaced them with an older pair.

"Those are for church."

"I am guarding the king."

"Is he a church?"

"No."

"Then old gloves."

He did not argue.

Before he left, she caught his sleeve.

"Listen to your sergeant."

"I do."

"Listen to your captain."

"I do."

She looked at him until he understood there was another instruction coming.

"And if the king tells you to do something stupid?"

Edrik thought of Luceran stepping into traffic.

"Stay close enough to stop somebody else killing him while he does it."

His mother nodded.

"Good."

---

Jory Fell did not sleep.

Neither did Ellyn.

They packed his tools on the floor because the tool chest was too heavy to carry full and because every object became an argument about what counted as necessary.

"Two planes."

"Three."

"You have two hands."

"They do different things."

"Then grow another hand."

Jory wrapped the third plane.

Their daughter had fallen asleep at the table with her head on her arms.

Ellyn watched him tie the bundle.

"You can still claim hardship."

"For what?"

"Us."

He looked at her.

"We are not hardship."

"I didn't mean—"

"I know."

He sat back on his heels.

"If I refuse because I don't like leaving, Olan has to find another carpenter. Maybe one without children. Maybe one with worse debts. The money is good. The work is royal. If Edselsto becomes the court, every builder in the kingdom will want a piece of it six months from now."

Ellyn understood that. She disliked understanding it.

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then I have a story about the time a king stole me from Lord Moncler's window."

"Recruited."

"Stole."

"You said recruited yesterday."

"Yesterday the king was in the room."

She laughed.

Then she knelt and pressed her forehead against his.

"Come home."

"I intend to."

"That is not a promise."

"No."

She kissed him anyway.

The daughter at the table opened one eye, saw her parents, and closed it again with the profound disgust of a child confronted by evidence that marriage continued after children.

---

The courier from Edselsto arrived in Luceran's private chamber while the king was arguing with Avarren about books.

"The law library travels."

"A selection travels."

"All of it."

"Majesty, unless you intend to eat precedents, we need the wagons for food."

A knock interrupted them.

Rhyse entered with a road-stained soldier whose boots were grey to the ankle.

Luceran forgot the books.

"From Edran?"

The courier bowed and held out a sealed packet.

"Yes, Majesty. Sir Edran Vey sends report and asks that I say the road above Kaelmont's lower toll is passable for wagons if taken slowly. One culvert is damaged. He has men marking the edge."

Luceran broke the seal.

Edran's handwriting was compact and ugly.

The report was not romantic.

That was reassuring.

The lower stable roof held. The upper stable did not. Two kitchens could be made usable; one chimney needed clearing before fire. The main well produced water after debris was hauled from the housing, but Edran wanted it boiled until Mara Kest was satisfied with the source. The great hall was dry except along the eastern wall. Several chambers in the inhabited wing were sound enough for bedding. The west roof leaked badly. The south gallery was closed because the floor had failed in two places. Rats were abundant. Firewood was available. The village could provide eggs, goats, rough bread, some preserved meat, labor, and more opinions than Edran had requested.

The elderly steward was alive.

His name was Orven Dast.

He had received the royal seal, looked at Edran for a long time, and said, according to the report, *About bloody time.*

Luceran read that line twice.

Avarren leaned over.

"Good steward?"

"Apparently."

"Castle?"

Luceran continued.

Several interior doors were swollen shut. Others had been locked long enough that nobody currently present had keys. Dast insisted some family rooms were not to be forced before the king arrived because they contained dynastic property. Edran had respected the instruction because nothing behind them was required for immediate habitation.

No army occupied the castle.

No rival banner flew above it.

No obvious ambush waited on the approach.

It was, in short, a ruin with a stubborn old man in it.

Luceran smiled.

Rhyse noticed.

"What?"

"It exists."

Avarren looked at the report.

"That was not seriously in doubt."

"Not the point."

Luceran handed it over.

For days Edselsto had been an argument. A name. A childhood painting. A place used by older men to explain why something could not be done quickly.

Now there were rats.

Rats were excellent.

Rats were real.

"We leave at dawn."

Avarren read the line about the culvert.

"We were already leaving at dawn."

"Now we know where we're going."

Rhyse said, "We knew where we were going."

Luceran looked between them.

"You are both determined to ruin this for me."

"Yes," said Avarren.

"Professionally," said Rhyse.

---

Dawn found the old capital awake and furious.

The first train occupied the avenue from the palace gate to the lower market.

There were fewer wagons than Luceran had imagined and more than Clavard had believed possible.

The Carters' Union had produced them at prices that made Clavard briefly religious.

House Damaris supplied two grain carts after receiving a sealed assurance that the loads would be paid at emergency carriage rates rather than requisitioned. House Bellisar loaned remount horses with a written condition that royal grooms, not household amateurs, handle them. House Nerezza sent a revenue clerk to witness every emergency expenditure and thereby made itself hated by everyone while preventing at least three obvious attempts at theft.

The Royal Kitchens Compact had packed bread, hard cheese, smoked meat, dried peas, salt, onions, ale, vinegar, and enough pots to start a small war over cookware.

The physicians lost half their wagon and won it back.

The chairs remained behind.

Clavard considered this his greatest victory.

Luceran came through the gate on horseback.

Bram rode to his right.

Edrik rode to his left.

Neither looked ornamental.

That, more than the new badges, was noticed.

So were the absences.

Windows along the avenue held faces. Noble servants stood beside merchants. Apprentices had climbed onto roofs. Children sat on walls. Men who had been dismissed from the Royal Companions did not appear in uniform, but their families' colors appeared often enough among the watchers.

Luceran felt the attention like weather.

Montegrain brought his horse alongside.

"Still think you are escaping politics?"

Luceran looked at him.

"I am escaping these politics."

"Ah."

"What?"

"Nothing. I look forward to meeting the new politics."

Luceran smiled despite himself.

Behind them, Olan Crewe shouted at a wagon driver who had placed chisels beneath bedding.

Jory Fell walked beside the tool cart until Ellyn pushed through the crowd and caught his hand.

The column slowed around them.

Jory looked mortified.

Ellyn did not care.

She kissed him hard enough to earn cheers from three carpenters and a scandalized noise from somebody's aunt.

"Come home," she said.

"Yes."

"Write."

"Yes."

"Don't let them pay you in promises."

Jory glanced toward Clavard.

"That one I think I can manage."

Derrin Voss's mother did not push through the crowd.

She stood on a step and watched him go with both hands wrapped around the purse containing his advance.

When he looked back, she raised one finger at him.

Derrin knew exactly what it meant.

Do not be stupid near important people.

He raised one finger in return.

His sister laughed.

Rhyse rode down the line once, then again.

The king had an escort now.

The convoy had guards.

Those were different problems.

At the rear gate of the palace, Lord Cassian Pell watched from beneath an awning.

His nephew stood beside him in ordinary clothes.

"You should have been there," Cassian said.

The young man looked at the column.

"I know."

It was the first useful thing he had said about the matter.

---

The capital did not end cleanly.

It thinned.

Stone houses became timber. Shops became yards. Yards became gardens. Gardens became fields wet with morning mist. The road widened where carts had worn the verges down and narrowed again where old walls refused to move for newer traffic.

Luceran had traveled royal roads all his life.

He had rarely traveled them at the speed of a loaded wagon.

This was educational.

By midmorning, one wheel had cracked.

By noon, a draft horse had thrown a shoe.

By early afternoon, the column had stretched so far that Luceran could not see the rear from the front.

He learned that a royal convoy did not move at the speed of the king.

It moved at the speed of the slowest necessary thing.

Today that thing was a wagon carrying archive chests and vinegar.

"Leave it," Luceran said.

Clavard stared at him.

"Which?"

"The wagon. Transfer the important things."

"Define important."

Luceran opened his mouth.

Clavard waited.

The king looked at the chests.

Royal correspondence. Tax rolls. Household warrants. Court seals. Property records. Petitions. Some could be replaced. Some could not. The vinegar was for food preservation and cleaning. The wagon itself belonged to a carter who expected it returned.

"Fine," Luceran said.

Clavard pointed at him.

"Do not walk anywhere."

Montegrain laughed from his horse.

Bram coughed into his fist.

Luceran dismounted.

They repaired the wheel.

It took forty-three minutes.

Nobody died.

The kingdom survived.

Luceran hated how instructive this was.

---

At the damaged culvert below Kaelmont's lower toll, the road narrowed to a single safe wheel track.

House Kaelmont's men had already set stakes along the broken edge.

A toll reeve waited beside them with three laborers and the expression of a man expecting to be blamed for geology.

Luceran looked down into the washout.

"How long?"

"To repair properly? Three days if stone arrives. Five if it rains."

"To get us across?"

"You can cross now. Slowly. One wagon at a time. No passing. Keep riders off the outer edge."

Luceran looked at the length of the column behind him.

"How long?"

The reeve considered.

"All of you?"

"Yes."

"Hours."

Luceran inhaled.

The reeve added, "Majesty, the road does not know you are king."

Silence.

Bram looked very carefully at the horizon.

Edrik's face became a study in duty.

Luceran stared at the reeve.

Then he laughed.

"Apparently nobody told it."

The reeve relaxed by a fraction.

"No, Majesty."

"Can we help?"

That surprised him.

"With the crossing? Yes. Men at the wheels. Keep the teams straight."

Olan Crewe was already climbing down from the tool wagon.

"Jory! Derrin!"

The workers came.

So did soldiers.

So did carters who had no interest in watching their livelihoods roll into a ditch.

The crossing took two hours and nine minutes.

The last wagon cleared before the first drops of rain.

The reeve watched it go and made a note in his toll book that the king had passed north with more wagons than sense.

He later crossed out the last three words.

Not because they were false.

Because toll books survived people.

---

The rain followed them into the hills.

It was not dramatic rain.

It was worse.

It was steady, cold, and competent.

Cloaks darkened. Wheels hissed. Horses lowered their heads. The road became a long brown argument.

Luceran's enthusiasm survived longer than anyone expected.

Then water ran down the back of his neck.

Montegrain saw it happen.

"Majesty?"

"Don't."

"I said nothing."

"You were about to."

"I was going to ask whether Edselsto has weather."

Luceran pulled his hood tighter.

"You may return to the capital."

"And miss the new politics? Never."

Bram rode ahead to check a bend.

Edrik stayed close.

The pattern had formed without ceremony.

When Bram returned, Edrik moved outward. When the road narrowed, one watched the king and one watched everyone else. At halts they argued quietly about doors that did not exist yet, because Rhyse's instruction had been clear and both men were taking it literally.

Luceran noticed.

He also noticed that neither asked him whether he wanted them there.

Oddly, that made their presence easier.

They were not pretending intimacy.

They were working.

---

Near evening the rain weakened.

The road climbed through darker country.

Pines replaced broad fields. Exposed roots gripped black stone beside the road. Farms became smaller and farther apart. Smoke rose from valleys rather than chimneys beside the highway.

The convoy rounded a long shoulder of rock.

Then everyone at the front stopped.

Luceran saw the mountain first.

Not because he was king.

Because he happened to be looking up.

It rose beyond the next valley, dark against the clearing western sky. Basalt and granite broke through the trees in long black shelves. Pines crowded its lower slopes. The road could be seen in pieces, climbing toward it and vanishing again.

Castle Edselsto stood above them.

From this distance it was less a palace than a wound in the skyline.

Towers rose unevenly from the ridge. One roofline sagged. Another caught the last pale light. The outer walls followed the mountain instead of correcting it, stepping across black stone as though the fortress had grown where the rock permitted.

No banners were visible at this distance.

No windows shone.

No miracle announced the king's return.

It was simply there.

Old.

Large.

Inconvenient.

Real.

Luceran reined in.

Behind him the convoy slowed by degrees as people realized the front had stopped.

Jory Fell came around the side of a wagon, saw the castle, and said something obscene.

Derrin Voss looked at the amount of visible stone and began laughing.

Olan Crewe did not laugh.

He was estimating scaffolding.

Clavard was estimating money.

Rhyse was estimating walls, approaches, fields of fire, blind ground, and how many men he did not have.

Montegrain looked at Luceran.

Luceran looked only at Edselsto.

He had imagined relief.

What he felt was something stranger.

The capital was behind him.

His parents were still dead.

The houses had not ceased wanting things. The guilds had not ceased counting. The dismissed guards had not ceased having families. Moncler's unfinished mansion had not repaired itself because the king had ridden north. Every wagon behind him contained evidence that politics had packed efficiently.

But Edselsto existed.

For the first time since he had given the order, he could point at the thing he had chosen.

"There," he said.

Nobody needed him to explain what he meant.

The road descended into the valley before beginning its final climb.

At the foot of the mountain, smoke rose from the nearly abandoned village.

Somewhere up there, an elderly steward who had waited through several reigns had apparently decided the king was late.

Luceran smiled.

"Come on."

Bram looked at the failing light.

"Majesty, the road is wet."

Luceran looked at him.

Bram did not move.

Edrik watched the mountain.

"And we don't know the upper road."

Luceran looked from one new escort to the other.

Yesterday, nine noblemen had failed to notice him leave a building.

Today, two ordinary guards were telling him not to charge up a mountain at dusk because it was stupid.

He considered being offended.

Then he looked at the wagons.

"Village first?"

Bram nodded.

"Village first."

Luceran turned his horse toward the descending road.

The convoy followed.

Edselsto remained above them, dark against the evening sky.

Seven candles burned somewhere inside it.

Luceran did not know that.

Not yet.

## Episode-end state

Season One, Episode 03 completes the capital-to-Edselsto road transition without entering the castle ahead of Episode 04.

### Durable changes

- Luceran's dawn deadline is met, but only because multiple institutions absorb real cost: carters, guilds, Crown household, Damaris grain interests, Bellisar horse interests, Nerezza revenue oversight, Royal Kitchens, physicians, guards and workers.
- Lord Edmund Moncler's grievance is formalized as a compensation/accounting claim rather than erased or villainized.
- Captain Tovan Rhyse's escort purge survives first political challenge. Lord Cassian Pell concedes his nephew's failure while warning that dismissal and replacement have factional consequences.
- The five Royal Companions not summarily dismissed remain subject to individual review; their differing circumstances are explicitly preserved.
- Bram Tolland and Edrik Saye begin functioning as an actual close-escort pair. Their families are established as continuing personal stakes. Bram's wife Nessa and daughters, and Edrik's mother and brother Len, remain independent continuity obligations.
- Jory Fell and Ellyn's marriage receives ordinary affection, humor, anxiety and economic reasoning before separation. Jory's royal work is opportunity and coercive disruption at once.
- Derrin Voss's family remains materially dependent on his wages and skeptical of proximity to power.
- Sir Edran Vey's first direct report reaches Luceran before departure: Edselsto is damaged but partly habitable; water, kitchens, stables and dry chambers exist; several areas are unsafe or sealed; elderly steward Orven Dast is alive and awaiting the king.
- The convoy experiences a cracked wheel, thrown shoe, road-speed limits, and a damaged Kaelmont culvert. Luceran learns materially that a royal convoy moves at the speed of necessary logistics rather than sovereign impatience.
- A Kaelmont toll reeve is established as a competent provincial official who tells the king an inconvenient physical truth without being punished for it.
- Edselsto and its village are physically visible by episode end. The convoy descends toward the village rather than attempting the wet upper road at dusk.
- Bram and Edrik successfully tell Luceran no on a practical security matter. Luceran accepts it, beginning a small but important baseline for loyal friction.

### Knowledge state

- Luceran knows Edselsto is partly habitable and materially difficult. He has no supernatural knowledge.
- Rhyse knows the escort purge is politically costly but professionally defensible.
- Noble patrons know factual denial of the dismissed guards' negligence is weak; future pressure is therefore likely to target procedure, status, replacement appointments and access.
- Workers know royal wages may materially improve their families' lives while royal command can disrupt them without meaningful bargaining power.
- Nobody in the moving court knows the buried truth, the prison, the castle-interface reality, or the candle function.

### Candle state

Seven of seven remain lit. No candle failure has occurred.

### Immediate handoff

Episode 04 begins at the village/upper approach and proceeds into Edselsto for the first night. Orven Dast, Edran's advance party, leaking roofs, usable kitchens, unsafe floors, sealed family rooms, and the seven candles can enter lived prose. Voices or strange acoustics may be experienced only within the Season One reveal ceiling and must remain deniable.
