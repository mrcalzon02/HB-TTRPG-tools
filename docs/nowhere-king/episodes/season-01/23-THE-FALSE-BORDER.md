# Season One, Episode 23 — The False Border

The toll shelter had burned.

That much, at least, was true.

By sunrise the next morning Luceran had received four versions of everything else.

In the first, forty armed riders had crossed Red Ford beneath foreign colors and put the keeper to the sword.

In the second, twelve cattle thieves had burned an empty hut after being challenged by shepherds.

In the third, nobody had crossed the ford at all; the fight had begun on Crown land between two families who had spent twenty years disagreeing about where a grazing stone belonged.

In the fourth, the shelter had caught fire because its keeper had stored lamp oil beside dry rush matting and everybody involved had invented soldiers afterward because accident was politically useless.

Luceran put the four messages side by side.

"Do we have a fifth?"

Pellin Joss, who had slept badly enough that one side of his hair remained flattened against his head, handed him another folded sheet.

"Naturally."

Luceran opened it.

The fifth version said the keeper was alive.

He looked up.

"I hate borders."

Tovan Rhyse took the paper from him. "You hate reports. Borders merely produce them."

"Where is the patrol?"

"Returning to Red Ford. Their first rider reached Northbridge before midnight. The full patrol should be back by noon if the road holds."

Mara Kest was already reading the quartermaster's note attached to the message.

"Our stores convoy is at Northbridge."

Luceran closed his eyes for half a heartbeat.

"Of course it is."

"That is where we sent it."

"I am aware, Mara. I am objecting to causality."

"It rarely accepts objections."

Miren laughed into her cup.

The map room smelled of damp wool, lamp smoke and the bread somebody had remembered to bring but nobody had yet eaten. Outside, Edselsto was waking around them: carts grinding in the lower yard, hammers beginning at the east roof, guards calling reliefs, kitchen boys swearing at a barrel that had split its hoop.

Normal life was becoming loud enough that Luceran had learned to value it.

He put one finger on Red Ford.

"What do we actually know?"

Rhyse answered first.

"A toll shelter burned."

"Confirmed?"

"Seen by the patrol rider."

"Bodies?"

"None seen."

"Armed men?"

"Tracks from several horses and boots near the boundary. Number uncertain."

"Crossing?"

"Uncertain."

"Colors?"

"The two original riders claimed grey-and-red cloth. The patrol rider saw no colors."

Captain Maelin Harrow leaned both hands on the table.

"Grey and red describes half the north after rain."

Jeron Bellisar, still condemned to a chair by his healing leg, said, "And the other half before washing."

Maelin ignored him.

"If armed men burned a Crown toll shelter, whether they crossed ten yards or ten miles matters less than whether they can do it again."

"It matters very much," Jeron said, "if we intend to accuse somebody of crossing a border."

"I said nothing about accusation."

"You said 'if armed men.' We have already improved two riders into a military premise."

Maelin turned toward him.

"And if we wait until every frightened shepherd agrees on heraldry, we may improve a raid into a campaign."

Luceran let them continue for another minute.

They were both doing what he needed.

That did not make listening pleasant.

Mara finally pushed the bread toward Jeron.

"Argue while eating. The kingdom will survive crumbs."

---

At Northbridge, Tomas Reed discovered that six wagons of military stores could become a political declaration merely by standing still.

The bridge crew had lifted two lengths of decking to shore the shifted abutment. That left the Crown convoy backed along the road beneath canvas covers while local carts squeezed past one at a time.

Every traveler slowed to look.

Tomas watched a wool merchant count the wagons.

The merchant noticed him noticing.

"Morning."

"Morning."

"Army coming?"

"No."

The merchant looked at the sacks, the repair iron, the four Crown guards and the Kaelmont road crew.

"Right."

Tomas sighed.

Ardel Kaelmont's road officer, Senn Marr, emerged from beneath the bridge with mud to his elbows.

"If you glare at merchants hard enough, do they become discreet?"

"I was trying to make him forget arithmetic."

"Ambitious."

Tomas looked north.

"How long?"

"For one wagon at a time? By midday. For sustained loaded traffic without praying to every god represented in the masonry? Tomorrow afternoon if the timber arrives."

"It is on wagon six."

Senn stared at him.

Tomas stared back.

Then both men turned toward the sixth wagon, trapped behind the other five on the wrong side of the lifted decking.

Senn began laughing.

Tomas did not.

"That is not funny."

"It is extremely funny."

"The king sent repair iron and timber forward so the bridge could carry the convoy."

"And the bridge must carry the convoy to receive the repair timber."

Tomas looked at the narrow footway.

"Unload it."

"By hand?"

"Unless House Kaelmont has developed levitation and failed to mention it in the carriage terms."

Senn grinned.

Within twenty minutes soldiers, carters, road workers and two men from a nearby mill were carrying braces, iron straps and tools across the bridge by hand.

Nobody had ordered the millers to help. Senn promised them payment anyway and wrote their names down because Kaelmont accounts, unlike heroic songs, required names.

The work had just begun when a patrol horse came down the northern road white with sweat.

The rider did not look like a man returning from battle.

He looked angry.

That distinction reassured Tomas more than it should have.

---

The first useful witness was the toll keeper.

His name was Davan Rusk. He was fifty-six, missing two fingers from his left hand, furious about the loss of his winter coat, and very much alive.

The patrol brought his statement to Edselsto before noon in the handwriting of Lieutenant Sera Vale, who had refused to let three separate local notables dictate it over his shoulder.

Luceran read it twice.

Then he passed it to Miren.

Davan had been collecting the seasonal grazing toll when six mounted men approached from the north bank with approximately twenty head of cattle behind them. Three wore mail beneath travelling coats. At least two carried bows. None displayed heraldry. They claimed the herd had been recovered from thieves south of the disputed grazing line and demanded passage without toll.

Davan refused.

An argument followed.

Then another group arrived from the south: eight local herders, two mounted, all armed in the ordinary manner of people who lived where cattle occasionally changed ownership without permission.

Both groups claimed the same animals.

Someone threw a stone.

Someone else drew a bow.

Nobody agreed who did either.

Davan took shelter behind the toll rail.

A horse broke the rail.

One of the northern men cut the tether on the toll bell.

During the shoving, shouting and brief exchange of arrows, a burning brand landed against the shelter wall.

The shelter burned.

One man from the southern group had an arrow through his upper arm. One northern rider had been struck in the face with a staff. No one was known dead.

The northern group withdrew across the ford with eleven cattle.

The southern group retained nine.

Luceran looked at the map.

"So we have been invaded by livestock accounting."

Maelin Harrow did not smile.

"Six armed riders crossed the ford."

"Yes."

"And burned Crown property."

Rhyse tapped the statement.

"The shelter burned during the fight. Rusk does not say they deliberately fired it."

"He also cannot say they did not."

"Correct."

Jeron asked, "Whose cattle?"

Nobody answered.

That was the problem.

The disputed grazing boundary was not a line in the way capital maps pretended lines existed. It was a succession of stones, stream bends, old trees, seasonal rights, remembered judgments and one royal survey made thirty-one years ago after a flood had moved part of the stream the survey used as a reference.

House Bellisar tenants grazed there.

So did families owing rents to a northern marcher lord whose authority beyond the ford was recognized in some matters, contested in others and cheerfully ignored whenever cattle prices were good.

The toll itself had been challenged twice in the last decade.

Miren finished reading.

"This is real enough to be dangerous and small enough for everyone to lie about."

"My favorite kind of crisis," Luceran said.

Pellin pointed to the original riders' report.

"And somebody changed six riders into armed men crossing a boundary before the patrol returned."

"That does not require conspiracy," Miren said. "Six armed riders did cross a boundary."

"Not necessarily the boundary the report means."

"Exactly."

Luceran looked at them.

There it was again: the difference between a false statement and a true statement arranged to produce a false belief.

He had begun to hate the second more.

---

By afternoon, the wounded herder had a name.

Pavel Tern.

He also had a mother who arrived at the local watch house before the Crown examiner did and announced that the cattle were hers.

This was not strictly true.

Three belonged to her. Two belonged to her brother. Four were held in common with a cousin who had married into a family across the ford, and the remaining animals were disputed because calves had been born while the herd was grazing on land whose rent was itself disputed.

Sera Vale sent the explanation south with the annotation:

**Ownership cannot presently be established without hearing both banks. Injury confirmed. Deliberate military incursion not confirmed. Armed boundary violation probable under at least one interpretation of the grazing rights.**

Rhyse read that aloud.

Luceran said, "Promote whoever taught her to write."

"Her father was a reeve."

"Promote him retroactively."

"He is dead."

"Then commend the dead for administrative restraint."

Miren looked over his shoulder.

"You are joking because you are angry."

"I am joking because if I say what I think of thirty-one-year-old surveys I may offend House Quorin."

"You have not yet met the surveyor who made it."

"Then I reserve judgment and several insults."

She smiled.

The smile did not last.

A second message had arrived with Sera's report.

Two local garrisons had suspended leave again.

A Bellisar captain had sent twelve riders toward Red Ford without waiting for Crown instruction.

A Harrowmere officer, hearing that Bellisar riders were moving, had ordered twenty infantry to prepare for road duty.

Nobody had rebelled.

Nobody had disobeyed a royal order.

Nobody had even acted irrationally.

And the kingdom was beginning to mobilize itself one anxious local decision at a time.

Jeron swore softly.

Maelin read the message and swore more clearly.

"That is not my order."

"I know," Luceran said.

"I want that recorded."

"It will be."

Her jaw tightened.

For the first time since the readiness debate, she looked less like an advocate for visible force than an officer watching her argument escape her control.

"If my people believe Bellisar is moving, they will prepare."

Jeron said, "And if mine believe Harrowmere is preparing, they will move faster."

Neither sounded triumphant.

Mara asked the question that mattered.

"Do we recall the stores convoy?"

Silence.

The convoy was already at Northbridge. The repair crew was already working. Men had already carried its timber across by hand. Grain had already been purchased and loaded. Recalling it would not restore the money, erase the sight of Crown wagons, or make the bridge less strategically important.

Sending it onward would place food closer to a crisis whose scale remained uncertain.

Luceran understood suddenly what it meant for departure to become costly before anyone departed.

"No," he said. "Northbridge stores remain at Northbridge until the bridge is sound. Nothing moves to Red Ford yet."

Maelin opened her mouth.

Luceran raised a hand.

"I am not finished. Bellisar's twelve riders stop at Vey Crossing unless the local patrol requests reinforcement. Harrowmere's twenty stand down from road movement but remain on their ordinary readiness. Leave decisions return to local captains based on local conditions, not rumors about neighboring garrisons."

Jeron nodded.

Maelin did too, more slowly.

"And Red Ford?" Rhyse asked.

Luceran looked at Sera Vale's statement.

"I want both banks heard. I want the cattle counted by marks, brands, age and sex. I want the old judgments, the flood survey and every toll challenge in the last twenty years. I want a Crown magistrate, a Bellisar representative, someone acceptable to the northern families, and a surveyor who does not own cattle there."

Pellin said, "That will look weak to people who want a war."

"Anything short of war will look weak to people who want a war."

"And to people who fear one."

Luceran nodded.

That was harder.

"Then Rhyse goes north with me."

Miren's head turned.

Rhyse said, "Majesty—"

"Not tonight. Not with an army. A royal progress to Vey Crossing and Red Ford with enough force that nobody mistakes me for a petitioner and not enough to turn six idiots and twenty cattle into an invasion."

Maelin asked, "How many?"

"You tell me. Then Jeron tells me why you are wrong."

Jeron smiled despite himself.

"Majesty improves."

---

Miren waited until the others had gone.

The map room seemed larger without them.

Luceran was still staring at Red Ford when she came around the table.

"You are going."

"Yes."

"I know."

He looked at her.

She was not asking permission to be unhappy about it.

That was one of the things he loved about her.

"You think I should stay."

"No."

That surprised him.

"I think if you send another officer, every house between here and the ford will spend three days deciding whether the officer speaks for you. If you go, they can spend those days deciding what to lie to your face about instead."

"Comforting."

"I try."

She touched the edge of the map, not him.

"I also think somebody wanted this report to become larger before verification."

"Pellin thinks so too."

"That does not mean they burned the shelter."

"I know."

"Or own the cattle."

"I know."

"Or know anything about the hunt."

"Miren."

"I am checking."

He exhaled.

"I know."

She finally put her hand over his.

"Good."

He turned his hand beneath hers and laced their fingers.

"Come with me."

Her expression changed, not romantically.

Politically.

"As what?"

He almost said *as yourself*.

That would have been affectionate and useless.

"As Miren Edevane, who understands that half the north will assume your family has an interest whether you come or not."

"Better."

"And as the woman I would rather have beside me."

"Also better."

"You are impossible."

"You invited me."

They kissed beside a map on which a burned hut had already become five different wars.

When they parted, she rested her forehead against his.

"Do not promise me this is nothing."

"I cannot."

"Do not promise me you will be safe."

"I cannot."

"Promise me you will not decide what happened before you get there."

That he could do.

"I promise."

---

Ardel Kaelmont objected to the route.

Not to the journey.

The route.

"If you take the high road from Northbridge to Vey Crossing with sixty mounted men, two baggage wagons and the royal coach, you will ruin the fresh shoulder where we have been laying stone."

Luceran stared at her.

"There is a possible border crisis."

"The road remains a road during crises."

"You said that yesterday."

"It remained true overnight."

Rhyse hid a smile.

Ardel put a finger on the map.

"Take the lower fork after Northbridge. It adds three miles and saves a week of repair. The coach can manage it if His Majesty tolerates discomfort."

"His Majesty has survived chairs in the old capital."

"Then His Majesty is prepared for war."

Miren, standing opposite her, laughed before she could stop herself.

Ardel looked up.

For one brief second the two women shared uncomplicated amusement.

Then both remembered the other complications.

Neither became stupid because of them.

Ardel continued.

"Also, six of the wagons at Northbridge are under Kaelmont carriage terms. If you move them beyond the contracted depot because the border situation changes, the Crown pays the additional distance."

"Of course it does," Luceran said.

"I wanted that said before someone discovers patriotism in my accounts."

"Put it in writing."

"Already done."

Miren glanced at Luceran.

He knew that look.

It meant she was pleased with Ardel and annoyed about being pleased with Ardel.

He loved her enough not to mention it.

---

By evening, the false border had become a real departure.

Not an army.

Not yet.

Rhyse settled on fifty-eight mounted guards and household troops, plus a dozen Black Pine scouts who would travel ahead in pairs rather than parade around the king. Maelin wanted more. Jeron wanted fewer. They compromised at a number neither liked, which Luceran had begun to recognize as one sign that arithmetic had defeated theater.

Bram Tolland kept Edselsto's fixed posts and relief reserve.

He surrendered only six additional guards beyond the king's normal travelling protection, and he did so after Rhyse personally showed him the revised watch board.

"West service remains covered?" Bram asked.

"Yes."

"Family corridor?"

"Yes."

"Lower gate reserve?"

"Reduced by two for one relief only."

Bram frowned.

"Restore them before the king leaves."

Rhyse said, "That is the plan."

"Plans are why I ask."

Luceran, listening from the doorway, said nothing.

The temptation to order was almost physical.

So was the satisfaction of not needing to.

In the lower yard, Mara Kest was having an argument with a baggage clerk about blankets.

Tomas Reed sent word from Northbridge that the bridge would carry the royal party by late tomorrow if the king did not insist on bringing half the court.

Orel Venn sent revised grain prices and a complaint that rumors had raised oats another half-mark.

Pellin brought three new versions of the border story and, for once, did not bother reading them aloud.

Calienne Orsanne sent a short note asking whether wounded people from either bank would be admitted to Crown-supported care if the dispute widened.

Luceran wrote **yes, without distinction of bank** beneath it and sealed the answer.

Then he went to the Hall of Veils.

The seven candles were burning.

Flowers had accumulated beneath them again: mountain asters, late herbs, a ribbon someone had tied around a brass stand despite repeated requests not to put cloth near open flame.

Brother Talen Veyr was removing the ribbon.

"Majesty."

"Brother."

Luceran looked at the lights.

He still did not know what his ancestors had meant by keeping them.

Tonight they meant that he was leaving home with too little certainty and too many people depending on him to distinguish danger from fear.

That meaning was his, not theirs.

He was careful about the difference.

"Will they be tended while I am gone?"

Talen gave him a mildly offended look.

"They were tended before you arrived."

Luceran smiled.

"Good."

He left all seven burning.

---

The last report came after midnight.

Sera Vale had found the first deliberate lie.

Not the fire.

Not the cattle.

Not the crossing.

The colors.

Neither original rider had seen grey-and-red cloth.

One had heard it from a carter at a roadside alehouse six miles south of Red Ford. The other, questioned separately, admitted he had repeated the description because the first rider sounded certain.

The carter was gone by the time the patrol reached the alehouse.

Nobody there knew his name.

The description had entered the report before either messenger reached Crown authority.

Rhyse read Sera's note in Luceran's chamber while Miren sat on the bed in her shift, hair loose over one shoulder, making no pretense that their intimacy was separate from the fact that a captain had just brought military intelligence into the room.

"That could be somebody shaping the story," Rhyse said.

"Or a carter repeating something he heard elsewhere."

"Yes."

"Can we find him?"

"Pellin will try."

Miren asked, "Does it change tomorrow?"

Luceran looked at the packed travelling case beside the wall.

The bridge was being repaired.

The stores were already north.

Local garrisons had already reacted.

A Crown shelter had burned.

A man had been shot through the arm.

Six armed riders had crossed some version of a boundary with disputed cattle.

And somebody, maliciously or stupidly, had inserted colors into the story before the Crown heard it.

"No," he said.

Rhyse nodded.

That was the unpleasant answer.

Evidence that part of the report was false did not make the rest harmless.

Miren rose and came to him.

"Then sleep."

"I have letters."

"You have a horse tomorrow."

"I have a coach."

"Ardel says you have a bad road tomorrow."

"Ardel says every road is bad when I use it incorrectly."

"She may be right."

Luceran looked at her.

Miren smiled sweetly.

"Jealousy does not require me to become wrong about roads."

Rhyse made a sound that might have been a cough.

Luceran pointed toward the door.

"Captain."

"Majesty."

Rhyse escaped.

Miren closed the door behind him.

The kingdom beyond it remained awake: guards changing watch, clerks copying orders, cooks preparing travel bread, stablehands checking shoes, messengers sleeping in their clothes, merchants calculating prices, families on both banks of Red Ford insisting that the same cattle belonged to them.

No single hand controlled any of it.

That was the danger.

It was also the kingdom.

At dawn, Luceran would ride into it.
