# Season One, Episode 17 — New Guard

The first casualty of the new security rules was breakfast.

Guardsman Edrik Saye discovered this at the west service door when a baker's boy arrived carrying two covered baskets and an expression of personal betrayal.

"You know me."

"I do."

"I came yesterday."

"You did."

"And the day before."

"Also true."

"Then why do I need this?"

The boy held up the little wooden tally Edrik had just given him as if it were evidence in a murder.

"Because yesterday I knew you and today the man relieving me may not."

"He ought to."

"He came from the north gate."

"That's his problem."

"At the moment it is yours."

Behind the boy, a mason began laughing.

The baker's boy turned on him. "You have one too."

The mason stopped laughing.

By second bell there were nineteen people waiting outside the west door, seven complaints written or threatened, one cart whose driver had lost his tally between the village and the castle, and a laundress who informed Edrik that if the king wanted clean sheets he could either admit her donkey or come down and wash them himself.

Edrik admitted the donkey after checking the load.

The laundress did not thank him.

That, he thought, was probably healthy government.

The new system was not complicated. That was Captain Rhyse's first command concerning it.

"If a guard cannot explain a rule before the person he's stopping loses patience," he had said, "the rule is too clever."

So there were three kinds of access.

People who lived or worked inside had marked household or works tallies. Regular suppliers had day tallies tied to named gates and ordinary delivery windows. Visitors without either were held long enough for the person expecting them to confirm that they were, in fact, expected.

Nobody carried a little card explaining the king's movements. Nobody needed to know where Luceran would be at third bell merely because they had permission to deliver onions at second.

The tally did not prove loyalty. It proved that somebody had issued a tally.

Rhyse repeated that sentence so often that Bram had begun repeating it in his sleep.

Probably.

Edrik had not asked.

At the west door, the mason finally reached the front.

"Derrin Voss sent me."

"Name?"

"You know my name."

"Humor me."

"Garren Holt."

Edrik checked the works list.

Garren was there.

His tally was not.

"Where did you get this?"

"Tool yard."

"Who gave it to you?"

"Nera."

"This is an east-yard mark."

Garren looked down.

"It is a piece of wood."

"It is a piece of wood telling me you should be at the east yard."

"Derrin moved me."

"When?"

"After supper."

"Did he move the list?"

Garren stared at him.

Edrik stared back.

Behind Garren, nineteen people became twenty-three.

"Stand aside. I'll send for Derrin."

"I have mortar going hard."

"Then Derrin will answer quickly."

"This is idiotic."

"Possibly. Stand aside."

Garren did.

He was still swearing when Bram arrived.

Bram looked at the line, the donkey, the baker's boy returning downhill, and Garren's expression.

"Going well?"

"Perfectly. The castle is secure from bread."

Bram took the wrong tally from Garren and examined it.

"Who changed his assignment?"

"Derrin. Allegedly."

"Then the error belongs to the change, not the man."

Garren stopped swearing long enough to look vindicated.

Bram ruined it.

"Which does not mean he goes through."

"Oh, piss off."

"That's Sergeant Piss Off until relief."

The line laughed.

Garren did not.

But when Derrin's runner arrived with a corrected list, Edrik crossed the old assignment with one line, wrote the new gate beside it, and made the runner initial the change.

He did not erase anything.

That was another new rule.

Mistakes were allowed to remain visible.

---

Captain Tovan Rhyse had put the guard table in the old grain room because nobody important wanted it.

This made it ideal.

The room had one door, two narrow windows, a scarred table, a wall large enough for a chalk plan, and no ancestral portraits to complain about nails.

Luceran arrived after breakfast and found thirty-two wooden markers arranged across the table.

"Are those my guards?"

"Posts," Rhyse said.

"I have more than thirty-two guards."

"I should hope so."

Bram stood at the wall with a piece of chalk. Edrik had been dragged away from the west door and was still wearing rain on one shoulder. Three other guards Luceran knew by face but not well by name stood opposite them.

Rhyse indicated them.

"Corporal Sera Vale, night watch. Guardsman Mikel Orr, lower gate. Guardswoman Anja Pell, family-wing relief."

Anja Pell bowed.

Luceran looked at the markers.

"Explain."

Rhyse did not.

He looked at Sera.

She hesitated only a moment.

"The old arrangement tells us who commands, Majesty. It does not always tell the next man exactly what he inherits."

"Meaning?"

"Meaning I relieve a post and the man I replace tells me what happened if he remembers, if he thinks it matters, and if he isn't already late for bed."

Mikel said, "And if he's your friend."

Sera glanced at him.

"And if he's your friend."

Luceran looked between them.

There was history there.

Good.

"So?"

Bram tapped the chalkboard.

Each gate and important interior threshold had a short line beneath it: relief time, key or latch responsibility, current restriction, unusual expected traffic, unresolved incident.

No royal route.

No list of who slept where.

No intimate information from the surveyor scare.

"A relief slate," Luceran said.

"A temporary one," Rhyse replied. "The post book remains the record. The slate is what the next watch needs before the old watch leaves."

"Why temporary?"

"Because chalk can be changed by anyone with fingers."

Anja said, "And because if we leave yesterday's warning up for a week, people stop seeing warnings."

Rhyse nodded toward her.

"Exactly."

Luceran moved one marker.

Rhyse moved it back.

Luceran looked at him.

"What?"

"That is the west service door."

"I know."

"You just left it without a relief reserve."

"I was seeing whether you would notice."

"Of course you were."

Bram looked at the ceiling.

Luceran smiled despite himself.

"What changes?"

Rhyse pointed to four markers marked with black cuts.

"These posts control information as much as passage. West service, lower gate, family corridor, and royal close route. They do not receive the same instructions."

"Why not?"

Mikel answered this time.

"Because I don't need to know where you are sleeping to decide whether a charcoal wagon belongs at the lower gate."

"And if I leave through your gate?"

"Then I need to know you are coming before you arrive. Not at breakfast."

Luceran looked at Rhyse.

"You taught them that sentence."

"No. You frightened them into learning it."

That landed cleanly.

Luceran let it.

The hunt had taught too many people that ordinary schedules could become useful to somebody watching. Episode sixteen's note had taught them that a public works visit could become bait whether or not the note itself had been intended as bait.

The answer could not be to make the castle ignorant of its own king.

It could be to make knowledge arrive when needed and not much earlier.

"And the night watch?"

Sera folded her arms.

"Needs more people."

Rhyse sighed.

"There it is."

"You asked."

"I did."

Sera looked at Luceran rather than Rhyse.

"We have doubled questions without doubling bodies. Every late worker now needs a supervisor mark. Every visitor without a tally needs confirmation. Every correction needs initials. Every relief needs a proper handoff. Those are sensible rules. Sensible rules still take time."

"How many?"

"Six more at night if you want the new procedure without shortening patrols. Four if you accept slower gates."

Mikel said, "Or move men from the inner family corridor."

Anja's head turned.

"No."

There it was again.

Luceran looked at her.

"Why?"

"Because everyone remembers the family corridor is quiet and therefore thinks it is spare manpower. It is quiet because we keep it quiet. Archives, your chambers, the old family rooms, the nave approach. If you strip it every time the lower gate gets busy, anyone watching learns exactly which alarm empties it."

Mikel said, "Nobody said strip it."

"You said move men."

"One man."

"From my relief."

"From a corridor where half the doors are locked."

"Locked doors are not guards."

Luceran almost spoke.

Rhyse raised one finger without looking at him.

It was astonishing what a king could become accustomed to.

Mikel continued. "Lower gate handles grain, charcoal, stone, carts, workers, villagers and half the complaints in the kingdom."

"Then ask for more men."

"I am."

"By taking mine."

"By putting bodies where bodies move."

Sera said, "You are both arguing daylight. Night is worse."

Rhyse let them go until the argument stopped producing new information.

Then he said, "Good. Now we know where the pressure is."

Luceran understood.

The guard was not a chessboard.

Every marker was a person who wanted sleep, promotion, prestige, easier duty, safer duty, better pay, a post near a lover, a post far from an enemy, a chance to be noticed, or simply not to be blamed for a rule somebody else had invented.

A security system made of people inherited all of that.

"Six at night," Luceran said.

Rhyse shook his head.

"Not yet."

"You just let her ask me."

"Yes."

"And now you refuse?"

"I want six. I do not know where six competent guards come from without creating six holes elsewhere."

Luceran looked at Sera.

She did not look pleased.

That, too, was probably healthy government.

"Then find out," he said. "No paper soldiers. If six move here, I want to know what stops being guarded there."

"Good," Rhyse said.

Anja glanced at Luceran, then at Rhyse.

Her model of the king had changed by a fraction.

Luceran could see it without knowing exactly into what.

---

The Royal Companions objected before noon.

Not all of them.

That would have been easier.

Three remaining Companion officers came to Rhyse with Lord Pell's cousin Jalen Pell, who had spent most of the morning explaining that he was not there as House Pell's representative while repeatedly mentioning House Pell.

Their complaint was precedence.

The new tallies treated a Companion not assigned to current close duty like any other armed court officer entering a restricted post.

Sir Caldus Ren found this insulting.

"My oath is my credential."

Rhyse sat behind the grain-room table.

"Your oath tells me whom you serve. It does not tell the west-door guard whether you are expected in the family wing at midnight."

"I have entered royal residences since before Saye could shave."

Edrik, standing by the wall, touched his chin.

"Still working on it, sir."

Caldus ignored him.

"The Companions have never been made to present workmen's tokens."

"They are not workmen's tokens."

"Wooden tallies."

"Yes. Wood remains affordable."

Jalen Pell coughed into his hand.

The second Companion, Dame Hesta Mor, was less offended.

"The objection isn't the wood. It's that a gate guard can delay a sworn Companion while sending for confirmation."

"Correct."

"That changes rank at the threshold."

"It changes authority at the threshold. Rank remains intact."

"That distinction will not feel intact when a twenty-year-old guardsman tells a knight to wait in the rain."

Rhyse leaned back.

"Then we should train the twenty-year-old not to be an ass about it."

Hesta almost smiled.

Caldus did not.

"And if the guard abuses it?"

"Complaint goes in the post book. Sergeant reviews it. I review the sergeant. The king can review me."

"So a common guard may publicly challenge a Companion."

"A guard may challenge a credential at the post he is assigned to hold. If he cannot, there is no point assigning him there."

The third Companion, Sir Orren Vale, had said nothing.

Now he asked, "Does this apply to Bram?"

Rhyse looked at him.

"Yes."

"To Saye?"

"Yes."

"To you?"

"If I cross a controlled post outside my command purpose, yes."

"To the king?"

Silence.

Edrik stopped touching his chin.

Rhyse said, "The king does not require permission to enter his own castle."

Caldus gave a small victorious breath.

Rhyse continued.

"But if he orders a route contrary to an active security restriction, my contrary advice is recorded. That rule already exists."

The victory vanished.

Orren nodded.

"Then I have no objection."

Caldus turned toward him.

"You should."

"Why?"

"Because this makes us interchangeable with gate men."

Orren's face hardened.

"No. It makes gate men responsible for gates."

That was the first crack Luceran would later remember.

Not between loyal and disloyal.

Between two men who both believed they were defending the dignity of royal service.

---

By evening, Nera Vos had discovered that the new guard system consumed string.

Not metaphorical string.

Actual string.

Tallies needed ties. Temporary visitor marks needed ties. Sealed packets for route instructions needed ties. Spare keys being transferred between relief officers needed ties and wax.

She stood at the stores desk counting bundles while Pera Sol leaned against the doorway with folded laundry.

"How much string can soldiers eat?"

"All of it."

"I thought that was horses."

"Horses eat oats. Soldiers eat everything else."

Pera set down the linen.

Nera checked the delivery mark.

"This came through west?"

"Yes."

"Any trouble?"

"Edrik made my donkey prove its identity."

Nera looked up.

"How?"

"I don't know. The donkey refused to answer."

Nera laughed.

Then Pera said, "Dena's name isn't in anything, is it?"

The laugh stopped.

Nera understood immediately.

"Not in my records."

"Good."

"The security note says she was located and her time established."

"Good."

Pera nodded once.

That was all.

Then she looked at the bundles again.

"You're going to run out."

"Tomorrow."

"Laundry has cord."

"Laundry cord is Crown issue?"

"Laundry cord is cord."

Nera considered this.

"If I borrow it, I record it."

"If you record that laundry saved royal security with twelve yards of string, I want my name spelled correctly."

"Done."

A kingdom could apparently be defended by privacy, procedure and clothesline.

Nera wrote the transfer down.

---

The first night test was Rhyse's idea and Bram's objection.

"If you tell them it is a test, it tests nothing."

"If I don't tell them, someone may put a spear through you."

"That would be informative."

"For your widow."

"I don't have one."

"Then for me. I dislike paperwork."

They compromised.

Rhyse told Sera Vale that sometime before dawn one authorized person would attempt to cross a controlled post with a deliberately wrong credential. He did not tell her who or where.

Sera told nobody else.

At the third hour after midnight, Bram approached the lower gate wearing his ordinary cloak and carrying a family-wing tally.

Guardsman Mikel Orr stopped him.

"Wrong mark."

"I'm Sergeant Tolland."

"Still wrong."

"Captain wants me inside."

"Then Captain can confirm."

"You're going to wake Rhyse?"

"No. I'm going to wake the duty corporal."

"She'll wake Rhyse."

"That sounds like his problem."

Bram smiled.

"Good."

Mikel's expression changed.

"Oh, you bastard."

"Also good."

The test should have ended there.

It did not.

Because while Mikel was complaining, a charcoal cart arrived early.

Its driver had a valid supplier tally for the lower gate.

Its second man had none.

"New hand," the driver said.

Mikel looked at Bram.

Bram looked at Mikel.

Neither spoke.

The second man shifted uneasily.

"Name?" Mikel asked.

"Tarris."

"Full name."

"Tarris Wen."

The driver said, "He's my sister's boy."

"Congratulations. He still isn't on the list."

"We load before dawn."

"Then somebody knew he was coming before dawn."

The driver began to get angry.

Bram watched Mikel's face.

This was the real test now.

Not whether Mikel knew the rule.

Whether he could enforce it without turning inconvenience into humiliation.

Mikel exhaled.

"Cart stays outside the bar. You can unload there if you want. Your nephew waits while I send to the yard clerk. If the clerk confirms him, he comes in. If not, he goes home. Nobody is accusing him of anything."

The driver muttered something about kings and mountains.

But he stopped shouting.

The clerk confirmed Tarris twenty minutes later.

The mistake belonged to a charcoal factor who had added a worker after the day's list was copied.

The correction was entered.

Tarris came through.

No one was arrested.

No gate was closed.

No conspiracy was discovered.

At dawn, Rhyse called that success.

Caldus Ren called it proof that bureaucracy had conquered courage.

Mikel Orr called it twenty minutes of his life he wanted back.

All three opinions entered the court by breakfast.

---

Luceran read the first night's report beside Miren in the small solar.

She was eating pear slices from his plate.

"Your guards have discovered administration."

"Apparently it is worse than combat."

"Combat ends."

He looked at her.

"You sound like Avarren."

"Cruel."

Luceran read the line about the charcoal nephew again.

"Caldus says the new rules dishonor sworn men."

"Does he believe that?"

"I think so."

"Then don't make him secretly evil because he's annoying."

Luceran lowered the page.

"I wasn't planning to."

"You make a face."

"What face?"

She demonstrated it badly.

"I do not look like that."

"You do when somebody is wrong in a way that inconveniences you."

He took back a pear slice before she could eat it.

"Orren Vale agrees with Rhyse. Hesta Mor mostly agrees. Caldus doesn't."

"Then the Companions aren't one thing."

"Nothing is one thing anymore."

"That is generally what happens when you learn people's names."

He smiled.

Then he looked toward the window, where the lower road could just be seen between roofs and pine.

"Tovan wants more night guards."

"Can he have them?"

"Not without taking them from somewhere else."

"Then perhaps he wants more guards in the kingdom."

"That costs money."

"Yes."

"You are not helpful."

"I am extremely helpful. I remind you that wanting a thing does not summon it."

He reached for the report again.

At the bottom Rhyse had written the part Luceran disliked most.

**No credential system proves allegiance. It establishes accountable access. A compromised person with a valid mark remains compromised. A loyal person with the wrong mark remains delayed. Procedures reduce ambiguity; they do not remove betrayal.**

Luceran read it twice.

The castle felt safer than it had a week ago.

Not safe.

Safer.

That difference mattered.

So did the fact that the improvement depended on people who argued with one another, forgot to update lists, borrowed laundry cord, resented being stopped, protected private information, wanted promotions, wanted sleep, and occasionally thought their king was being an idiot.

He could command them.

He could not make them simple.

For the first time, that seemed less like a defect.

---

At the west service door, Edrik handed the post book to his relief.

"Garren Holt moved from east yard. Correction is here. Baker comes at first bell. Laundry donkey is apparently a citizen of impeccable character. Quill's people have before-light restrictions unless she marks them. No exact royal route comes through this book. If somebody claims it should, send for Bram."

The relieving guard ran a finger down the page.

"What about this?"

A line near the bottom read:

**UNKNOWN MAN — asked twice this week which watch holds west service after midnight. Description inconsistent. No offense committed. Do not invent one. Record if repeated.**

Edrik looked at it.

The first report had come from a kitchen porter. The second from a stone carter. One thought the man was old. One thought young. Neither had a name.

It might be nothing.

It might be somebody learning the guard.

"That," Edrik said, "is why you read the book."

He left.

The relieving guard read the line again before he turned the page.
