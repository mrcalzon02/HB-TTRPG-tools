# Season One, Episode 28 — The Price of Betrayal

By breakfast, the coup had discovered paperwork.

Men who had spent the night forcing doors now stood in corridors asking servants where the duty books were kept. Someone had found red cord for sealing rooms. Someone else had found a clerk willing to write *By order of the Emergency Council* across the top of fresh sheets of paper.

Edselsto looked less conquered in daylight than badly administered.

The blood had been scrubbed from the great hall and had left pale brown shadows in the mortar. A chair stood beneath the old council-chamber latch because the proper stool had vanished. Three armed men argued over whether kitchen deliveries required a military escort. No one knew who had authority to release lamp oil.

Sir Aven Marr had slept for forty minutes.

When he entered the old council chamber, the crown was where he had left it: inside its oak coffer, beneath two wax seals bearing his signet, watched by four Royal Companions who had served beside him before any of this had become possible.

Cale Rusk was already waiting.

"You sealed it," Rusk said.

"Yes."

"From us."

"From everyone."

Rusk smiled.

Aven disliked that smile more each time he saw it.

On the table lay the first arrest list.

Aven read six names and stopped.

"Mera Doss is already detained."

"Then that one is easy."

"She surrendered herself."

"She admitted giving us access."

"She admitted being paid to sign a movement she should not have signed. She also warned the defenders."

Rusk leaned back.

"Do you intend to reward betrayal because it changed direction?"

"I intend to distinguish testimony from disposal."

"That sounds expensive."

Aven put the list down.

"Who wrote this?"

"The council's security committee."

"Which members?"

"You keep asking for names as though names improve facts."

"They improve responsibility."

Rusk's expression barely changed.

Aven had spent his adult life among men who believed law was a weapon civilized people agreed to hold by the handle. Rusk seemed to believe the handle was simply the part one used until stabbing became convenient.

"Nothing happens to Doss without my order," Aven said.

"Of course."

"No transfer. No private questioning. No movement from her present room."

"Of course."

Aven looked at him.

Rusk looked back.

"Put that in writing," Aven said.

For the first time that morning, Rusk stopped smiling.

---

Nera Vos found the washhouse wage book because Mera had told the guard exactly where it would be.

Third shelf. Behind the soap tallies. Blue cloth cover with a grease mark shaped like a thumb.

The book was there.

Two pages were not.

Nera stood with it open in both hands.

The missing leaves had been cut close to the stitching rather than torn. Whoever had removed them had used a small knife and had known that rag paper tore noisily when pulled from a tight binding.

"What dates?" asked the guard.

Nera counted backward.

"Six weeks."

"Anything important?"

"Wages."

"Whose?"

"Everyone's."

The guard waited for more.

Nera gave him the look washhouse women reserved for people who had mistaken brevity for ignorance.

"Wages mean shifts. Shifts mean who was here. Who was sick. Who covered. Who had children needing money. Who took advances. Who lost hours."

The guard looked again at the cut pages.

"Parel?"

"I don't know Parel."

"Mera says he knew about her daughter."

"Half the washhouse knows about her daughter."

"Her dye vat?"

Nera's mouth tightened.

"Fewer."

"Her rent?"

"Fewer."

"Her old guard-washing rotation?"

Nera closed the book.

"Three."

"Names."

"Me. Mera. Pellon Rusk."

The guard stared.

Nera stared back.

"No relation that I know of."

"Where is he?"

"He should have opened the lower drying room before dawn."

"Did he?"

"No."

"Where does he live?"

"Village east row. His sister keeps goats."

The guard reached for the book.

Nera did not release it.

"Receipt."

"What?"

"If you take a wage book from my washhouse, you sign for it."

"There has been a coup."

"Then I imagine records are unusually important."

The guard found a clerk.

Nera got her receipt.

---

Pellon Rusk's sister had been waiting since sunrise.

Not at the castle gate. Ardel Kaelmont's stone carts still blocked the lower road, and Ardel had converted the obstruction into something halfway between a checkpoint and a declaration of personal sovereignty.

She sat on an overturned granite block drinking tea while two of her retainers inspected anyone claiming Kaelmont authority.

The woman with the goats had been turned away twice.

On the third attempt she shouted.

"My brother works in your damned castle!"

Ardel looked over the rim of her cup.

"Most people shouting at me this morning have brothers in my damned castle."

"He didn't come home."

"That narrows it slightly."

"He always comes home."

Ardel set the cup down.

The woman was perhaps fifty, weathered, furious and frightened enough that the two emotions had become indistinguishable.

"Name?"

"Pellon Rusk."

Ardel's gaze sharpened.

"Relation to Cale Rusk?"

"Who?"

"Good answer."

"I don't know any Cale."

"Also possibly a good answer."

The woman stepped forward.

"He opens the drying room. He eats with me on sixthday because I make onion bread. He did not come home."

"When was he last seen?"

"Yesterday before the bells."

"Did he carry anything?"

"A lunch."

"Anything unusual?"

"Why would I know?"

"Because people notice unusual things and then insist later they noticed nothing."

The woman rubbed her hands together.

"He had new boots."

Ardel waited.

"He said he won at dice."

"Does he win at dice?"

"No."

That was useful.

Ardel stood.

"You're not entering the castle."

"My brother—"

"But I will send someone who knows what he looks like."

"Why should I trust you?"

"You shouldn't. You should remember my name, the time, and exactly what I promise. Ardel Kaelmont. I will ask whether Pellon Rusk is inside, wounded, detained or dead. If I learn which, I will tell you."

The woman's jaw worked.

"That's all?"

"That's more than anyone promised you before breakfast."

It was enough to keep her at the road.

It was also enough to make Pellon Rusk a person somebody would miss.

---

Mera heard about the missing pages from the same guard who had carried her message.

He did not tell her out of kindness. Sir Aven had ordered that her questioning be recorded, and the guard had decided that meant answers should be tested while they were fresh.

"Pellon," Mera said.

"You know him."

"I know everyone in the washhouse."

"Did he know about your daughter?"

"He knew she wanted the vat."

"The rent?"

Mera frowned.

"Maybe."

"Your old guard rotation?"

"Yes."

The guard wrote.

"Was he Parel?"

"No."

"How do you know?"

"Parel had all his teeth."

The guard looked up.

Mera almost laughed.

"Pellon lost two on the left when a wringer handle kicked back three years ago. Parel smiled at me plenty."

"Could Pellon have told him?"

"Yes."

"Would he?"

Mera looked at the locked door.

Yesterday she would have said no.

Yesterday had become an expensive place to live.

"He borrowed money."

"For what?"

"Everything. Cards. Boots. His sister's winter roof once. Then he paid it back and borrowed again."

"From whom?"

"I don't know."

The guard wrote that too.

Mera watched the pencil.

"Did you find him?"

"No."

"Then find him before the men who paid him do."

The guard's hand stopped.

"Why?"

"Because I confessed."

She swallowed.

"Pellon didn't."

---

They found his lunch first.

A heel of dark bread, two pickled onions and a piece of hard cheese sat wrapped in cloth behind the lower drying-room furnace.

His coat hung from its peg.

His new boots were gone.

The drying room had two exits: one into the washhouse and one narrow coal passage leading toward a service yard.

The coal passage door was locked from the yard side.

There was blood on the lower hinge.

Not much.

Enough.

The guard sent for Sir Aven.

Cale Rusk arrived first.

That was the second useful thing.

"Accident?" Rusk asked.

Nera Vos stood beside the furnace.

"No."

"You are a laundress."

"Yes."

"Then perhaps allow soldiers to—"

"If he cut himself here, there'd be blood where he cut himself."

Rusk looked at her.

Nera pointed.

"The blood is outside the swing. Someone bled after the door opened."

Rusk crouched.

Aven entered before he could touch it.

"Don't."

Rusk rose.

"You summoned me?"

"I did not."

"No. Someone else did."

Aven looked at the guard.

"I sent for you, sir."

"Good."

Aven examined the room without crossing the blood.

"Pellon Rusk?"

"Missing," the guard said. "Washhouse worker. Possible information source for the recruiter called Parel."

Rusk's face did not move.

Aven noticed anyway.

"Do you know him?"

"No."

"Same surname."

"Half the eastern valley has six surnames."

"Perhaps."

Aven turned to the coal door.

"Open it from outside."

Rusk said, "We have a castle to secure."

Aven looked back.

"This is securing it."

---

The body was not in the yard.

That would have been simple.

The trail ended at a cart rut where rainwater and traffic had turned the ground to paste. Someone had dragged something heavy far enough to reach wheels.

They found one of Pellon's new boots under a stack of empty charcoal sacks.

There was blood inside it.

The other boot was gone.

The sister identified the first before noon.

Ardel did not make her walk to the castle.

She brought the boot down wrapped in sacking.

The woman stared at it for a long time.

"He hated buckles," she said.

Ardel said nothing.

"He said these were cheap."

Ardel crouched so they were at the same height.

"Did he say where he bought them?"

"No."

"Who gave him the money?"

"He said dice."

"Did you believe him?"

"No."

"Why didn't you ask?"

The woman's face twisted.

"Because the roof doesn't leak."

There it was.

Not conspiracy. Not ideology.

A dry roof.

Ardel knew enough about great houses to understand how often history was built from somebody else's roof.

"I need the boot," she said.

The woman nodded.

Then she grabbed Ardel's wrist.

"If he's dead, don't tell me he died for a king."

"I won't."

"He didn't care about kings."

"I believe you."

"He cared about being cold."

Ardel carried that sentence back up the road.

---

Sir Aven ordered the coal yard sealed.

Cale Rusk objected.

Aven wrote the order himself.

By midday they had three facts.

Pellon had access to washhouse routines.

Pages covering six weeks of wage and shift records had been cut from the book.

Pellon had acquired boots and roof money he could not readily explain.

They did not have Parel.

They did not have Pellon's body.

They did not know whether Pellon had sold information knowingly, passed gossip carelessly, been threatened, or simply introduced one man to another.

Aven refused to let uncertainty become convenience.

Rusk found this exhausting.

"You seized a castle," he said. "Now you are investigating a missing washerman."

"I seized a castle because I claimed lawful government had failed."

"And?"

"It would be embarrassing to demonstrate my alternative by murdering washermen."

"You assume murder."

"I assume nothing. That is why the yard is sealed."

Rusk's patience broke by a fraction.

"People disappear during violence."

"Yes."

"People flee."

"Yes."

"People steal."

"Yes."

"Then perhaps your washerman bought boots, cut pages from a book and ran."

"Perhaps."

Aven leaned closer.

"Which is why I would very much like to know why you reached the room before I did."

Silence.

Rusk smiled again, but this time it arrived late.

"Your guards talk."

"Mine do."

Aven left him with that.

---

In the old laundry gallery above the eastern kitchens, Bram Tolland was developing a fever.

Mikel Orr had already developed one.

Edrik had discovered that hiding twelve people required more water than heroism.

They had three jugs, stale linen, half a sack of starch, two knives, one sword and a service door they could not open without risking the corridor.

Below them, boots passed at irregular intervals.

Bram lay against the wall with his bandaged thigh extended.

"How long?"

"Before what?"

"Before you tell me I'm too sick to make decisions."

Edrik considered.

"Yesterday."

"Good."

A faint scrape came from the wall.

Everyone froze.

Three scrapes.

Pause.

Two.

Anja's pattern.

Edrik moved the warped panel aside.

Anja Pell crawled through dust first, then one of her guards.

The second guard was not with her.

Bram saw the answer before he asked.

"Captured?"

"Separated."

"Alive?"

"Was when I lost him."

Anja sat hard on the floor.

"They're inventorying the works passages."

"Who?"

"Not Aven's Companions."

That mattered.

She drank from the jug and stopped herself after two swallows.

"I heard them looking for people who helped with receiving. Names. Cart drivers. Washhouse. Grain."

Edrik looked at Bram.

"Cleaning."

"Maybe."

"That's what it sounds like."

Bram closed his eyes briefly.

"Evidence first. Conclusions later."

Anja gave him a tired smile.

"You've become unbearable."

"I was unbearable before."

"True."

She reached inside her coat and produced a folded scrap.

"I took this from a man checking the unfinished east run."

It was not an arrest list.

It was a payment list.

Initials, sums, and marks indicating paid, due or transferred.

One line had been scratched through so hard the paper had nearly torn.

**P.R. — 18 — due**

Beside it someone had written:

**settled**

Bram stared at the word.

"Can we prove who P.R. is?"

"No."

"Can we prove what settled means?"

"No."

"Can we keep the paper?"

"Yes."

"Then today is improving."

Nobody laughed.

---

Mera's transfer order arrived after sunset.

It carried Sir Aven Marr's name.

The guard read it twice.

Then a third time.

Mera stood.

"Where?"

"Lower holding."

"Why?"

"Security consolidation."

The guard looked at the signature.

Aven had personally ordered that she not be moved.

He had also personally signed this.

Or someone wanted it to look that way.

"Sit down," he said.

Mera sat.

The two men who had brought the order shifted.

"We have instructions."

"So do I."

"Newest order controls."

"Not until I verify it."

One of the men sighed.

"We don't have time for this."

The guard's hand moved toward his sword.

"Then you definitely have time for this."

The corridor became very quiet.

The second man said, "We'll fetch an officer."

"Do."

They left.

The guard waited until their steps disappeared.

Then he looked at Mera.

"Do you know them?"

"No."

"Neither do I."

He barred the door.

Mera's hands began shaking.

"Was the signature real?"

"I don't know."

"What happens if they come back with more men?"

"I find out whether Sir Aven meant what he wrote."

That was not comforting.

It was, however, a plan.

---

Aven had not signed the transfer.

The signature was good enough to survive a hurried glance.

The seal impression was worse.

It had been made from a seal used on one of the surrender copies.

Not the seal itself.

An impression copied from warm wax while the original document was still soft.

Aven understood the implication before anyone explained it.

Someone had been close enough to his own administrative chain to see the surrender terms before they hardened.

His constitutional coup had not merely admitted men he could not control.

It had given them his authority to imitate.

He went to the old council chamber and found Rusk gone.

The crown remained sealed.

One of the four Companions said, "He left ten minutes ago."

"With whom?"

"Two men."

"Names?"

"I don't know them."

Aven's anger was quiet.

"From now on, if you don't know a man, he does not pass a door because he is standing beside someone you do."

The Companion stiffened.

"Yes, sir."

Aven almost laughed.

Bram Tolland had probably issued the same order yesterday.

That was the price of betrayal too.

Eventually, everyone learned the lesson of the people they had defeated.

---

At dusk, Pellon's sister lit a lamp in a house with a repaired roof.

He did not come home.

At the washhouse, Nera Vos wrote the missing dates back into a fresh ledger from memory, leaving blank spaces where memory failed rather than pretending certainty.

At the chapel, Calienne kept Aven's surrender terms beneath a medicine chest and refused three requests to surrender wounded men before they could stand.

In the old laundry gallery, Anja's stolen payment list dried beneath Bram's hand.

At the village road, Ardel placed Pellon's single boot beside the false Kaelmont credential and told one of her clerks to record who had delivered each object, when, and from where.

Mera remained alive in the same locked room.

That fact had required more resistance than her death would have.

And somewhere beyond the mountain roads, Luceran kept riding toward a castle whose new rulers had already begun betraying one another.

All seven lights burned through the night.

Edselsto did nothing.

People were doing enough.
