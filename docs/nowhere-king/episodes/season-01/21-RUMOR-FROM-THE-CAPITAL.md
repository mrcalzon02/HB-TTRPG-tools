# Season One, Episode 21 — Rumor From the Capital

The first report from the capital arrived before Luceran's demand to House Valcere could possibly have reached it.

That fact saved Deren Holt from becoming responsible for everything.

Pellin Joss put the folded broadside on the breakfast table between the butter and a bowl of stewed apples.

"It was copied in Vey Crossing three days ago," he said. "A carter brought it up with the salt wagons."

Luceran read the heading twice.

THE MOUNTAIN COURT CLOSES ITS DOORS.

Below it, somebody with an excellent memory and an unfriendly pen had assembled facts.

The new credential system was real.

The west-service watches were real.

Kaelmont's carriage opportunity was real.

The Moncler compensation dispute was real.

So were the Seven Ancestral Lights, the argument over burial jurisdiction, the king's relationship with Miren Edevane, the village property protections, the Crown's grain negotiations, and the fact that several old routes inside Edselsto remained barred.

Almost nothing in the sheet was invented.

That was what made it good.

Miren read over his shoulder.

"Apparently I am governing the kingdom from your bed."

"You have been very lazy about it."

"I thought so too."

Pellin coughed into his fist.

Luceran continued.

Kaelmont had not received a bounded carriage opportunity; the Crown had "placed the northern approaches in friendly hands." Moncler had not received a compensation review after royal seizure of labor; the king was "unable to satisfy debts incurred in flight." Guard credentials were not an ordinary response to real security failures; Edselsto had become a fortress in which petitioners could no longer reach their sovereign. The Crown's refusal to surrender the candles to temple jurisdiction became hostility to proper religion.

The village protections became seizure because the Crown had surveyed land.

The grain negotiation became shortage because grain was being negotiated at all.

Luceran set the sheet down.

"Who printed it?"

"Nobody," Pellin said. "Not originally. Scribal copy. Then another. This one was copied from a copy in Vey Crossing."

"Author?"

"Unsigned."

"Valcere?"

"Could be."

Luceran waited.

Pellin added, "Could also be anyone who attended court, employed a servant, bought a drink from a carter, wrote home, heard a sermon, sold us timber, or knows Miren has a bedroom."

Miren took a piece of bread.

"My bedroom has become a remarkably productive institution."

Luceran looked at Pellin.

"You are enjoying this."

"Only the parts that are funny."

"Which parts are those?"

"Not the next one."

He produced three letters.

---

The broadside was one story.

The letters were three different kingdoms reacting to it.

The first came from a Damaris factor at a river depot. He did not care whether Miren Edevane slept upside down from the ceiling. He cared that merchants in the old capital had begun asking whether Edselsto's grain purchases meant the northern stores were failing.

Prices had risen two copper weights on rumor alone.

The second came from a junior Orsanne canon in Southmere. He had received a much shorter account claiming the Crown had forbidden proper clergy from the Seven Lights and was inventing a private royal cult.

The third came under Bellisar seal.

Tovan Rhyse read that one standing.

A frontier captain had heard that Edselsto was closing its roads and concentrating guards because Luceran expected revolt. Two neighboring garrisons had delayed routine leave rather than risk being short-handed if the rumor proved true.

Nobody had ordered mobilization.

Nobody had declared rebellion.

Nobody had even agreed on what was supposedly happening.

But horses were being kept in barracks yards that should have been resting in pasture, grain factors were changing prices, and a priest two hundred miles away was preparing a sermon about candles he had never seen.

Luceran said, "How many people have this?"

Pellin shrugged.

"Which version?"

That was the answer.

---

By noon the council chamber contained five proposed solutions and no agreement about the problem.

Master Clavard Clifford wanted a formal royal correction carried to the largest provincial courts.

"Not a denial of every insult," he said. "A statement of facts. Roads remain open. Petition remains available. No betrothal exists. No temple has been expelled."

Mara Kest shook her head.

"If you answer every copied sheet, you teach every bored clerk in the kingdom how to command the royal chancery."

Rhyse wanted neither silence nor argument.

"I want to know whether garrisons are changing readiness because of it."

Miren wanted Edevane correspondence opened rather than guessed at.

"My family will have heard three versions by now. If you ask me what they think before I read what they wrote, I can provide you a beautifully informed fiction."

Luceran looked to Ardel Kaelmont.

Ardel had been quiet.

"Your house is accused of buying my roads."

"We should have bought them more cheaply."

"Ardel."

"Kaelmont will dislike the accusation because it makes us look successful enough to tax. We will also dislike any royal correction that sounds as though you regret dealing with us. Those are separate dislikes."

Miren's eyes moved to her, then back to Luceran.

The movement was small enough that only someone already watching for it would have noticed.

Luceran noticed.

He did not answer it.

Ardel continued.

"The carriage opportunity is defensible because it is nonexclusive, bounded, and useful. Defend the terms if you must. Do not defend my virtue."

Clavard rubbed his forehead.

"Nobody proposed defending your virtue."

"Good. It is expensive."

That got a laugh, which helped for perhaps six seconds.

Then Rhyse laid the Bellisar letter on the table.

"This is what concerns me."

Mara read it.

"Two delayed leave rotations."

"Yes."

"That is not an army."

"No."

"It is barely a decision."

"It is two officers deciding uncertainty is expensive enough to keep men and horses available. If ten more make the same decision, roads change. Feed changes. Local lords notice. Then somebody else sees preparation and prepares in answer."

Luceran understood the shape of it.

Not conspiracy.

Reciprocal caution.

The kingdom could frighten itself with perfectly rational decisions made by people who possessed different scraps of the same story.

"Then we need to know what is actually moving," he said.

Rhyse nodded.

"Yes."

Clavard said, "And we still need to answer the political story."

"Also yes."

Mara looked at Luceran.

"Which means you cannot solve this with one proclamation."

"You say that as though you expect me to be disappointed."

"I know you."

"Cruel woman."

"Majesty."

---

The most useful correction did not come from the king.

It came from Canon Calienne Orsanne, who discovered before supper that three copies of the Southmere religious account had reached the lower village.

She read one in the Hall of Veils while a mason was measuring damaged plaster above the western alcove.

Brother Talen Veyr waited for outrage.

Calienne laughed.

"They say I was denied the nave."

"You were not."

"I know. I was there."

"Should we send a correction?"

"To whom?"

Talen looked at the page.

That was harder.

Calienne folded it.

"Merel will enjoy this."

"Canon Merel did object to the Crown's language."

"Merel objects professionally. That does not mean he invented this."

She found Mother Ysara before she found Luceran.

Together they wrote to Orsanne houses and temple foundations they personally knew. Not a royal defense. Not a theological ruling. A clerical account: ordinary clergy remained admitted under ordinary household rules; funerary jurisdiction was disputed in specific places; the Seven Lights remained physically in the royal nave; their ancient purpose remained unknown.

Calienne refused Luceran's request to let the royal seal accompany it.

"Then it becomes your defense," she said.

"It is my castle."

"It is my name at the bottom of the letter."

"You are very difficult."

"That is why you asked me."

He let her send it without his seal.

Later, Miren said, "You hated that."

"A little."

"Good."

"Why good?"

"Because she can say something for you precisely because she is not saying it for you."

Luceran looked toward the messenger yard where Calienne's letters were already being bundled by destination.

"I am beginning to suspect sovereignty consists mostly of discovering things I cannot order people to do effectively."

"That is one of its more charming features."

---

Pellin spent the afternoon discovering that rumor had logistics.

A copied sheet did not move by magic.

It moved because copyists wanted coin, messengers wanted return loads, merchants wanted information, priests wanted sermons, nobles wanted warnings, and travelers wanted something interesting to repeat at dinner.

He and Selise Varo worked at opposite ends of an archive table under Ysabet's supervision.

Selise was still barred from current correspondence.

That mattered.

So Pellin did not hand her the Bellisar or Damaris letters.

Instead he gave her three public copies of the hostile account and asked her to compare phrasing.

She found that they were not copies of one original.

One called the guards "the king's locked household."

Another called them "new mountain men."

A third did not mention guards at all but spent half a page on Kaelmont tolls.

"Different parents," Selise said.

Ysabet looked up.

"Explain."

"They share facts, not sentences. Someone did not write one master account and distribute it. People are building versions from a common stock of news."

Pellin leaned back.

"Which means arresting one writer would accomplish almost nothing."

Selise gave him a tired look.

"It would give the next writer a better story."

Ysabet almost smiled.

Almost.

Selise saw that too and returned to her papers.

The damaged trust between them remained damaged.

But the work was real.

By evening Pellin could identify several ordinary routes by which Edselsto news reached the capital and provinces: merchant letters, household correspondence, temple networks, discharged workers, guild notices, court visitors, road inns, and paid political digests like Holt's.

No single hand controlled them.

That was worse in one way and better in another.

There was no throat to cut.

There was also no mastermind required.

---

The Edevane reply arrived after dark.

It had been written before Miren could have sent word of Corin Varo.

Her uncle's clerk reported that capital salons were treating the mountain move as evidence Luceran feared scrutiny. Edevane cousins were being asked whether Miren had secured a marriage promise. One provincial relative wanted to know whether he should postpone sending his eldest son to Edselsto because "the northern court may soon require soldiers rather than pages."

Miren read that line twice.

Luceran watched her.

"What do you think?"

"I think my cousin is frightened and pretending to ask about his son."

"Of what?"

"Of choosing the wrong court if this becomes a contest between the capital and you."

"There is no other court."

"Legally? No. Socially? There is always another room where people decide you are not present."

He sat beside her.

"Will Edevane support me?"

Miren did not answer immediately.

He appreciated the delay more than reassurance.

"Edevane will support Edevane," she said. "Usually that includes you. It includes me. It includes the dynasty. Those things are not identical."

"And Corin?"

"We do not know yet."

"You could tell me he is a distant clerk and save us both discomfort."

"I could."

"Thank you for not doing it."

She leaned against him.

"You are painfully improving."

"Rhyse has copyrighted that insult."

"He can sue me."

For a while they sat without discussing the kingdom.

That did not make the kingdom disappear.

It made the distinction worth preserving.

---

The next morning brought the report Rhyse had asked for.

Not from spies.

From officers.

Routine readiness returns had begun arriving because he had sent sealed questions to garrison captains after reading the Bellisar letter: present strength, horses fit for road service, grain days on hand, wagons available, leave delayed, and the time required to assemble for movement if ordered.

The answers were ugly mostly because they were ordinary.

One garrison could put eighty riders on the road in two days but only if it borrowed remounts.

Another had men but not wagons.

A third had wagons but a bridge under repair.

Two companies were under strength after autumn sickness.

A Bellisar post had kept leave suspended because of the rumor.

A Harrowmere officer had done the opposite and refused to alter routine without an order, specifically because neighboring preparations looked like rumor feeding rumor.

Jeron Bellisar sent his own note from Edselsto after reviewing the returns.

He still could not ride.

His leg remained useless for field command.

His judgment was not.

He marked three northern approaches where small troop movements could be mistaken for concentration and two depots whose grain stocks were insufficient for sustained movement.

Then he wrote one sentence beneath the figures.

If His Majesty wishes to display strength, first determine how much strength can arrive hungry.

Luceran read it aloud.

Clavard said, "He has become cheerful in convalescence."

Rhyse did not smile.

"We need an army question."

"We have an army."

"We have soldiers. I need to know what portion can move, where, under whom, how quickly, with what food, without stripping Edselsto, the roads, and the frontier posts that are currently frightening one another."

Mara said, "And asking the question will itself be noticed."

"Yes."

Luceran looked around the table.

The hostile sheet had accused him of hiding in a mountain fortress.

His first instinct had been to prove he was not hiding.

Now that seemed almost childish.

A proclamation could deny weakness.

A parade could display men.

Neither told him whether those men could reach a border with food after a bridge washed out.

"No general summons," he said. "No theatrical mustering. Send for the people who actually know the roads, depots, horses, companies, bridges, grain, and command obligations. Bellisar. Harrowmere. Kaelmont. Damaris. Rhyse. The quartermasters. The road officers. Whoever else they say we need."

Clavard asked, "For what purpose do I put on the summons?"

Luceran looked again at Jeron's figures.

"Readiness. Not war."

Rhyse said, "Those are different things."

"I know."

Outside, Edselsto's gates remained open.

Merchants entered beneath the credential posts. Masons worked on the Hall of Veils. Kaelmont carts came up the road under terms half the capital had apparently rewritten for entertainment. In the family nave, seven candles continued to burn for reasons nobody living could prove.

By midday, sealed invitations were riding out from the mountain.

Not orders to march.

Questions about how marching would actually work.

That was enough to change the kingdom again.
