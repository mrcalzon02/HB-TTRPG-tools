# Season One, Episode 18 — Temple and Crown

Sister Calienne Orsanne was stopped at the family nave by a guardswoman young enough to have been one of her novices.

Calienne looked at the woman's hand, which was open rather than resting on the sword at her hip, and decided that somebody had trained her properly.

"Your name?"

"Anja Pell, my lady."

"Sister is sufficient."

"Sister, then. Captain Rhyse's order says the nave is open for ordinary household devotion. The family gallery beyond it is not."

Calienne held up the folded royal invitation she had been carrying since breakfast. "And this?"

"Gets you into the nave."

"I was invited to inspect the ancestral observances."

"Then someone who can authorize the gallery will come and authorize the gallery."

Behind Calienne, Canon Merel Dast made a small sound of satisfaction.

Calienne did not turn around. "If you laugh, Canon, I will tell everyone you were stopped at the lower gate because you left your tally in a privy."

"That would be malicious."

"It would also be true."

Anja's mouth twitched.

Calienne noticed and approved of her slightly more.

The nave smelled of old wax, cold stone and pine smoke carried in from elsewhere in the castle. Seven tall candles burned before the royal screen. Their flames were steady despite a draft that touched Calienne's cheek.

She had seen drawings of them. She had read three contradictory temple commentaries about them. She had also read a family memorandum insisting that temple commentary was none of the temple's business.

That last document had been written by a king who had later endowed two hospitals and died with an Orsanne priest holding his hand.

Families were rarely consistent for longer than a generation. Temples merely had better shelves on which to preserve the evidence.

Merel moved toward the candles.

Anja said, "Canon."

He stopped.

"I am not touching them."

"Good."

"I was going to read the inscriptions."

"From there."

Merel looked at Calienne.

"Do not involve me," she said. "I have already decided I like her."

The third member of their party, Brother Talen Veyr of the Ashen Lamp, had said nothing since entering. He was broad, grey-haired and dressed in the plain black wool of his order. He looked not at the candles but at the memorial names cut into the side wall.

"These are household dead," he said.

"Some," Calienne answered.

"Some are servants."

"Yes."

"And two are masons."

Merel glanced over. "Is that surprising?"

"Only if you think ancestral means noble."

Calienne smiled despite herself.

That was the argument, or one of them.

By noon there would be six more.

---

Luceran had expected theology.

He had not expected drainage.

"The Hall of Veils cannot take another winter of funerals if the north wall continues sweating," said Mother Ysara Orsanne, who was seventy-two, tiny, and capable of making a statement about damp plaster sound like a declaration of war. "If Your Majesty intends Edselsto to remain a royal seat, then people will die here."

Luceran looked at Mara Henn.

Mara looked back without sympathy.

"She is right about the wall."

"I know she's right about the wall."

"Then stop looking at me as if I invented mortality."

Around the long table, several people failed to hide smiles.

The meeting had been called to discuss ancestral rites and the seven candles. It had acquired burial ground, winter access, drainage, guard jurisdiction, village graves, funeral fees, the right of temple officers to inspect royal memorial spaces, and whether a royal servant buried under Crown patronage belonged to the household dead or the parish dead.

Luceran was beginning to understand why his ancestors had preferred wars.

Wars at least usually admitted what they were about.

Mother Ysara tapped the table. "The village burial field is already protected by the agreement made when the works expanded. I am not asking to seize it. I am asking what happens when the household grows large enough that the old ground cannot carry everyone who dies in royal service."

Bren Vess, invited because half the practical questions in Edselsto eventually became questions about somebody's grandfather's boundary stones, said, "You don't put them uphill of the spring."

"Agreed," Mara said.

"And you don't widen into the east strip without speaking to the families whose people are already there."

"Also agreed."

Mother Ysara inclined her head. "Then we have made more progress in forty breaths than three letters managed in a month."

Canon Merel folded his hands. "The burial question is important, but it is not why we were summoned."

"It is why I came," said Talen Veyr.

Merel's jaw tightened.

Luceran saw it and, more importantly, saw Calienne see it.

House Orsanne's public reputation encouraged outsiders to imagine a temple interest that spoke with one voice. In the room were four members of the same religious network who had already disagreed about why they were there.

Luceran found that reassuring.

Miren, seated at his right, leaned close enough that only he could hear.

"You look pleased."

"They disagree."

"You have developed peculiar tastes."

"Competence has ruined me."

Her knee touched his beneath the table. Deliberately, he thought. Probably.

He had learned not to confuse probably with knowledge.

Canon Merel said, "The seven lights are already becoming a public devotion."

Luceran's attention returned to him.

"They are becoming what?"

"People leave flowers."

Orven Dast, the old steward, frowned. "People leave flowers everywhere."

"They kneel."

"People kneel in a nave."

"They ask for blessings before journeys."

Orven looked genuinely offended. "From candles?"

Calienne covered a cough.

Merel persisted. "The court has returned to the ancestral seat. Seven lights have burned here beyond living memory. They survived neglect. They survived the absence of the court. They now burn before a restored royal household. People will assign meaning whether we authorize meaning or not."

That silenced the room because it was plainly true.

Luceran thought of the first night. Seven flames in an almost empty castle, Orven treating them as ordinary because they had always been ordinary to him.

"What meaning?" Luceran asked.

Merel answered too quickly. "Continuity of the Crown under divine witness."

Mother Ysara said, "No."

The canon turned toward her.

"No?"

"You have just invented doctrine because servants are leaving flowers."

"I have described the obvious interpretation."

"You have described the interpretation most flattering to the Crown and most useful to a temple that wishes to stand beside it. Those are not the same thing as obvious."

Merel flushed.

Luceran liked Mother Ysara more than was politically convenient.

Calienne said, "There is a narrower claim we can actually support. The lights are an ancestral observance associated with the royal household. Their original explanation is not presently established."

"That sounds like an archivist refusing to have an opinion," Merel said.

"It is a priest refusing to lie."

Talen Veyr spoke without raising his voice. "And if the lights are memorial?"

Nobody answered immediately.

He continued. "The names in the nave include servants and craftsmen. If the candles commemorate household dead, the Ashen Lamp has an interest in how the dead are invoked, whether rites are being performed for them, and whether anyone is claiming authority in their names."

"No one is," Orven said.

"Good."

"You sound disappointed."

"I am a funerary brother. I am delighted whenever the dead are being left alone."

That got the laugh the room needed.

Then Merel said, "The Crown cannot simply declare the lights private when they stand in a nave used by the household."

Luceran said, "I haven't."

"Not yet."

There it was.

Not theology. Jurisdiction.

Luceran sat back.

"Tell me what you actually want."

Merel hesitated.

Mother Ysara did not. "Funeral access that cannot be revoked by whichever guard dislikes a priest that morning. Consultation before burial ground is moved or expanded. No new royal rite involving the dead without a funerary authority present. Repair of the Hall of Veils north wall before winter."

"And the candles?"

"Leave them burning. Stop pretending we know why."

Merel stared at her.

"Mother—"

"You may ask for your own things, Canon."

So he did.

He wanted formal recognition that the seven lights were a public emblem of dynastic continuity and divine favor. He wanted a yearly rite around them. He wanted temple representatives guaranteed access to observe their maintenance.

Calienne wanted something different: permission to examine copies of old household inventories and ceremonial books for references to the lights, with family-private material excluded unless separately authorized.

Talen wanted a written prohibition against using the names, remains or likenesses of household dead in any new rite without consent from lawful kin where kin existed and funerary review where they did not.

Luceran listened.

Miren watched the listeners.

Rhyse watched the doors.

Orven watched everyone who spoke about the candles as though they were proposing to repaint his kitchen.

The court was becoming a capital because people had started fighting over things that had been sitting untouched for generations.

---

At the Hall of Veils, the damp was worse than Mother Ysara had claimed.

Luceran discovered this when a piece of plaster detached itself from the north wall and landed beside his boot.

Mara looked at him.

"Don't."

"I said nothing."

"Your face did."

The hall had once served royal funerals and village observances together. Its old arrangement made that obvious: a raised stone platform at one end, side doors toward the village track, a narrow covered passage toward the castle, storage niches for biers and cloth, and a washing room whose drain had long ago clogged with roots.

Three village women were already there airing old mourning cloth because Mother Ysara had asked them to determine what could be saved.

One of them, Jessa Morn, did not stop working when the king entered.

Luceran appreciated that.

"My mother was laid out here," she said while folding a black length of linen. "So was Lord Aster's groom. Same winter. Different tables. Same roof leak."

Merel looked uncomfortable.

Talen asked, "Who washed them?"

"My aunt and old Pella Reed."

"Temple?"

"People."

Talen nodded as if she had answered correctly.

Mother Ysara pointed toward the north wall. "The temple can pay for lime and two skilled plasterers. We cannot supply the stone work before winter."

Mara said, "Royal Works can supply stone if the drainage trench is done first. Otherwise you repair the wall and teach it to fail again."

"How much?"

"Less than a funeral. More than a sermon."

"Everything costs more than a sermon."

"Some sermons cost kingdoms."

Luceran left them bargaining.

That was deliberate.

He walked with Miren toward the side yard where old grave markers leaned beneath black pines.

"You left them," she said.

"They don't need me to price lime."

"Six months ago you would have stayed because leaving would have felt like losing control."

"Six months ago I thought control meant being in the room."

"And now?"

"Now I think it means knowing which room I should not be in."

She smiled.

"Careful. You may become tolerable."

He caught her hand where the wall hid them from most of the yard.

"That would ruin my reputation."

She kissed his cheek, quick and familiar.

Then she looked back toward the Hall of Veils.

"Merel is going to make the candles political whether you help him or not."

"I know."

"And if you forbid him?"

"He says I fear the symbol."

"If you endorse him?"

"I let him invent an ancestral doctrine because it flatters me."

"Good."

"Good?"

"You understand the trap."

"Understanding traps has not yet caused them to disappear."

"No. But it makes stepping into them less impressive."

He squeezed her fingers.

Across the yard, Calienne emerged carrying a stack of old funeral inventories.

She saw their joined hands.

She looked away with enough deliberate courtesy that Luceran immediately knew she had seen.

That knowledge would go somewhere.

Not necessarily maliciously. That was the more difficult lesson.

Information did not need an enemy to travel.

---

The afternoon dispute began when Canon Merel tried to enter the family archive with Calienne.

Sera Vale stopped him.

Calienne had a written archive warrant naming her and Ysabet Marris. Merel did not.

"I am her ecclesiastical superior in this inquiry."

"Are you named?"

"No."

"Then you are not entering."

"This is precisely the arbitrary gatekeeping Mother Ysara objected to."

"No," Calienne said. "This is precisely the opposite."

Merel turned on her.

"You are content to have a guardswoman decide which clergy may inspect religious evidence?"

Sera's expression hardened.

Calienne answered before she could.

"She is deciding who is named on a royal archive warrant. She has expressed no opinion about whether the candles represent continuity, dead masons or seven particularly persistent drafts."

Sera's mouth twitched.

Merel did not find it funny.

The argument reached Luceran because arguments involving barred doors always reached kings eventually.

He did not order Sera to admit Merel.

That mattered more than he expected.

Instead he asked Avarren whether the warrant could lawfully be amended.

It could.

Then he asked Calienne whether Merel's presence was necessary to her work.

She said no.

Merel's face went still.

"Then the warrant stands," Luceran said.

"Your Majesty chooses a guardswoman's tally over the Church."

"I choose the warrant over whichever important person is irritated by it. Yesterday that person was a Companion. Today it is you. Tomorrow it may be me."

Rhyse, standing near the door, said nothing.

Luceran knew what the silence meant anyway.

Merel bowed.

It was perfectly correct.

It was also angry.

A grievance had just been born, and being right did not make it unreal.

---

Calienne and Ysabet found no answer to the candles.

They found something more useful.

Three inventories separated by almost a century each listed the seven nave lights as Crown household charges: wax was never purchased for them, replacement wicks were not recorded, and their maintenance appeared under a formula that translated poorly into modern language.

Ysabet tried three versions.

"Kept," she said. "Maintained. Continued. None is exact."

"By whom?"

"It doesn't say."

"Temple?"

"No temple mark."

"Royal household?"

"The account sits among household charges. That proves where the clerk put it, not who understood it."

Calienne smiled.

"I like you."

"That is a dangerous basis for archival method."

"So I have recently been told."

They also found an old funeral order requiring that the lights not be extinguished during royal mourning.

Not because extinguishing them was dangerous. Not because they were magical. Not because they contained souls or guarded doors or meant anything that anyone alive could prove.

The instruction gave no reason.

Calienne wrote exactly that.

When Merel later asked what she had discovered, she told him.

He was disappointed.

"It supports continuity."

"It supports continued burning during royal mourning."

"Which is continuity."

"That is interpretation."

"All ritual is interpretation."

"Yes. Which is why we should label ours honestly."

He looked at her for a long moment.

"You embarrassed me before the king."

"You embarrassed yourself before a door."

"You enjoyed it."

Calienne considered lying.

"A little."

That almost saved them.

Almost.

---

By evening they had an agreement.

It was less glorious than Canon Merel wanted and more durable because of it.

The Hall of Veils would remain jointly usable for royal-household and village funerary purposes under its existing custom. Royal Works would provide stone and drainage labor before winter; House Orsanne's foundations would provide lime, plasterers and replacement mourning cloth where salvage failed. No expansion of burial ground would occur without village-family consultation, water review and Crown agreement.

Recognized funerary clergy would receive ordinary nave access during published devotional hours under the same access rules as other authorized household visitors. Archive, family-gallery and restricted-space access would still require specific authority. Guards would enforce warrants and thresholds, not decide doctrine.

No new Crown rite invoking named household dead would be established without funerary consultation.

The seven candles would receive no official declaration of divine favor.

Instead the Crown recognized them publicly as **the Seven Ancestral Lights of Edselsto**, an inherited household observance whose original meaning was not presently established. They would remain lit according to inherited custom. Temple scholars could research documentary references through ordinary archive permissions. Priests could preach interpretations as interpretations. The Crown would not certify those interpretations as historical fact.

Canon Merel hated the wording.

Mother Ysara called it unusually sensible, which probably meant somebody would attack it within a week.

Orven objected only to the new name.

"They were candles this morning."

"They are still candles," Luceran said.

"Then why do they need a title?"

"Because apparently kingdoms cannot leave nouns alone."

Orven considered that.

"Fair."

The first public consequence arrived before supper.

Someone had tied seven narrow strips of white cloth to the nave rail.

Nobody knew who.

By the time Luceran came to look, there were eleven.

"Seven lights," Miren said beside him. "Eleven ribbons. Excellent start."

"Should we remove them?"

"Do you want twelve by morning or forty?"

"Neither."

"Then I recommend accepting that other people exist."

Across the nave, Brother Talen quietly untied one ribbon that had been looped around a memorial name and moved it to the rail instead.

He did not forbid the devotion.

He did not let it appropriate a dead woman's name either.

Calienne watched him, then wrote something in her notebook.

Canon Merel stood farther back speaking with two courtiers. Luceran could not hear what he said.

He did not need to.

The candles had possessed public meaning before the day began. The mistake had been believing that because nobody had agreed upon the meaning, no meaning existed.

Now the disagreement itself had become part of court life.

Seven flames burned steadily in the old nave.

Luceran looked at them and felt, for a moment, the childish comfort of inheritance: something older than argument, older than his reign, older than anyone presently trying to use it.

Then he remembered Calienne's report.

The old records did not explain the lights.

His father had known things about Edselsto he had not explained either.

Comfort and ignorance, Luceran was learning, could occupy the same room.

He did not mistake one for the other.

Not yet.
