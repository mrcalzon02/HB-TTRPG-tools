# Season One, Episode 22 — The Army Question

The army arrived at Edselsto as arithmetic.

Not soldiers.

Arithmetic.

By the third morning after Luceran sent his questions, Mara Kest had covered the long table in the old map room with little stacks of paper weighted against the drafts by whatever came to hand: an inkstone, two pewter cups, a horseshoe, a mason's plumb weight, and one smooth river rock that nobody admitted bringing into the castle.

Tovan Rhyse stood at the head of the table and regarded the kingdom with professional dislike.

"That bridge is still out."

Ardel Kaelmont leaned over the northern road map.

"No. That bridge is half out."

"Can cavalry cross it?"

"Yes."

"Wagons?"

"One at a time if you enjoy gambling with flour."

"Artillery?"

"If you hate artillery."

Jeron Bellisar, seated because his healing leg remained braced straight beneath the table, tapped the margin with one finger.

"Then for military purposes it is out."

Ardel looked at him.

"For your purposes."

"Those are the purposes presently under discussion."

"A road continues to exist when an army is not using it, Lord Bellisar."

"An army notices that mostly when it cannot use one."

Luceran sat back and watched them disagree.

Three months ago he would have found it irritating that two people answering the same question could produce opposite answers while both clearly knew what they were talking about.

Now he was beginning to understand that this was what useful expertise sounded like.

The map room had been chosen because it was one of the few chambers large enough for the people the question required and ugly enough that nobody could mistake the gathering for a council of war. Its plaster was cracked. One shutter did not close properly. A faded hunting map still showed a road that had not existed for forty years.

Mara had crossed that road out in charcoal.

Around the table sat Rhyse, Jeron, Ardel, Master Clavard Clifford, Quartermaster Mara Kest, Tomas Reed from the river-depot work, Captain Maelin Harrow of the Ash Banner's provincial levies, Damaris factor Orel Venn, and two road officers whose names Luceran had learned that morning and intended not to forget by supper.

No one wore ceremonial armor.

That had been Rhyse's first victory.

The second had been refusing to let the gathering call itself a muster.

The third was proving harder.

"Begin again," Luceran said. "Not with how many soldiers exist. How many can move north within four days without borrowing food they do not have?"

Mara slid three returns toward him.

"If you mean north by the high road, two hundred and forty infantry from Crown obligations, perhaps ninety-eight mounted men fit for continuous road service, and forty-one more if you accept horses that should not be ridden four days in succession."

Jeron said, "Do not."

"I was not planning to injure horses for arithmetic."

"Kings have done worse for arithmetic."

Luceran looked at him.

Jeron did not apologize.

Good.

"Wagons?"

"Thirty-two fit," Mara said. "Eleven questionable. Six currently carrying stone or lime for Edselsto works."

Ardel said, "Eight Kaelmont contract wagons could be diverted after their present loads."

Orel Venn immediately asked, "At whose cost?"

Ardel turned her head.

"At the Crown's, if the Crown diverts them."

"Then say Crown wagons hired from Kaelmont, not Kaelmont wagons generously appearing."

Ardel smiled without warmth.

"I believe I just did."

Luceran rubbed the bridge of his nose.

"This is useful. Continue being unpleasant to one another."

That earned the first laugh of the morning.

It also made nobody less unpleasant.

---

The difficulty with feeding an army was that soldiers ate even when politics had not decided whether they were an army yet.

Orel Venn's figures were less dramatic than Rhyse's and more frightening.

Damaris factors could assemble grain quickly along the river corridor, but grain at a warehouse was not bread in a soldier's hand. Mills had capacity. Bakers had capacity. Barrels had to exist. Salt meat had to be purchased before everybody else learned the Crown wanted salt meat and raised the price. Draft animals ate too, and a wagon carrying fodder for horses was a wagon not carrying flour for men.

"Seven days," Orel said.

Luceran frowned. "For what?"

"For me to stop lying to you."

Clavard looked up from his notes.

Orel folded her hands.

"I can promise grain tomorrow. I can promise flour in three days. I can promise an impressive number of wagons in four. If you ask whether I can sustain five hundred men and their animals beyond the northern depots without stripping local stores or buying at panic prices, I want seven days of actual tallies."

"Suppose I have four."

"Then you have an estimate."

"How bad an estimate?"

"Good enough to march. Not good enough to know what the march costs afterward."

Tomas Reed spoke for the first time.

"And not good enough to know which depot empties first."

Orel glanced at him.

Tomas had spent most of the meeting with his hands clasped between his knees, looking uncomfortable among people whose houses appeared in chronicles. He was not noble. He had been given the river-depot assignment because he understood stores and movement and had demonstrated the inconvenient habit of checking whether numbers described physical things.

Luceran nodded to him.

"Show me."

Tomas rose.

He took a piece of chalk to the slate board and drew four boxes.

"Edselsto. Vey Crossing. Northbridge. Red Ford."

Then he drew lines between them.

"If you send grain from here, you use the mountain road and Kaelmont carriage. If you buy lower, you use river warehouses and local carts. If the force moves through Northbridge, the repaired span limits wagon intervals. If it moves through Red Ford after rain, the ford itself limits you."

Captain Maelin Harrow said, "The ford is passable."

"For men."

"For wagons too."

"Empty wagons."

Maelin's mouth tightened.

Tomas did not seem to notice.

"A loaded grain wagon sinks differently."

Ardel leaned back in her chair.

"I like him."

"You like anyone who insults a road accurately," Miren said from the window.

She had not been invited as Edevane's military representative. She had come because Luceran had asked her to listen to the political assumptions being made around the military ones, and because both of them had learned that pretending their private relationship had no political consequences merely left those consequences to other people.

Tomas drew another line.

"If His Majesty orders movement before the tallies are complete, I would stage food forward first. Quietly if possible. Flour, salt meat, oats, replacement shoes, wheel grease. Not enough to announce an army. Enough that the first companies do not consume the villages they pass through."

Rhyse asked, "How much?"

Tomas answered.

Mara corrected him by twelve barrels.

He looked at her figures, nodded, and corrected the slate.

Nobody died of humiliation.

Luceran made a note.

---

At noon Bram Tolland refused to give up twelve guards.

He did it in the lower guard office with such matter-of-fact certainty that for a moment Luceran thought he had misunderstood.

"I asked whether twelve could be reassigned to convoy security."

"Yes, Majesty."

"And you said no."

"Yes."

Bram pointed at the watch board.

Colored pegs marked gates, service entries, family corridors, stores, the lower works, the king's close route, and the relief reserve.

"I can give you four. Six if Captain Rhyse accepts longer reliefs for two nights. Twelve means taking people from fixed posts or ending the reserve."

Rhyse, beside Luceran, said nothing.

Luceran studied the board.

Episode Seventeen's reform had looked almost excessive when the immediate crisis had passed. Credentials. Reliefs. route channels. People whose job was to know exactly which door mattered and when.

Now every peg was also a soldier somebody might want elsewhere.

"What if the convoy carries military stores?"

"Then I would like it guarded."

"Helpful."

"Majesty asked what I could spare, not what I wanted."

Luceran looked at Rhyse.

"You trained him badly."

"I promoted him badly too."

Bram's expression did not move.

Luceran smiled despite himself.

"Four. Six only if the convoy actually moves and only if you can restore the reserve before the next relief."

"Yes, Majesty."

Outside the office Edrik Saye was checking a merchant credential against a receiving mark while two carters complained about waiting in rain that had not yet begun.

The system was irritating people.

It was also functioning.

Luceran had no intention of dismantling it merely because soldiers looked more impressive on roads than beside doors.

---

By afternoon the question had escaped the castle.

It did not need a spy.

A Damaris factor had asked for oat prices.

A Kaelmont clerk had asked when eight wagons might finish their current loads.

A farrier in the lower village had received an inquiry about replacement shoes.

Two Bellisar messengers had arrived within an hour of each other.

Someone from Harrowmere had bought six new map cases.

At the Black Pine tavern below the castle, three different men had concluded that war was imminent and disagreed only about which war.

Pellin Joss brought Luceran the first written version before supper.

"You have invaded the north."

Luceran took the note.

"Have I won?"

"Apparently you are still assembling."

"Then the report is at least temporally cautious."

Pellin sat on the edge of the table.

"This one came from a cloth factor's clerk. No sign it is coordinated with the capital sheets."

"Do you ever bring me reassuring information?"

"You pay other people for that."

Miren, reading beside Luceran, laughed.

The note claimed three thousand men were gathering at Edselsto.

Luceran looked toward the window, where the lower yard contained twenty-seven soldiers, four masons, a dog, and a cart missing one wheel.

"Three thousand?"

"People prefer armies that justify the story they already bought," Pellin said.

"Correct it?"

Miren asked the question before Luceran could.

Pellin shook his head.

"Correct what? We are asking about readiness. We are discussing troop movement. We are buying stores. If you publish exact numbers, you give strangers a better return than some of our own captains have sent."

Luceran folded the note.

"Then we say nothing."

"We say what is true when asked," Miren said. "The Crown is reviewing readiness. No general summons has been issued."

Pellin looked between them.

"Boring."

"Good," Luceran said.

"Boring facts travel slowly."

"Then perhaps they will arrive after the exciting lies have embarrassed themselves."

"That is not how lies work."

Luceran sighed.

"I know."

---

The hardest disagreement came after dark.

Captain Maelin Harrow wanted one company moved immediately to Vey Crossing.

Jeron Bellisar wanted none.

Both had reasons.

Maelin stood over the map with one hand braced on the table.

"A company at Vey Crossing shortens response north by two days and demonstrates that the Crown can reinforce the corridor."

Jeron said, "It also tells every frightened garrison that the rumors were right to frighten them."

"Some are already delaying leave."

"Which is why adding movement without a threat may produce the threat socially before it exists militarily."

"You cannot deter a border problem with a philosophical objection."

"And you cannot feed deterrence with confidence."

Maelin's eyes narrowed.

Jeron's did not.

Luceran let the silence stand.

Rhyse finally said, "I agree with both of you enough to dislike this."

"Useful," Maelin said.

"The northern approaches are vulnerable to rumor because reinforcement is slow. Moving a full company improves the military problem and worsens the political one. Doing nothing preserves ambiguity and leaves us slow if the reports become real."

Mara moved one of the little paper stacks.

"What about the depot without the company?"

Everyone looked at her.

She shrugged.

"You keep arguing about men. Tomas wants food forward. Kaelmont has wagons finishing loads. Damaris can release bounded stores without emptying anything. Move the things that make later movement possible."

Maelin said, "Stores do not deter anyone."

"Neither do hungry soldiers."

Jeron smiled.

"Quartermasters are vicious creatures."

"We become so from listening to officers discover eating."

Luceran looked down at the map.

There was another possibility.

"A road party."

Ardel's attention sharpened.

"For which road?"

"Northbridge. Inspect the repair. Verify the span. Put replacement timber and iron near it. Not soldiers pretending to be engineers. Actual road people."

Ardel nodded slowly.

"That improves the route whether an army uses it or not."

"And if somebody calls it preparation for invasion?"

Miren asked.

Luceran looked at her.

"They will not be entirely wrong."

That mattered.

He did not want a clever fiction by which every military preparation became innocent because it also repaired a bridge.

The kingdom had begun reacting to the possibility of force. So had he.

The distinction was that he intended to know what he was doing.

"One stores convoy," he said. "Bounded. Flour, oats, salt meat, shoes, wheel grease, repair iron. Enough to sustain an initial movement without stripping villages. Four Crown guards from Bram, local escort thereafter. No company moved to Vey Crossing yet. Kaelmont and Crown road officers inspect Northbridge and stage repair material. Bellisar garrisons return delayed leave to their captains' discretion unless there is an actual local reason to hold it. Harrowmere units remain at ordinary stations but update assembly rolls."

Maelin was not pleased.

"That displays nothing."

"It displays that we can count."

"Our opponents may not be impressed."

"I am not yet certain who my opponents are."

That ended the argument without resolving it.

Maelin bowed.

Not deeply.

Luceran noticed.

He also noticed that she had argued against him without once implying disloyalty.

He would need people like that.

He suspected he would often wish he did not.

---

Later, Miren found him in his rooms with his boots off and a readiness return balanced on one knee.

"Seductive," she said.

"I have learned there are forty-one horses I am forbidden to ride."

"All at once?"

"Jeron was unclear."

She took the paper from him and put it on the floor.

"You have spent twelve hours asking how quickly people can leave you."

The joke landed badly enough that she saw it.

Her expression changed.

Luceran looked toward the fire.

"That is not what I was doing."

"I know."

"It is a little what I was doing."

She sat beside him.

Neither of them mentioned the capital rumor about her governing from his bed.

They had already laughed at it.

Repetition would not make it less invasive.

"You kept the guards," she said.

"Bram kept the guards. I merely failed to overrule him."

"That is becoming one of your better royal powers."

He looked at her.

"Not overruling people?"

"Knowing when someone else's no is doing work for you."

He leaned his head against hers.

"Maelin thinks I am timid."

"No. Maelin thinks deterrence requires visible force. You think visible force may manufacture evidence for people already expecting aggression. Those are different judgments."

"Which of us is right?"

"Ask me after the border crisis."

"There is no border crisis."

"Then you are both going to be insufferable if one appears."

He laughed.

She kissed him once, lightly.

Then she said, "Edevane will hear about the stores convoy before it arrives."

"From you?"

"Not from me. Probably. But if my uncle asks whether you are mobilizing, I will tell him what I know: you are preparing the ability to move."

Luceran considered that.

It was exactly the sort of sentence he would once have wanted her not to send.

Now he understood that asking her to lie to her family about visible Crown action would not create privacy. It would create a lie inside their relationship and leave the action visible anyway.

"Tell him no general summons has been issued."

"That is true."

"Yes."

She watched him for a moment.

"Painfully improving."

"Rhyse will sue you."

"He has not found a court willing to hear him."

---

The stores convoy left Edselsto the next morning.

It was disappointingly small.

Six wagons.

Four Crown guards rode with it as far as the lower road. Two Damaris clerks carried tallies. A Kaelmont roadwright named Sena Orrel rode ahead with three carpenters, a smith, replacement iron, measuring rods, and an opinion about bridges that began before breakfast and had not ended by the gate.

Tomas Reed went with the convoy.

Luceran had assumed he would remain at Edselsto to coordinate the figures.

Tomas had disagreed.

"If the first forward stores are wrong," he said, "I would rather discover that while I can still blame myself accurately."

Mara approved the departure sheet.

Bram approved the four guards and no more.

Rhyse approved the route.

Orel Venn signed the Damaris quantities.

Ardel signed nothing because the Kaelmont carriage terms did not give her personal command of hired wagons, a distinction she made loudly when a clerk brought her the wrong paper.

Luceran watched the convoy pass beneath the gate.

No trumpets sounded.

No banners unfurled.

No army marched.

Below the castle, however, people watched six wagons turn north carrying the things an army would need if an army later followed.

A messenger left the village less than an hour afterward.

Pellin saw him go.

He did not stop him.

There was no lawful reason to stop a man for noticing wagons.

By evening the first report came back from Northbridge.

Not from Tomas.

From Sena Orrel.

The bridge was worse than the old return said.

One abutment had shifted after rain. Loaded wagons could still cross individually if the damaged side was shored, but sustained military traffic risked finishing what the weather had begun.

Her recommendation was blunt: repair before concentration.

Underneath, in another hand, Tomas had added the depot count.

The grain was lower than the paper return by fourteen sacks.

Not stolen, apparently.

Issued during the previous week's leave suspension to men who had remained at post and to two patrols sent out because neighboring garrisons had begun watching one another.

Rumor had already eaten grain.

Luceran carried the note back to the map room.

Rhyse was there with Jeron and Mara.

Jeron read it first.

"There," he said.

Luceran waited.

Jeron tapped the missing fourteen sacks.

"That is your army question."

"Fourteen sacks?"

"No. The fact that nobody ordered an army to move and the possibility of one already consumed supplies."

Mara took the note from him.

"And changed leave. And patrols. And horse use."

Rhyse looked at the bridge report.

"And now the road requires work because we bothered to ask whether it could carry what everyone was afraid we might send."

Luceran stood over the map.

The kingdom had not mobilized.

It had begun preparing to be capable of mobilization.

That distinction was real.

It was also much less visible from a distance than he would have liked.

"Repair the bridge," he said. "Replace the fourteen sacks. Keep the forward stores bounded. No troop concentration without new evidence."

Mara nodded.

Rhyse did not.

He was looking at a fresh message in his hand.

Luceran noticed.

"What?"

Rhyse passed it across the table.

The seal belonged to a northern road captain.

The report was short.

Two riders had arrived from beyond Red Ford claiming that armed men had crossed a disputed grazing boundary and burned a toll shelter.

No names.

No confirmed colors.

No bodies reported.

The nearest local officer had sent a patrol and requested instructions.

Jeron read over Luceran's shoulder.

"Could be nothing."

Maelin Harrow, entering behind them, heard him.

"Could be exactly what we were preparing for."

Luceran looked from one to the other.

On the table lay the bridge report, the missing grain, the readiness returns, and a map full of roads that could carry soldiers only if ordinary people first made them capable of doing so.

Outside, Edselsto remained guarded.

The seven candles remained lit.

Six wagons of military stores were already on the northern road.

And now, for the first time, there was a report that might give those preparations somewhere to go.
