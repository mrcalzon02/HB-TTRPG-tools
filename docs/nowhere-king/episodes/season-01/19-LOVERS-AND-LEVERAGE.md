# Season One, Episode 19 — Lovers and Leverage

Miren Edevane discovered she was expected to marry the king when a seamstress asked whether the winter blue was intended for the announcement.

The question came with pins in the seamstress's mouth, which made it difficult to tell whether she understood that she had just altered the morning.

Miren stood on a low fitting stool in her shift while two assistants worked at the hem of a court gown. Rain worried the narrow windows. Somewhere below, hammers struck stone around the Hall of Veils drainage trench.

"What announcement?" Miren asked.

The seamstress froze.

Her name was Tessa Vale—not related to Sera, as she had explained to everyone at least twice—and she had been dressing noble women long enough to recognize danger before it acquired a title.

"No announcement, my lady."

"You just asked whether my dress was for one."

"I asked whether the blue was intended for an announcement."

"That is an impressive distinction."

One assistant stared fiercely at the floor.

The other developed an urgent interest in thread.

Miren stepped down from the stool.

"Tessa. Who told you?"

"Nobody told me anything."

That, Miren believed.

It was not reassuring.

"Then why did you ask?"

Tessa removed the pins from her mouth and placed them carefully on a tray.

"Because Lady Perra Maret's woman asked yesterday whether Edevane blue had precedence over royal mourning colors for a betrothal feast. Because two cloth factors have started holding pale blue silk instead of selling it. Because somebody from the kitchens asked whether almond paste keeps for six weeks. And because Canon Merel's clerk asked my nephew whether the family nave had ever hosted a royal marriage."

Miren closed her eyes.

Nobody had told anyone anything.

The court had built a wedding from procurement.

"There is no betrothal," she said.

Tessa nodded immediately.

The assistants nodded with her.

Miren looked at all three of them.

"And now you know that because I told you. Which means by supper half the lower household will know I denied it during a fitting."

Tessa had the grace to look pained.

"Probably before supper."

Miren laughed despite herself.

"Finish the hem. It is a winter gown. Nothing more."

She stepped back onto the stool.

The pins returned.

After a moment, Tessa said, "For what it is worth, my lady, I thought the almond paste was excessive."

"Thank you. That restores my confidence in the realm."

---

Ardel Kaelmont heard the same rumor from a man trying to sell her road rights.

He was a minor factor named Ossin Brey, and his error was not believing the rumor. His error was assuming Ardel would be frightened by it.

"Naturally," he said, "if House Edevane is about to acquire a more permanent position near the Crown, Kaelmont may wish to secure its carriage privileges before the household settles into a different arrangement."

Ardel sat across from him in a half-repaired solar that Kaelmont used as an office. Three maps lay open between them. One showed the north road. One showed quarry access. The third showed where a spring had eaten half a retaining wall and did not care whom Luceran might marry.

"Our carriage opportunity is already written," she said.

"Three years. Nonexclusive."

"Yes."

"A marriage changes influence."

"Marriage changes many things. It does not change ink retroactively."

Brey smiled as though she were being charming.

Ardel disliked him immediately.

"You have not heard?"

"I have heard seven versions. In two of them I am apparently devastated. In one I have already left Edselsto."

"And have you?"

Ardel looked around the room.

"You are sitting in my office."

He laughed too loudly.

She let him.

Then she slid the retaining-wall map toward him.

"You came here because you want the lower-road hauling subcontract. Tell me what you can put on that road before first frost and what happens to your price if the east culvert fails."

Brey's smile thinned.

That was better.

He knew carts. He knew axle loads, mule teams and what wet stone did to a grade. Once forced to discuss the thing he actually understood, he became useful.

Twenty minutes later Ardel had learned that his proposed price assumed use of Vess water for two temporary animal stations.

"You don't have that right," she said.

"The Crown is using the spring."

"The Crown is negotiating use of the spring. That is not the same sentence."

"It would be easier if Kaelmont held the carriage road outright."

"Many thefts become easier if one first calls them roads."

He stopped smiling.

Ardel marked his proposal for revision and sent him away.

Only after the door closed did she permit herself to think about Miren.

Not the rumor. Miren.

Miren was intelligent, warm when she chose to be, and sufficiently secure in Luceran's affection to disagree with him in public rooms where lesser courtiers performed agreement. Ardel respected that. She also wanted Luceran, which made respect inconvenient without making it false.

She had no intention of becoming stupid because two things hurt at once.

Her aunt's letter lay unopened beside the maps.

Ardel knew what it would say anyway.

If the king was marrying Edevane, Kaelmont must secure what it could before the announcement.

If he was not, Ardel should discover whether he might marry Kaelmont instead.

Families could turn uncertainty into instructions faster than rain turned roads into mud.

She opened the letter.

She had been exactly right.

---

Luceran learned of his engagement at luncheon.

Montegrain arrived late, sat down, stole a piece of roast onion from Luceran's plate and said, "Congratulations."

Luceran looked at him.

"On what?"

"Your impending marriage."

Miren, seated two places away, covered her mouth with her cup.

"To whom?"

Montegrain considered. "That appears to depend on which corridor you use."

Luceran put down his knife.

"I am not marrying anyone."

"Excellent. I had not bought a gift."

"Where did this start?"

Miren said, "That is the wrong question."

He looked at her.

She had the expression she wore when she had already been irritated for several hours and had decided not to waste the irritation.

"The useful question," she continued, "is what people have done because they believe it."

Montegrain pointed at her with the stolen onion.

"That is why you should marry her."

Luceran threw a napkin at him.

The conversation might have remained funny if Tessa Rane had not entered with three notes.

One was from the kitchens asking whether proposed winter feast purchases should be held.

One was from the Keeper of Wardrobes asking whether any household colors were changing.

The third was from Avarren, reporting that two petitioners had asked whether marriage negotiations affected access to the king's private audience hours.

Luceran read them twice.

"Nobody has announced anything."

"No," Miren said. "People observed things. Then they observed other people observing things."

"Calienne?"

Miren's voice sharpened. "Do not do that."

He stopped.

"She saw us holding hands yesterday."

"Yes. She also looked away. You have no evidence she told anyone."

Luceran felt the correction land.

"You're right."

"I know."

Montegrain ate the onion.

Luceran glared at him.

"I am preserving the intimacy of the moment."

Tessa Rane said, "Majesty, the rumor did not need Sister Calienne. You sit beside Lady Miren. She enters your private rooms. Your household knows when breakfast is sent for two. You touch each other when you think fewer people are looking than are actually looking."

Miren's ears reddened.

Tessa continued without mercy. "And House Edevane is royal blood. A court does not need a spy to count chairs."

There was no accusation in it. That made it harder to dismiss.

Luceran looked at Miren.

"We should talk privately."

Montegrain stood.

"I have never obeyed a royal command with greater enthusiasm."

"I did not command you."

"Then I have preserved my independence as well."

He left with Tessa.

Miren waited until the door shut.

"Do you want to marry me?"

Luceran had imagined that question before.

In none of those imaginings had there been roast onions on the table.

"Yes," he said.

Miren went very still.

He continued before courage could become ceremony.

"Not tomorrow. Not because the court invented it. Not because Edevane is convenient. But yes. I can imagine wanting that."

Her expression changed slowly.

"That is not a proposal."

"No."

"Good."

He laughed once, startled.

"Good?"

"If you proposed because three cloth factors hoarded blue silk, I would hit you with the wine jug."

"It is silver."

"Then you would remember the lesson longer."

He reached for her hand. She let him take it.

"Do you?" he asked.

"Want to marry you?"

"Yes."

Miren looked down at their hands.

"I want the possibility to be ours before it becomes theirs."

Luceran said nothing.

"I love you," she continued. "I like waking beside you. I like arguing with you. I like that you have finally learned to ask people what they know instead of what you fear they know. I also know that marrying you would make every cousin I have more important overnight. Every favor I ask would be counted differently. Every person I dislike would decide it was policy. Every person you sleep with—or are rumored to sleep with—would become a question about me."

He looked at her.

"Every person I what?"

"Do not become twelve."

"I am not planning an itinerary."

"Neither was your grandfather, according to my aunt."

He laughed, then saw that she was smiling too.

The humor did not erase the question.

"Ardel," he said.

Miren's thumb moved once against his hand.

"I am jealous of Ardel."

It was the first time she had said it without disguising it as political caution.

Luceran waited.

"That does not mean she is false," Miren said. "It does not mean Kaelmont's engineering is bad. It does not mean I get to turn jealousy into state judgment. But I am jealous. I would rather tell you that than become clever enough to hide it from both of us."

"I am attracted to her."

"I know."

"You know I have not—"

"I know what you have told me. And I believe you. Those are not identical things, but they are close enough for love."

That hurt and comforted him simultaneously.

"What do we do?"

Miren leaned back.

"We make one promise. A private one. Not a betrothal."

"Which is?"

"Neither of us will allow the court to announce our future by behaving as though its inference is our consent. If we decide to marry, we decide it together first. If either of us changes what we think this relationship permits, we tell the other before the court gets to explain it for us."

Luceran considered the shape of it.

It was not exclusivity by implication. It was not marriage by rumor. It was an obligation to speak before ambiguity became policy.

"Yes," he said.

Miren squeezed his hand.

"Yes what?"

"Yes, I promise."

"So do I."

Then she kissed him across the remains of luncheon.

It was not a royal act.

It would nevertheless have royal consequences.

---

By midafternoon, Ardel had received a request from her aunt to seek private audience.

She refused it.

Not the audience. The purpose.

Her reply was four lines:

The carriage agreement stands on its written terms. No royal betrothal has been announced. I will not convert rumor into a demand for marriage or additional privilege. Send the revised culvert estimates.

She sanded the letter herself.

Then she went to see Miren.

That was harder.

Miren received her in a small west sitting room with no servants inside and one guard beyond the door. For several seconds neither woman seemed interested in pretending the meeting was casual.

Ardel decided to spare them both.

"My family wants me to find out whether you are marrying him."

Miren blinked.

Then she laughed.

Ardel had not expected that.

"Mine probably does too," Miren said. "They have simply had the good manners not to ask me yet."

"Will you tell me?"

"There is no betrothal."

Ardel absorbed the answer.

Miren watched her do it.

"You are relieved."

"Yes."

There was no advantage in lying.

Miren nodded.

"I dislike how much I respect you for saying that."

"I dislike several things about you."

"Good. I was worried this would become sentimental."

Ardel sat opposite her.

"I am attracted to him."

"I know."

"Does everyone know everything in this castle?"

"No. They merely know enough to be dangerous."

Ardel smiled despite herself.

Then Miren said, "I am jealous of you."

That erased the smile.

"I did not come to ask permission."

"I know. I would think less of you if you had."

"And I am not offering to stop wanting him because that would be a ridiculous promise."

"Also good."

They sat with the discomfort.

Outside, a cart rattled over wet stones.

Ardel said, "My aunt believes your marriage would close Kaelmont's access."

"Would it?"

"Not by the written bargain. Politically? Perhaps. Your cousins would be fools not to use proximity."

"Some of them are fools. Unfortunately not all."

"That is reassuring."

Miren's expression softened.

"If Luceran and I marry someday, I will not promise that Edevane gains nothing. That would be another kind of lie. But I will not treat affection as proof that everyone attached to me deserves the kingdom."

Ardel thought of the three-year carriage agreement.

"He is learning that lesson."

"Slowly."

"He is a king. Slow learning is traditional."

Miren laughed.

The meeting did not make them friends.

It did something more useful.

Each left with a more accurate model of the other.

---

The person who turned the rumor into leverage was not Calienne, Merel, Ardel, Miren or any great house.

It was Pellin Joss.

Pellin had spent enough years around accounts to know that belief moved money before truth caught up.

He arrived at Nera's temporary stores table with two invoices and an expression of disgust.

"Look."

Nera looked.

Both invoices were for preserved fruit and fine flour. Both had been revised upward since the previous week.

"Wedding premium," Pellin said.

"There is no wedding."

"Tell the merchants."

"I intend to tell them something less polite."

The suppliers had not invented a royal marriage. They had merely noticed demand, anticipated feast purchases, and raised prices while buyers were nervous.

Nera refused both invoices.

Pellin sent copies to Tessa Rane.

By evening, the Crown had a small, embarrassing fiscal consequence attached to a romance rumor that nobody had officially spread.

Luceran read the invoices in his study.

"I hate this place."

Miren, sitting barefoot in a chair near the fire, looked up from a letter.

"No, you don't."

"I hate people."

"Also untrue."

"I hate preserved fruit."

"That I believe."

He tossed the papers onto the desk.

"If I publicly deny a betrothal, I humiliate you."

"Potentially."

"If I say nothing, people keep pricing a wedding."

"Probably not for long. Markets dislike buying silk that nobody needs."

"Avarren suggests a household notice saying no change in precedence, livery, feast procurement or marriage status has been proclaimed."

"That sounds painfully like Avarren."

"It also sounds correct."

"Then issue it."

Luceran looked at her.

"You don't mind?"

"It denies an announcement, not us."

He understood the distinction.

The notice went out before supper.

It did not say Luceran would never marry Miren.

It did not say he might.

It said what the Crown actually knew: no betrothal had been proclaimed; no household precedence had changed; no feast procurement had been authorized; existing contracts remained existing contracts.

By midnight, the price of almond paste had already begun falling.

---

Canon Merel read the notice and smiled for reasons that had nothing to do with romance.

A Crown forced to deny what it had not announced was a Crown learning that public meaning did not wait for permission.

He considered that useful.

Sister Calienne, when he mentioned it, stared at him.

"You think this proves your candle argument."

"I think it proves people assign meaning."

"To lovers and candles alike?"

"To anything visible."

Calienne folded the notice.

"For once, Canon, you may have found an argument I cannot immediately kill."

"I shall mark the date."

"Do. But if you claim the king's love life is evidence of divine continuity, I will kill the argument with a chair."

Merel laughed.

He was still angry about the archive door.

Calienne still thought he had deserved it.

Neither fact prevented them from recognizing when the other was right.

---

Late that night, Luceran and Miren lay together while rain tapped the repaired glass.

The castle around them was not quiet. It was becoming too inhabited for quiet: guards changed below, someone rolled a barrel through a distant passage, a door slammed, and a drunk worker sang half a verse before another voice told him to shut up.

Luceran preferred it to the silence of his first night.

Miren rested her head against his shoulder.

"Do you regret saying yes?" she asked.

"To what?"

"The possibility."

He thought before answering.

"No. I regret that it immediately became grain for everyone else's mill."

"That part will not improve."

"Comforting."

"You are welcome."

He touched her hair.

"Do you regret it?"

"No."

A pause.

"I still dislike that you want Ardel."

"I know."

"I may dislike it tomorrow too."

"That seems likely."

"Do not become smug."

"I am terrified."

She pinched him.

Then her hand settled against his chest.

"Our promise stands," she said.

"It does."

Neither of them knew what shape their relationship would eventually take.

That was now an uncertainty they had chosen to keep honest rather than allow the court to solve for them.

Elsewhere in Edselsto, House Edevane was calculating, House Kaelmont was calculating, merchants were repricing flour, priests were discovering analogies, servants were correcting one another's gossip, and clerks were filing a notice denying an announcement nobody had made.

Love had not become policy.

But it had acquired paperwork.
