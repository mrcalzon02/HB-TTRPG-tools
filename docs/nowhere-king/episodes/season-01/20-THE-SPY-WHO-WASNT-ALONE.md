# Season One, Episode 20 — The Spy Who Wasn't Alone

The first proof that someone had been studying the new guard was a bill for lamp oil.

Nera found it because the quantity was wrong.

Not enormously wrong. Enormous errors were easy. They acquired shouting, seals, and eventually a nobleman asking why his money had vanished.

This was three extra jars charged to the west service stores over six nights.

Nera sat at her temporary table beside the lower storehouse door, put the bill beside the issue tally, and counted again.

Three jars purchased.

None issued to Sera Vale's night watch.

None missing from the shelf.

Which meant somebody had paid for oil that had never reached the Crown.

Pellin Joss leaned over her shoulder.

"Merchant mistake?"

"Then he made it three times."

"The same merchant?"

"Same factor. Different carter marks."

Pellin stopped leaning.

Nera tapped the first entry.

"And look at the delivery hours."

All three had supposedly arrived during west-service relief.

The first at the second bell after midnight.

The second six nights later, shortly before the same bell.

The third the previous night.

Pellin said, "That is a very specific time to make the same small mistake."

"Yes."

"Security?"

"Accounts first. If I hand Sera a spy every time arithmetic annoys me, she will eventually throw me down the stairs."

"She would document it."

"That is why I respect her."

They took the invoices to the factor.

---

The factor's name was Ren Vask, and he was insulted before he was frightened.

His booth stood in the lower village between a cooper's shed and a roofless house being used to stack lime barrels. Rain had turned the lane to brown paste. Two apprentices were arguing over a mule that had decided royal restoration was somebody else's problem.

Ren examined the copies.

"Those are mine."

"You supplied the oil?" Nera asked.

"I supplied a charge."

Pellin said, "That sentence has improved my morning."

Ren ignored him.

"A man paid cash for three jars on three nights and asked that they be entered against west-service stores. Said he was settling small watch purchases that had been missed at the gate."

Nera's expression did not change.

"Name?"

"Harl."

"Family name?"

"He didn't give one."

"Whose authority?"

"He had a west-service token."

Pellin and Nera looked at each other.

Episode Seventeen had made tokens legible precisely so that people would stop accepting vague statements about belonging.

Nera asked, "What kind?"

Ren described a grey lead disk with a punched notch and black cord.

It was an old works-yard marker.

It had not been valid for guard access since Sera's reorganization.

"Did he take the oil?" Pellin asked.

"No."

"Then what did he buy?"

Ren frowned.

"The entry."

The rain sounded louder against the awning.

Nera understood first.

The jars had never mattered.

Someone had been buying timestamps.

Three false deliveries, all recorded during relief, gave anyone reading the store accounts a cheap way to confirm when west service changed hands.

Pellin swore softly.

Ren looked from one to the other.

"Am I in trouble?"

"Probably," Nera said. "But not for lamp oil."

---

Sera Vale did not throw Nera down any stairs.

She did, however, become extremely still.

Rhyse read the three entries twice. Anja Pell stood beside the door with the old works token Ren kept as an example, now sealed in a cloth pouch.

Luceran wanted the gates closed.

He knew he wanted it before anyone said anything, and Episode Sixteen had taught him to distrust that feeling enough to keep his mouth shut for another ten seconds.

Rhyse noticed.

"Majesty?"

"My first thought was to close the gates."

Sera said, "That would tell whoever did this that we found the test."

"Yes."

"And trap several hundred workers inside who have nothing to do with it."

"Yes."

Anja added, "And if the person already left, we inconvenience ourselves for them."

Luceran looked at Rhyse.

"I am improving."

"Painfully."

Miren, who had been reading the invoices near the window, said, "You should embroider that on the royal standard."

Luceran ignored both of them.

"What do we actually know?"

Nera answered because the evidence was hers.

"Someone paid Ren Vask three times to create false oil deliveries against west-service stores. They used an obsolete works token. The recorded times fall during the relief interval. We do not know whether the purpose was to confirm the relief time, to make us think somebody was confirming it, or something else."

"Do we know Harl exists?"

"Ren says he does."

"Description?"

"Ordinary enough to be useless. Brown coat. Beard. Missing the end of one little finger."

Sera's eyes moved to Anja.

Anja shook her head.

"Not one of mine that I know."

Luceran said, "The man who asked which watch held west service after midnight?"

Sera answered immediately. "Possibly the same person. Not established."

"The rope-carrying questioner?"

"Also possibly. Also not established."

"The hunt?"

Rhyse said, "No evidence connects it."

The refusal was almost comforting now.

Luceran looked again at the invoices.

"Then find the money."

Pellin smiled without humor.

"That I can do."

---

The money did not lead to a mastermind.

It led to a laundress.

Her name was Mera Doss, forty-six, widowed, with a daughter apprenticed to a dyer in Aster Vale and a left wrist that hurt when the weather changed. She worked three days in the castle laundry and two in a private washhouse below the village because the Crown did not yet have enough heated space for the volume of linen Edselsto generated.

Pellin found her because Ren Vask remembered that Harl had paid with clipped river crowns wrapped in blue paper. The paper bore a dyer's tally mark. There were six dyers supplying the growing settlement. Five used red or plain wrapping.

The sixth employed Mera's daughter.

That was not proof.

So Pellin did not send guards.

He asked Nera who collected small private remittances for workers whose families lived elsewhere.

The answer was a copyist in the archive office.

Selise Varo.

Luceran stared at the name when Pellin brought it to him.

"Of course."

Miren looked up.

"That is not evidence either."

"I know. I am merely offended by narrative symmetry."

"You are becoming unbearable."

"Becoming?"

They found Selise in the archive with Ysabet Marris, copying a water-rights agreement that was older than three of the houses currently arguing about it.

Selise listened to Pellin's question and went pale.

"Mera sends money through me because her daughter works near my aunt."

Ysabet put down her pen.

"How much?"

"Usually two crowns. Sometimes three."

"How often?"

"Every second week."

Pellin asked, "And does money ever come back?"

Selise hesitated.

That hesitation changed the room.

Luceran felt Rhyse shift behind him.

Selise saw it too.

"Yes."

"From whom?"

"My aunt."

"For Mera?"

"Sometimes."

"How much?"

"Five crowns once. Eight last month. Ten three days ago."

Miren said, "Why?"

Selise looked miserable.

"She said Mera's daughter was doing well and the family that sponsors the dyehouse wanted to help her mother."

Pellin asked, "Which family?"

Selise closed her eyes.

"Valcere."

Nobody spoke for several seconds.

House Valcere's reputation for correspondence and discreet investigators was old enough to be a joke at court.

That did not make every Valcere coin espionage.

Luceran forced himself to ask the next question carefully.

"Selise, have you been sending information to your aunt?"

She opened her eyes.

"Yes."

Ysabet's face changed.

Not anger first.

Hurt.

Selise saw that too.

"Not archive documents. Never sealed material."

Ysabet said, "That was not the question."

"I know."

"Then answer it."

Selise swallowed.

"Every week. A letter. Who has arrived. Which offices are hiring. Which roads are passable. Which houses are taking rooms. Whether the kitchens are short. Whether the village is growing. Things people can mostly see."

"Mostly?" Rhyse asked.

Selise looked at him.

"Sometimes which archive room was busy. Sometimes whether the king had asked for older family records. Never what the records said."

Luceran felt cold despite the fire.

Episode Ten returned to him with embarrassing clarity.

Would you deliberately report archive business?

No.

At the time she had meant it.

"When did that change?" he asked.

Selise looked at him.

That was the question that broke her composure.

"After my mother got sick."

No one interrupted.

"My aunt sent money. Then her husband's office asked whether I could include a little more detail because everyone in Aster Vale was trying to understand whether Edselsto was becoming a real court or a temporary royal tantrum. They paid for the paper and postage. Then they paid me. Not much."

"How much?"

"Four crowns a letter."

Pellin's mouth tightened.

Not enough to become rich.

Enough to buy medicine.

Luceran asked, "Did you know the letters went beyond your aunt?"

"Yes."

"To Valcere?"

"Eventually."

"When?"

"Six weeks ago."

Ysabet turned away.

Selise said, "I should have told you."

"Yes," Ysabet said.

"I thought—"

"I know what you thought. You thought because you were not copying sealed pages, you were keeping the promise you made yourself."

Selise's eyes filled.

Ysabet remained turned away.

"And because most of what you wrote could be learned elsewhere, you decided the part that could not was too small to count."

"Yes."

Luceran understood then that Ysabet knew her very well.

That made the betrayal smaller than treason and larger than paperwork.

"Do you care what happens here?" he asked.

Selise looked almost angry.

"Of course I do."

"That is not obvious from where I am standing."

"I know."

"Do you care what happens to me?"

She stared at him.

"Yes."

"And you reported on me."

"Yes."

The answer hurt more because she did not try to escape it.

Miren said quietly, "Those things can both be true."

Luceran looked at her.

She held his gaze.

Edevane blood. Edevane cousins. A woman who loved him and would still tell her family things because family did not vanish when affection began.

He hated the lesson because he had already learned it and apparently needed to learn it again.

Rhyse said, "We need the letters."

Selise nodded.

"I kept copies."

Pellin blinked.

"Why?"

She looked offended despite everything.

"I am a copyist."

---

The copies saved her from being mistaken for something worse.

They also made the actual network visible.

Selise's weekly letters went to Aunt Leth Varo in Aster Vale. Leth passed them to her husband, Corin, an Edevane household clerk. Corin bundled observations from several relatives and correspondents and sold a digest to a Valcere broker named Deren Holt.

Holt did not rely on Selise alone.

That was the point.

He paid a stable boy for departures.

A kitchen supplier for changes in meal counts.

Mera Doss for laundry volume, guard cloaks, mud, blood, and unusual late washing.

A carter for which roads received royal stone and timber.

A dismissed Companion's cousin for complaints about the new credential system.

And, through a chain of two small factors, Ren Vask's mysterious Harl for three false lamp-oil entries testing whether west-service relief happened when the new schedules suggested it did.

No one source knew the whole collection.

Most did not think of themselves as spies.

Mera thought she was answering harmless questions about work in exchange for money that helped her daughter.

The stable boy thought rich people were idiots for paying him to say which horses had left a public yard.

The kitchen supplier believed meal counts were commerce.

The dismissed Companion's cousin believed he was documenting an insult to his family.

Corin Edevane believed he was selling political intelligence that every serious house purchased somewhere.

Deren Holt believed he was doing his profession.

Only Harl, if that was his name, appeared to have crossed from observation into active testing of security.

And even there, the Crown did not yet know who had instructed him.

Sera spread the reconstructed network across a table with pieces of string.

Luceran hated the string immediately.

"This makes it look as though everyone is connected."

"Everyone is connected," Pellin said.

"Not in the same way."

"Exactly."

So they removed the string.

They used columns instead.

Observed.

Paid.

Passed onward.

Tasked.

Unknown.

It was less dramatic and more useful.

Rhyse pointed at the west-service entries.

"This is the only confirmed active test in the network we have reconstructed."

Sera nodded.

"The rest is collection. Some of it intrusive. Some of it ordinary."

"And the hunt?" Luceran asked.

"Still separate."

"Episode Sixteen's note?"

"Separate unless evidence changes that."

"The man asking about west service?"

Sera considered.

"That one may belong here. Description is too weak to confirm."

Luceran looked at the columns.

"So we have exposed a spy network and learned almost nothing about the person trying to kill me."

Rhyse said, "We have not established that anyone is trying to kill you."

Luceran gave him a long look.

"I walked into that."

"You did."

Miren hid a smile.

---

Mera Doss did not deny anything.

She sat in the guard office with her bad wrist wrapped in wool and answered every question Sera asked.

"Did you report blood on guard clothing?"

"Yes."

"Whose?"

"I didn't know. I said three cloaks came in bloodied after the hunt."

"Did you report relief times?"

"No. I don't know relief times."

"Did you report which guards worked west service?"

"I said I washed six west-service cloaks one week and nine the next."

Sera glanced at Rhyse.

That was enough to reveal staffing growth without names.

"Why?"

Mera's jaw tightened.

"Because they paid me."

"Why did you need the money?"

"Because my daughter wants her own dye vat before she is thirty and I would prefer she not marry one."

Sera almost smiled.

"Did you know who ultimately received your reports?"

"Valcere people."

"Did you know they were studying royal security?"

"I knew they study everybody."

"That was not my question."

Mera looked at her.

"No."

Sera believed her enough not to pretend belief was proof.

Luceran watched from the side of the room.

Mera noticed.

"Are you going to hang me?"

"No."

She looked surprised.

"Why not?"

"Because selling laundry observations is not treason."

Rhyse added, "Using privileged access to defeat guard security may be a crime. We are still determining who did that."

Mera looked back at Luceran.

"Then am I fired?"

That was harder.

Ysabet had asked the same question about Selise without asking it aloud.

Luceran said, "You cannot keep unrestricted access to guard laundry while taking money to report what you see there."

Mera nodded once.

"Fair."

"You may continue private washhouse work. Crown laundry will decide whether there is work for you outside restricted loads."

"My daughter—"

"Keeps the money already sent to her unless it is proven payment for a crime."

Mera's shoulders dropped.

Relief was not gratitude.

Luceran did not ask for gratitude.

---

Selise's consequence was worse because Ysabet mattered to her.

The archive chief removed her from family papers, security records, current correspondence, and all uncatalogued material pending review. Selise could continue copying public petitions, old tax rolls, and duplicate inventories under supervision.

"You are not dismissing me?" Selise asked.

Ysabet's face was tired.

"I have not decided whether I can trust you with private paper again. That is different from deciding you are useless."

Selise nodded.

"I am sorry."

"I know."

"Does that matter?"

"Yes."

Selise looked up.

Ysabet continued, "It does not restore the trust."

That hurt more honestly than punishment would have.

Luceran, hearing about it later, thought of Miren's promise from the previous episode.

Tell each other before the court explains it for you.

Selise had failed a similar test in another form. She had let money and family redefine what she believed she was permitted to share without telling the people whose trust made the information valuable.

The problem was not that she had an aunt.

The problem was that she had quietly changed the boundary.

---

Deren Holt was gone before the Crown reached his rented room.

That was the first thing about the affair that felt like professional opposition rather than ordinary human leakage.

He had left no dramatic cipher, poisoned cup, hidden blade, or list of conspirators.

He had paid his rent.

He had taken his clothes.

He had burned the scraps in his grate thoroughly enough that only black flakes remained.

And he had departed through the lower road during daylight on a hired mule under his own name.

Anja Pell found the stable receipt.

"He left yesterday at noon."

Sera read it.

"Before Nera found the oil entries."

"Yes."

"So either he was warned, or he had already finished."

"Or he had somewhere else to be."

Sera looked at her.

Anja shrugged.

"We are trying not to turn possibilities into facts."

Sera handed the receipt back.

"Annoying habit. Keep it."

Holt's room did yield one useful thing.

Not hidden.

Not secret.

A discarded draft of a letter to a Valcere factor requesting payment for "Edselsto household and access observations, fourth packet," with a separate line item for "verification of west-service change interval."

Verification.

That word mattered.

It tied the false oil entries to Holt's operation.

It did not tie Holt to the hunt.

It did not identify Harl.

It did not prove Valcere's house leadership had ordered an attack, a coup, or anything beyond intelligence collection.

But it proved someone in the Valcere network had paid to test the guard schedule rather than merely observe it.

That was enough for a warrant, questions, and a political problem.

Not enough for war.

---

That evening Miren found Luceran in the half-restored gallery overlooking the lower village.

Below them, lamps moved between workshops. The settlement had become too large to go dark when the castle did. Hammering continued somewhere beyond the wall. A mule objected loudly to existence.

Luceran held Selise's first Episode Ten receiving papers in one hand and the copied Valcere letter in the other.

"I asked her if she would deliberately report archive business."

"I remember."

"She said no."

"She meant it then."

"That does not make it better."

"No."

He looked at her.

"You are very irritating when you agree with me correctly."

"I practice."

He leaned against the cold stone.

"I wanted spies to be people who hated me."

Miren came beside him.

"Some probably are."

"That would be easier."

"Yes."

"Selise likes me."

"Probably."

"Mera seems to think I am an idiot but not a monster."

"A promising constituency."

He laughed despite himself.

Then he looked at her.

"And you love me."

Miren did not flinch from where the thought was going.

"Yes."

"And if Edevane asks you what happened here?"

"I will tell them some of it."

The answer landed exactly where it should.

"What part?"

"That a Valcere collection network was exposed. That you did not arrest half the laundry because you were frightened. That House Edevane has an embarrassing clerk problem because Corin Varo sold digests. That I expect my family to deal with him before I have to become unpleasant."

"Would you tell them the west-service verification method?"

"No."

"Why?"

"Because that belongs to active Crown security, and because I know the boundary."

"And if the boundary changes?"

Miren looked at him.

"I tell you."

Their promise had acquired another meaning in less than a day.

Luceran took her hand.

"I hate that love does not simplify anything."

"It would be a very poor political system if it did."

"I was hoping for a private life."

"You moved a monarchy into a mountain."

"That sounded more private at the time."

She rested her head briefly against his shoulder.

Below, people continued carrying wood, washing linen, arguing over carts, sending letters, falling in love, selling information, and building a capital around them.

None of them belonged entirely to the king.

That was the danger.

It was also, Luceran was beginning to understand, the only kind of loyalty worth having.

---

The formal response went out the next morning.

House Valcere received a sealed demand to identify Deren Holt's commissioning authority and account for payment made for active verification of royal guard relief. The demand accused no one of treason. It did not mention the hunt. It did not mention the sealed stair. It did not claim the west-service questioner and Harl were the same man.

House Edevane received notice that Corin Varo had sold compiled Edselsto observations onward and that the Crown expected an answer about whether his office had authorized it.

The archive issued new disclosure rules for staff with paid outside correspondence, but did not forbid family letters.

Sera changed west-service relief without announcing the new interval outside the guard command and deliberately left the obsolete visible schedule in place long enough to see who asked about it.

Nera changed the stores rule: no retroactive charge against a Crown office without the receiving officer's mark.

Pellin opened a ledger for small intelligence payments because large conspiracies, he observed, appeared to be built from remarkably cheap people.

Mera returned to the private washhouse.

Selise copied tax rolls under Ysabet's supervision and endured the much harder punishment of being trusted less by someone she admired.

Deren Holt remained absent.

Harl remained unidentified.

The hunt remained unresolved.

The unsigned west-cut note remained unresolved.

The rope-carrying questioner remained unresolved.

And in the old capital, before Edselsto's sealed demands could possibly arrive, a pamphlet was already being copied describing the mountain court as a frightened king hiding behind locked doors, private favorites, foreign road interests, disputed ancestral rites, and guards who demanded papers from honest nobles.

Some of it was false.

Almost none of it had been invented from nothing.

The old capital had begun telling a story about Edselsto.

And unlike a spy, a story could not be arrested.
