# Season One, Episode 12 — The King's Table

**Status:** Committed episode prose  
**Season:** One — The Return to Edselsto  
**Episode:** 12 of 32  
**Reveal ceiling:** Seven candles remain lit. Edselsto remains strange but unconfirmed as an acting intelligence. The hunt attack is treated as human political/security action. No containment truth, sentient-castle proof, or buried-entity explanation.  
**Primary dramatic purpose:** Turn the aftermath of the hunt into ordinary court life: injury, food, seating, flirtation, rumor, provincial news, and a road concession. Let Luceran discover that a table can govern as effectively as an audience chamber while a competent opponent begins retaliating through prices and carriage rather than melodrama.

---

Jeron Bellisar attended dinner with his arm tied to his chest and strict instructions not to enjoy himself.

He failed before the soup.

"The physician said no wine," Lady Miren Edevane told him.

"The physician said very little wine."

"He said none."

"That is a very little amount."

Master Tal, seated two places away, said, "He also said not to ride."

Jeron lifted his good hand. "I am sitting."

"You tried to mount this afternoon."

"I wanted to know whether I could."

"You cannot."

"I know that now."

Luceran laughed despite himself.

That was useful. The room had been waiting to learn whether it was permitted.

The hunt had ended with a rope stretched across a forest track, a dislocated shoulder, bruised ribs, one terrified horse, three hours of searching, and no attacker. By sunset it had also produced six contradictory rumors, two accusations against House Kaelmont, one against House Bellisar, one against the village, and an astonishing theory involving Lord Moncler's roofers.

Rhyse had forbidden discussion of the evidence outside the inquiry.

This had ensured that everyone discussed the evidence without calling it evidence.

So Luceran had refused to cancel dinner.

Not because nothing had happened.

Because something had.

If the court learned that one cord in the woods could empty his table, whoever had tied it would have accomplished more than injuring Jeron.

The King's Table was held in the old west dining chamber because the great hall still smelled of wet timber. It was long enough for forty if everyone liked one another and thirty if they did not. Tonight there were thirty-four.

The kitchens had objected.

The king had overruled them.

The kitchens had then won by serving smaller first portions.

---

Seating began the argument before food did.

Bellisar expected Jeron near Luceran because he had been injured while following the king.

Rhyse wanted him near the door because the physician might need to remove him.

Miren wanted him where his sling would not collide with a server.

Avarren wanted precedence preserved because changing it after an attack would be interpreted as a declaration.

Luceran wanted dinner.

"Put him there," he said, pointing to the place opposite Montegrain.

Avarren looked at the chair.

"That places him above Lady Ardel."

Ardel Kaelmont said, "I will survive."

Jeron said, "I may not."

"You fell off a horse."

"I was removed by hostile architecture."

"It was a rope."

"An architectural rope."

Montegrain leaned toward Luceran. "Promote him."

"For falling?"

"For vocabulary under injury."

Avarren closed his seating book.

"Fine."

That single word cost him more than any of them appreciated.

They sat.

---

The first course was onion broth thickened with bread because eggs had become absurd.

Luceran noticed.

So did Tessa Rane, who had been invited after the village council's work on water, lodging, and the funeral grade became impossible to pretend was merely local business.

She sat below three nobles and above a customs officer who had objected until Avarren asked whether he wanted the king to hear that he had delayed the person responsible for half the lower settlement's water arrangements.

He had discovered humility.

"No eggs?" Luceran asked.

Master Cook Havel, passing behind the service line because he trusted nobody else with the first formal dinner after an attempted attack, heard him.

"There are eggs."

"Where?"

"Tomorrow's breakfast."

"I see."

"You will if nobody buys them from under me before dawn."

Several guests laughed.

Tessa did not.

Luceran looked at her.

"Still rising?"

"Eggs?"

"Everything."

"Rooms have steadied. Bread hasn't. Firewood is up. Oats are worse."

Bellisar's Master Tal stopped eating.

"How much worse?"

"Ask your grooms."

He did not need to. His face said he already knew.

Lady Ardel said, "The north carts are losing half a day at the switchback."

Tessa looked at her.

"And Kaelmont has a road proposal."

Ardel smiled. "Yes."

"Convenient."

"Mountains are often convenient to road houses."

Luceran watched them.

Neither woman disliked the other.

Neither intended to surrender an inch.

This, he thought, was considerably healthier than the capital, where people smiled while pretending the inch did not exist.

---

The road map came out between fish and roast hare.

Avarren objected.

"Majesty, this is dinner."

"Then why did Ardel bring a map?"

"Because she is Kaelmont," Miren said.

Ardel unfolded it over an empty section of table.

"The current grade costs loaded wagons between two and four hours depending on mud and team. The cut across the shoulder removes one switchback."

Tessa pointed with the handle of her spoon.

"And moves traffic above the east pasture."

"Not through it."

"Above it means runoff through it."

Ardel paused.

"That is a fair objection."

Luceran noticed the absence of defensiveness.

"Can it be drained?"

"Yes."

Tessa said, "Into whose ditch?"

Ardel looked at her.

Then laughed softly.

"I begin to understand why he likes you."

Tessa's eyes moved to Luceran.

Luceran said, "Do not make that political."

"Majesty," Montegrain said, "everything at this table is political."

"The hare?"

"Especially the hare."

Havel, from the service door, said, "Falbrecht preserve."

The table broke into laughter.

Luceran put his forehead briefly against his hand.

"Fine. The hare is compromised. Continue."

Ardel proposed a stone-lined runoff cut and offered Kaelmont surveyors to mark it at no charge.

Tessa immediately asked who would own the improved road.

"The Crown," Ardel said.

"Who collects toll?"

"That has not been agreed."

"There it is."

Ardel looked toward Luceran.

"My house would ask a carriage concession for maintaining the cut. Seven years."

Avarren stopped pretending this was dinner.

"Exclusive?"

"No. Preferred Crown carriage for heavy stone and official loads originating in Kaelmont quarries."

Clavard Clifford, who had been quiet through most of the meal, finally spoke.

"At what rate?"

"Published schedule. Crown audit."

"Maximum?"

"Negotiated annually."

"No."

Ardel smiled. "I was speaking to the king."

Luceran said, "He is speaking for my money."

Clavard nodded once.

Ardel considered.

"Maximum tied to grain carriage index plus axle class."

Clavard said, "Better."

Tessa said, "Village labor?"

"Paid."

"Land?"

"Purchased or leased. No assumption from map labels."

Luceran heard his own recent mistakes returning to him as contract language.

That was uncomfortable.

It was also governance.

"Three years," he said.

Ardel shook her head. "Six."

"Four."

"Five, with first refusal on quarry stone for Crown retaining walls along that road."

Clavard inhaled.

Luceran raised a hand.

"Five years. No first refusal. Published maximum rates. Crown may carry its own freight. Village council reviews drainage before work crosses the east slope. Existing owners are paid before ground is taken. Kaelmont maintains the cut to a standard Rhyse and Royal Works agree before the first Crown load uses it."

Ardel looked at him for a long moment.

"You rehearsed that."

"No."

"Annoying."

"Is that agreement?"

"That is agreement if the written instrument says what you just said."

Avarren muttered, "It will."

Tessa said, "And the old funeral grade stays village access."

Ardel glanced at her map.

"Not part of my road."

"Write it anyway."

Luceran smiled.

"Write it anyway."

Avarren sighed.

The road concession was born between hare and apples.

---

At the lower end of the table, Selise Varo was learning that employment in the royal archive did not prevent her from being interrogated by relatives.

Her aunt's husband, Pellan Edevane, had arrived that afternoon with a packet of letters and the kind of smile that made every statement sound harmless.

"Your mother worries," he said.

"My mother always worries."

"You could write more."

"I arrived three days ago."

"Three days is enough to acquire news."

Selise looked at him.

"There it is."

"What?"

"The second question."

Pellan smiled.

"I asked only one."

"You asked whether I was well. Now you want to know what I have seen."

"I want to know what your life is like."

"Those are not enemies."

He laughed.

Across the room, Ysabet Marris saw the exchange and deliberately turned back to her plate.

Selise noticed that too.

Later she would remember it as the first time Ysabet trusted her to manage her own patronage instead of treating every family connection as an archive breach.

"The castle leaks," Selise said. "The catalog is a crime. Master Marris frightens bad handwriting into improving. The king asks too many questions."

"About what?"

"Everything."

"And the family papers?"

Selise's smile disappeared.

"Restricted."

Pellan lifted both hands.

"I did not ask for contents."

"You asked where the boundary was."

He looked at her differently then.

Not angry.

Proud, perhaps.

"Good," he said.

That was worse in its own way.

---

Jeron's injury changed the table whenever anyone forgot it.

He laughed and went pale.

He reached for bread and stopped halfway.

A servant learned to approach from his left.

Master Tal cut his meat without asking, which embarrassed them both enough that neither mentioned it.

Bellisar had sent a formal letter thanking Bram Tolland for preventing the king from crossing the rope and another thanking the household physician for Jeron's care.

They had also sent a private complaint asking why the hunt route had not been secured in advance.

Rhyse had answered both.

Luceran knew because Rhyse had shown him the copies.

Now Lady Senra Bellisar, Jeron's older cousin, raised her cup.

"To Sergeant Tolland."

Bram was not at the table.

He was on duty outside it.

Luceran looked toward the door.

"Bring him in."

Rhyse said, "He's posted."

"Then bring Edrik in and put Bram on the inner door for thirty seconds."

Rhyse considered the security consequence.

Then nodded.

Edrik appeared, confused. Bram entered behind him.

Thirty-four people looked at him.

He immediately wished to be attacked by something simpler.

Senra stood.

"My cousin is an idiot."

Jeron said, "I object."

"Quiet. You have one useful arm. Sergeant, you kept His Majesty from taking the cord first. Whatever else follows, House Bellisar remembers it."

Bram swallowed.

"My lady."

"That is all."

It was not all.

Everyone at the table had heard House Bellisar create a memory.

Not a contract.

Not an oath.

A debt with no stated price.

Luceran understood that now.

He wondered how many such things he had accumulated without noticing.

Too many.

---

The provincial news arrived with dessert.

A messenger from the south road came in mud to the thighs and refused food until his dispatch was delivered.

The seal belonged to House Damaris.

Avarren read it first.

His face tightened.

Luceran held out his hand.

The letter was not dramatic.

That made it dangerous.

Two river factors had begun withholding forward grain contracts for Edselsto until carriage prices stabilized. No embargo had been declared. No house had refused the Crown. Merchants were simply declining to promise autumn deliveries at spring assumptions while road demand, horse fodder, and mountain storage remained uncertain.

Clavard read over Luceran's shoulder.

"Reasonable."

"Helpful."

"Those are not enemies."

Luceran looked at him.

Clavard almost smiled.

Montegrain said, "You have infected everyone with that phrase."

Ardel tapped the road map.

"This helps."

Tessa said, "In five years."

"In six weeks if weather holds."

"That is not five years."

"The concession is five years. The cut is six weeks."

Clavard shook his head.

"Even six weeks is late for current prices."

Luceran looked toward Havel.

"How much grain do we have?"

The cook stared at him.

"In the kitchen?"

"In Edselsto."

"Ask stores."

Pellin Joss, household stores, was not invited.

That was suddenly absurd.

Luceran looked around his beautiful political table and realized the man who knew how much flour they possessed was eating somewhere below stairs.

"Send for Pellin."

Avarren said, "Now?"

"Now."

Pellin arrived with bread still in his hand.

He looked horrified.

Luceran pointed at an empty chair.

"Sit. How many days of grain?"

"For whom?"

"Everyone."

"That is not a number."

Luceran almost snapped.

Then stopped.

"Explain."

"Castle household, guard, workers, village residents, visiting households, animals?"

"People first."

"At current issue? Crown stores can feed Crown mouths twenty-six days without new purchase. Less if labor keeps arriving. Private houses have their own stores. Village stores are not Crown stores."

Tessa said, "Thank you."

Pellin looked confused.

Luceran said, "Can we buy enough locally?"

"No."

"Can Damaris move it?"

"Yes, if they contract."

"At what price?"

"More than last week."

Clavard said, "How much more?"

"I need tomorrow's market letters."

There it was again.

Government refusing to become a speech.

Luceran folded the Damaris dispatch.

"Then tomorrow we answer with numbers, not outrage. Ask Damaris what volume they will guarantee, at what price, and what carriage risk they require us to bear. No threats. No accusation of disloyalty."

Clavard nodded.

"And tonight?"

Luceran looked at the table.

"Tonight we eat dessert."

Havel, still at the service door, said, "Finally."

---

The dessert was baked apples because the village had apples.

It was the best thing served all night.

Montegrain stole Luceran's second one.

Luceran stabbed his hand with a fork.

Not hard.

Montegrain looked offended.

"Attempted regicide."

"Document it."

Miren said, "I saw nothing."

Ardel said, "I saw everything."

"Of course you did."

The room softened.

That mattered too.

Jeron told the story of his fall badly and improved it every time someone corrected him. By the third telling, the rope had become a siege engine and Bram had struck the king's horse with a tree.

Bram, returned to his post, could hear the laughter through the door.

Selise promised her uncle a letter about her mother and nothing about restricted papers.

Ysabet quietly moved her chair closer to listen when Pellan began describing which capital copyists were considering the journey north.

Tessa and Ardel argued drainage with less hostility than before.

Rhyse ate for the first time in an hour.

Luceran watched them.

This was what he had wanted when he left the capital.

Not privacy exactly.

Choice.

He had imagined choosing people would make politics smaller.

Instead it made politics personal.

He liked these people.

Some of them liked him.

Some desired things from him.

Some would report tonight's dinner to houses, spouses, aunts, guilds, councils, kitchens, barracks, and villages.

None of those facts canceled the others.

For the first time, that did not feel entirely like betrayal.

---

After dinner, Rhyse found him in the passage outside the old family nave.

Luceran had stopped there because the door was open.

Seven candles burned beyond it.

All seven.

He counted without meaning to.

Rhyse noticed.

"Still there."

"Apparently."

"Orven says they have burned since before he was born."

"Orven says many things."

"True."

Neither man entered.

Luceran looked at Rhyse.

"The rope?"

"Human."

"You know that?"

"I know someone tied it. I know the fibers are new. I know the knots are competent. I know the horn was placed where someone could watch our response. I do not know whether the same person did both."

"Do you think they did?"

"Yes."

"Confidence?"

"Enough to change procedure. Not enough to arrest anyone."

Luceran nodded.

That distinction would have infuriated him a month ago.

Now it steadied him.

"What changes?"

"Hunt routes are no longer distributed beyond those who need them. Signals change after use. Funeral grade gets observation, not a gate. We review who knew today's route and when. Quietly."

"No purge."

"No purge."

Luceran looked back at the candles.

"Good."

Rhyse hesitated.

"There is another thing."

"Of course there is."

"The Damaris grain letter arrived very quickly after the first court."

Luceran turned.

"You think connected?"

"I think merchants can count. Edselsto is consuming more every day. Anyone with agents on the roads could see it."

"That sounds disappointingly ordinary."

"Ordinary things kill kingdoms more reliably than ropes."

Luceran looked at him.

"You should embroider that on a banner."

"No."

They walked away from the nave.

The seven candles continued burning behind them.

---

Before midnight, three things left Edselsto.

A Kaelmont courier carried the draft terms of a five-year carriage concession.

A Crown messenger carried a request to House Damaris for guaranteed grain volume and firm prices.

And a private letter from someone at the dinner table traveled south in a packet that did not pass through the royal dispatch room.

The letter contained no state secret.

It did not need one.

It said the king had not panicked after the hunt.

It said Bellisar now owed a personal debt to one of his close guards.

It said Kaelmont had won a road bargain.

It said the village council had begun sitting at royal tables.

It said the young king was learning.

By morning, people who had hoped Edselsto would isolate him would begin deciding what to do about that.

And in the household stores, Pellin Joss began checking old grain tallies against new purchases.

One number did not agree.

He checked it twice.

Then a third time.

He did not wake the king.

He went looking for the clerk who had written it.
