# Nowhere King — Season One, Episode Two
## Packing the Monarchy

**Status:** Committed episode prose  
**Season:** One — The Return to Edselsto  
**Episode:** 02 of 32  
**Reveal ceiling:** Political and material reality. No confirmed supernatural event, no candle failure, no undercroft truth.  
**Primary dramatic purpose:** Show Luceran discovering that a sovereign order does not move a court by itself, then using the most primitive power available to him—his own body, voice, purse, and crown—to smash through institutional delay. Make that action funny, effective, disruptive, coercive, and enormously consequential to people who were not expecting the king to walk into their workday and change their lives.

---

The first obstacle was money.

Luceran had expected that one.

The second was wagons.

He had not expected wagons to become political.

The third was carpenters.

By the time Lord Chancellor Aurel Avarren informed him that carpenters might be the obstacle that defeated the Crown of Embar-Zara, Luceran had been awake for less than two hours and was already considering treason against civilization.

They met in the long household chamber because the council room had become unusable beneath stacks of inventories.

Lists covered the table.

Lists of people.

Lists of beds.

Lists of horses.

Lists of chests.

Lists of royal plate.

Lists of archives that absolutely must accompany the king and other archives which, according to the archivists responsible for them, also absolutely must accompany the king.

Comptroller Mereth Nerezza stood beside three clerks and a box of counters.

"The first departure alone exceeds the ordinary household movement allowance by—"

"I do not care."

"You keep saying that."

"Because you keep telling me numbers as though one of them will cause me to remain here."

"Numbers have brought down better kings than you."

Luceran pointed at him.

"That was almost encouraging."

"It was not."

Avarren slid another sheet across the table.

"The Guild of Stone and Beam cannot provide the labor requested on this notice."

Luceran looked down.

"What labor requested?"

"Sixty masons, thirty carpenters, roofers, sawyers, lime burners, two surveyors, cartwrights, laborers—"

"I did not request sixty masons."

"Your household works office did."

"Why?"

"Because you told them to prepare Edselsto."

"I told them to start packing."

"That caused them to ask what must be ready when you arrive."

"And someone decided sixty masons."

"Someone who has seen a castle before."

Luceran looked at Marshal Harrowmere.

"Do we need sixty masons?"

She shrugged.

"If walls are falling down, I prefer more."

Nerezza said, "If walls are falling down, I prefer fewer kings beneath them."

Luceran ignored him.

"So get sixty masons."

Avarren exhaled.

"That is the difficulty."

"Why?"

"Because they are employed."

Luceran stared.

"All of them?"

"Most skilled men in the capital are generally doing something."

"Then pay them more."

"The guild contracts—"

"Pay the guild."

"The guild says crews cannot simply be removed from contracted works without notice."

Luceran looked from face to face.

Nobody was laughing.

"Carpenters?"

"Same problem."

"Roofers?"

"Same."

"Cartwrights?"

"Worse. Half the city has already begun hiring wagons because rumor says the court is moving."

"Why are private people hiring my wagons?"

"They are not your wagons."

"They are wagons in my city."

Avarren closed his eyes.

"Majesty."

Luceran leaned back.

He had thought the difficult part was deciding to leave.

Apparently the difficult part was that reality had opinions.

A clerk approached Nerezza and whispered into his ear.

Nerezza's expression soured further.

"What?"

"The Royal Kitchens require another twenty-two carts."

"For what?"

"Cooking iron, dry stores, service ware, salt, flour, preserved meat, charcoal, barrels—"

"We are not transporting the entire kitchen."

The clerk swallowed.

"The Master of Kitchens says His Majesty cannot hold court from a cookfire."

Luceran considered this.

"Tell him I can."

The clerk looked horrified.

Avarren said, "Do not tell him that."

Luceran stood.

Everyone looked at him.

"Fine."

Avarren did not like the word.

"What does 'fine' mean?"

"It means find the wagons we have. Load what fits. The rest follows later."

"Majesty—"

"Take the Crown seals. Take coin. Take enough archive material for me to govern. Take food. Take beds if someone thinks beds are sacred. Take my clothes. Take the guards. Everything else can catch up."

Nerezza said, "And the work crews?"

Luceran picked up the guild refusal.

The page contained several paragraphs explaining shortages, prior obligations, customary notice, guild privilege, seasonal commitments, and why a royal demand that had arrived yesterday could not reasonably be fulfilled by tomorrow.

He read half of it.

Then he folded it.

"Fine."

Avarren stood too.

"Again, I dislike that word."

Luceran walked toward the door.

"Where are you going?"

"To find a carpenter."

There was a pause.

Avarren said, "Personally?"

Luceran kept walking.

Behind him came the sound of chairs.

"My lord."

He opened the chamber door.

"My lord, what are you doing?"

Luceran entered the outer hall.

Two secretaries flattened themselves against the wall.

Avarren followed.

"Luceran."

That earned him a glance.

"Where are you going?"

"Outside."

"Why?"

"Because apparently all the carpenters have vanished."

"That is not what I said."

"Then I shall be relieved to find one."

Nerezza emerged behind him carrying his coat.

Harrowmere followed because she had begun enjoying herself.

Brother Calven appeared from another corridor, saw the procession, and made the mistake of asking what was happening.

"The king is going to find a carpenter," Avarren said.

Calven looked at Luceran.

"Why?"

"Because nobody else can."

They reached the main stairs.

Captain Tovan Rhyse was coming upward with two guards.

He saw Luceran descending at speed.

"Your Majesty?"

"Good. You."

Rhyse stopped.

Luceran pointed at the two guards.

"You and you."

Both men straightened.

"Come."

Rhyse looked toward the palace doors.

"Majesty, you should not leave the palace without an escort."

Luceran kept descending.

"I have one."

"Two men are not an escort."

Luceran snapped his fingers over his shoulder.

"Then bring more."

The guards looked at Rhyse.

Rhyse looked briefly toward heaven.

Then he shouted for six more men and ran after his king.

---

The front gate guards had perhaps thirty seconds to decide whether the king walking toward them on foot constituted an emergency.

They chose correctly.

It did.

The gate captain stepped forward.

"Your Majesty, is there a carriage—"

"No."

"Horse?"

"No."

"Destination?"

Luceran walked past him.

The captain joined the procession.

By the time Luceran crossed the outer square he had accumulated Avarren, Nerezza, Harrowmere, Calven, Captain Rhyse, nine guards, three secretaries, two pages, one horrified household clerk, and Montegrain, who had emerged from the guest wing after hearing that the king had escaped.

Montegrain caught up.

"Where are we going?"

"To find civilization."

"Have you checked the taverns?"

"Carpenters."

"Ah."

Avarren said, "Do not encourage him."

"I don't yet know what we're doing."

"Neither does he."

Luceran heard that.

"I know exactly what I am doing."

Montegrain looked at Avarren.

"That is worse."

The capital unfolded around them.

Morning traffic split before the royal party.

Vendors bowed too late. Apprentices stared. A woman carrying onions dropped three of them and then seemed unable to decide whether retrieving onions in front of the king was disrespectful.

Luceran had lived in the palace so long that he had forgotten the city contained people who were not waiting to speak to him.

Then he heard hammering.

He stopped.

Avarren nearly walked into him.

Across the street, scaffolding covered the front of a large noble townhouse.

Stone blocks stood beside the curb.

Two men were setting a window frame.

Another was shaping a lintel.

Half a dozen laborers carried timber through an open gate.

Luceran pointed.

"There."

Avarren looked.

"No."

Luceran crossed the street.

"Majesty."

The guards surged after him.

Workers began noticing.

Hammering stopped in sections, as though silence were spreading across the building.

A man on the second scaffold looked down.

The king looked up.

For several seconds they regarded one another.

Then the man on the scaffold said, very carefully:

"Your Majesty."

"What are you doing?"

The worker looked at the half-fitted window frame.

"Window casements, Majesty."

"Are you a carpenter?"

"Yes."

Luceran pointed at the stoneworker.

"You."

The stoneworker froze.

"Majesty?"

"Mason?"

"Yes, Majesty."

"Good. Come down."

Avarren reached Luceran.

"Please do not conscript Lord Edmund Moncler's building site."

Luceran turned.

"Whose?"

"Lord Edmund Moncler."

Luceran looked at the house.

"Does he live here?"

"Eventually."

"Then he can wait."

The carpenter climbed down.

He was perhaps thirty, broad-handed, with wood dust in his beard and the expression of a man expecting either promotion or execution.

The mason joined him.

Luceran asked the carpenter his name.

"Jory Fell, Majesty."

"Mason?"

"Derrin Voss, Majesty."

"Do you have families?"

Both men hesitated.

Jory said, "A wife. Two children."

Derrin said, "Mother, sister, three nephews."

Luceran nodded.

"You are both entering royal service."

Silence.

Jory blinked.

Derrin said, "Majesty?"

"You leave for Castle Edselsto tomorrow morning."

Avarren made a quiet choking sound.

Luceran kept going.

"I need carpenters and masons. You are carpenters and masons."

Derrin looked toward the unfinished house.

"Majesty, Lord Moncler—"

"May file a petition."

Montegrain turned away to hide his face.

Jory looked at the scaffolding, then at Luceran.

"Tomorrow?"

"At dawn."

"My wife does not know I'm leaving."

"Then tell her."

"Majesty, I—"

Luceran saw it.

Not refusal.

Something worse.

The man was afraid to refuse.

For the first time that morning, Luceran slowed down.

He had walked out of the palace furious at people who told him no.

Now he was standing in front of a man who did not know whether he was allowed to.

That was new.

Luceran looked at Nerezza.

"How much?"

Nerezza stared.

"For what?"

"Royal field pay."

"Depends on grade."

"Master carpenter."

Jory opened his mouth.

Luceran glanced at him.

"Are you a master carpenter?"

"Journeyman, Majesty."

"Today you are a royal journeyman carpenter."

Nerezza named a weekly rate.

Jory's eyes widened.

Luceran said, "Double it for the first month."

Nerezza's expression became religiously hostile.

"No."

"Why?"

"Because you cannot set wage scales by walking down a street."

"I just did."

"You will have every carpenter in the city chasing you."

"Excellent. We need carpenters."

Harrowmere laughed aloud.

Luceran pointed at Derrin.

"Same."

Nerezza said, "No."

"Yes."

Avarren stepped between them before the treasury became a battlefield.

"Majesty, if you intend this to be royal compulsory service, say so. If you intend to hire them, let them answer."

Luceran looked back at Jory and Derrin.

That distinction had not occurred to him.

Which irritated him because it should have.

"Fine."

He faced the two tradesmen.

"I am rebuilding Castle Edselsto. I leave tomorrow. I need skilled men. Royal service, double ordinary field wage for the first month, food on the road, wages continuing while employed, and..." He looked at Nerezza.

Nerezza said nothing.

"Family allowance."

Nerezza shut his eyes.

"Modest family allowance," Avarren supplied quickly.

Luceran nodded.

"You may refuse."

That surprised everyone.

Jory looked at Derrin.

Derrin looked at the guards.

Luceran noticed.

"You may actually refuse."

Derrin swallowed.

"My lord Moncler will not like it."

"Your lord Moncler is not king."

"True, Majesty."

A long pause.

Then Jory said, "Double?"

"Yes."

"For a month?"

"Yes."

"And royal wage after?"

"Yes."

Jory looked up at the half-finished window.

"I was fitting casements yesterday."

"It is still today," Derrin muttered.

"You understand me."

Jory rubbed both hands over his face.

"My wife is going to kill me."

"Tell her the king did it."

"She may kill you."

Montegrain coughed.

Luceran smiled.

"Will you come?"

Jory looked once more at the house.

Then at the king.

"Yes."

Derrin sighed.

"If he goes, I go."

"Why?"

"He owes me three silver."

Jory turned.

"I do not."

"You do."

Luceran pointed at both of them.

"Good. Dawn. North gate."

Then he looked past them at the rest of the site.

There were sixteen men watching.

Avarren saw the look.

"No."

Luceran raised his voice.

"Any other carpenter, mason, roofer, cartwright or laborer on this site who wants royal wage and a month at double field rate, report to the north gate before dawn."

The site exploded into whispers.

Avarren closed his eyes.

Nerezza looked physically ill.

Harrowmere said, "You may have solved the labor shortage."

Nerezza said, "He has created twelve new ones."

---

Lord Edmund Moncler learned that the king had recruited his building crew while standing in his bath.

The messenger was made to repeat it.

"His Majesty did what?"

"Offered royal service, my lord."

"To whom?"

"Fell. Voss. Then the whole site."

"On my contract?"

"Yes, my lord."

"From my scaffolding?"

"Yes."

Moncler stood.

Water ran off him.

"Get me a robe."

His valet handed it over.

"And my carriage."

"To the palace?"

Moncler stopped.

He imagined arriving at the palace to demand that a newly bereaved king return six carpenters and four masons so his west gallery could be finished.

"No."

The valet waited.

Moncler swore.

"Send to the guild."

"What shall I say?"

"Say the Crown is stealing contracted labor off private works."

"Stealing?"

Moncler considered.

"Recruiting."

The valet nodded.

"And send to my steward. Cover the open masonry before it rains."

He dressed.

By the time he reached breakfast, the story had changed twice.

The king had personally conscripted fifty men.

The king had threatened to hang a guild master.

The king had seized Moncler's entire estate.

None of those things had happened.

Moncler still had no carpenters.

---

Jory Fell's wife did not kill him.

She considered it.

He found Ellyn at home mending a sleeve while their eldest daughter copied letters at the table.

Jory entered carrying a purse.

Ellyn looked up.

"You are early."

"The king came to the site."

She waited.

Jory put the purse down.

"The king."

"Yes."

"Our king."

"There is only one."

"What did you do?"

"Nothing."

"Jory."

"He hired me."

"For what?"

"Edselsto."

Ellyn stared.

Their daughter stopped writing.

"Where is that?"

"North."

"How far north?"

Jory sat.

"Far enough."

"When?"

"Tomorrow morning."

Ellyn stood.

"No."

"He asked."

"Then tell him no."

"I said yes."

"Why?"

He pushed the purse toward her.

She looked inside.

Her expression changed.

Not greed.

Calculation.

Rent.

Winter coal.

Shoes.

Their son's apprenticeship fee.

The debt to her brother.

The roof patch they had delayed.

"That is for a month?"

"Advance."

"How much after?"

He told her.

Ellyn sat down again.

Their daughter whispered, "Are we rich?"

"No," both parents said.

Jory rubbed his face.

"I was building Lord Moncler's windows this morning."

Ellyn began laughing.

He looked offended.

"I am serious."

"I know."

"Tomorrow I am riding with the royal wagons to rebuild the king's ancestral castle."

She laughed harder.

Their daughter started laughing because her mother was.

Jory eventually did too.

Then Ellyn stopped.

"How dangerous?"

"I don't know."

"How long?"

"I don't know."

"Where will you sleep?"

"I don't know."

"Will you come home?"

"I don't know."

The laughter died.

There it was.

The other half of royal fortune.

Jory reached across the table.

"I could say no."

Ellyn looked at the purse.

"No."

He looked at her.

She hated herself for the answer.

"No," she repeated. "You cannot."

He knew she did not mean legally.

---

Derrin Voss had no wife to persuade.

He had a mother.

This was worse.

She listened in complete silence while he explained that the king had personally hired him in the street.

When he finished, she slapped him.

Derrin held his cheek.

"What was that for?"

"Standing close enough to a king to be noticed."

"I was working."

"Work farther away."

"Mother."

She took the royal advance.

Counted it.

Counted it again.

Then slapped the purse down on the table.

"This is too much."

"It is double field wage."

"Why?"

"Because His Majesty was angry."

"Never take money set by an angry king."

"That is not a proverb."

"It is now."

His sister leaned in through the doorway.

"Are you really going to Edselsto?"

"Tomorrow."

"Can you take Tavin?"

"No."

"He's nearly fourteen."

"No."

"He needs a trade."

"I am not taking your son to a ruined mountain castle because the king had a tantrum about carpenters."

His mother pointed at him.

"That. Remember you said that. Never say it near anyone important."

---

By noon, the Guild of Stone and Beam had discovered that skilled labor could, in fact, be found.

This discovery occurred with miraculous speed.

Three guild officers arrived at the palace demanding an audience.

Behind them came lists.

Crews.

Names.

Rates.

Existing obligations that could apparently be rearranged after all.

The Master of Royal Works, who had spent the morning explaining why no properly chartered team could depart before the following week, now stood in front of Avarren insisting that unregistered street recruitment threatened the integrity of royal construction.

Avarren let him finish.

Then asked:

"How many masons can you provide tomorrow?"

The master hesitated.

"Twenty-four."

"Carpenters?"

"Seventeen."

"Roofers?"

"Eight."

Avarren looked at the clerk beside him.

"Write that down."

The master swallowed.

"With proper royal contracts."

"Of course."

"And guild rates."

"Discuss them with Nerezza."

The master paled.

Avarren almost smiled.

"His Majesty appears to have found the remaining labor himself."

---

Luceran returned to the palace before luncheon.

He felt triumphant.

This was unfortunate.

Montegrain walked beside him.

"You understand that you did not actually solve the problem."

"I found carpenters."

"You found two."

"I found a building full."

"You also made every guild in the city terrified you will continue walking around."

"Good."

"That is one interpretation."

Luceran looked toward the palace gates.

People were already waiting.

Guild officers.

Household servants.

Petitioners.

A noble carriage with Moncler's crest.

Luceran stopped.

Montegrain followed his gaze.

"Ah."

"What?"

"The court followed you outside."

Luceran glared at him.

"It was one street."

"Apparently sufficient."

Avarren caught up.

"Before you do anything else today, we are establishing rules for royal recruitment."

"I thought you opposed bureaucracy."

"I oppose you creating it accidentally."

"I hired men."

"You altered private contracts, guild privileges, wage expectations, household labor markets and one nobleman's construction schedule before breakfast."

Luceran considered that.

"I was efficient."

Avarren stared at him.

Montegrain said, "Do not encourage him."

"I am not."

"You sounded impressed."

"I am furious."

"You can be both."

Luceran kept walking.

He was still smiling.

---

By afternoon, the palace yards looked like a siege conducted by furniture.

Chests filled the paving stones.

Servants argued over which linens were royal property.

Three scribes nearly came to blows over archive space in the first wagons.

The kitchens produced a list of copper pots so long Luceran assumed it was satire.

It was not.

Captain Rhyse divided guards between those leaving with the first convoy and those remaining to secure the capital residence.

The stablemaster informed everyone that horses could not be multiplied by decree.

Nerezza replied that neither could oats.

Household officers discovered that half the people Luceran intended to leave behind had already begun petitioning to go.

People wanted proximity to the king.

People wanted new posts.

People feared losing influence.

People feared mountain winters.

People feared staying behind more.

Frederick Doss found his name on the second household list.

He found Mara's name nowhere because she was already north.

He found his own designation beside:

**upper household footman — provisional transfer**

He read it three times.

A senior footman leaned over his shoulder.

"Congratulations."

"For what?"

"You are going."

Frederick looked toward the wagons.

"Tomorrow?"

"First household after the royal party."

"I thought tomorrow was the royal party."

"It is."

Frederick turned.

"What?"

The senior footman grinned.

"The king has decided he is going tomorrow."

"Did Edselsto answer?"

"No."

"Does anyone know if there is a roof?"

"No."

Frederick stared.

The senior footman shrugged.

"He's king."

That answer explained too much.

---

Oren Edevane learned of the dawn departure from his mother.

Lady Isolde Edevane had received the information from a Pell cousin whose husband dealt in wagon leather.

Oren sat in her private parlor while she folded a letter.

"He really means to go."

"He usually means what he says for at least a day."

"Mother."

"Your uncle said the same."

"He recruited masons off Moncler's site."

"I know."

"Personally."

"I know."

"Moncler is furious."

"Moncler is always furious when someone else remembers he is not sovereign."

Oren leaned back.

"What do we do?"

Isolde looked at him.

There was affection in the look.

And calculation.

Luceran would have hated it.

"We decide whether House Edevane follows immediately, follows later, or keeps one hand here and one there."

"Luceran will see that as maneuvering."

"Luceran sees breathing as maneuvering at present."

"His parents just died."

"I am aware."

"You sound unsympathetic."

"I am trying to keep sympathy from making us stupid."

Oren looked toward the window.

"He thinks he can get away from all of us."

Isolde smiled faintly.

"He is taking all of us with him."

"Not all."

"No. Which will make being chosen matter."

Oren understood.

Rooms.

Road places.

Appointments.

Access.

The new court had begun before anyone had reached it.

---

Montegrain sent a letter to Elbren that evening.

It did not contain state secrets.

It did not need to.

**Luceran is leaving the capital tomorrow. Yes, actually tomorrow. He has decided that the proper response to being told qualified masons cannot be found on short notice is to walk into the city and recruit them personally. Lord Edmund Moncler now lacks a west gallery. The Guild of Stone and Beam has consequently discovered twenty-four available masons it did not possess this morning.**

He stopped.

Then added:

**He is grieving. He is angry. He is not mad. There is an important difference. He believes Edselsto will give him distance from politics. Do not laugh when you hear this. He has had enough people laugh at him without knowing why.**

He sealed the letter.

Then wrote a second note, this one for Luceran.

**If you leave at dawn, I am coming. Someone must tell you when your solutions create three additional problems.**

---

At sunset, Lord Edmund Moncler finally received his audience.

Luceran had intended to refuse him.

Avarren insisted.

Moncler entered controlled enough to prove he had spent the afternoon practicing.

"Your Majesty."

"Lord Moncler."

"I understand several men formerly employed upon my residence are now in royal service."

"Yes."

"My west gallery remains open to the weather."

"So I am told."

"I have also been told that Your Majesty invited the remainder of the site to abandon their contracted labor."

"I offered them employment."

"Under royal gaze. Surrounded by guards."

Luceran's expression changed.

Moncler saw that he had struck something real.

Good.

"Did any refuse?" Luceran asked.

"Would you have?"

"I told them they could."

"Majesty, kings do not possess an ordinary voice."

Luceran disliked the sentence immediately because it sounded like Avarren.

Moncler continued.

"If you ask a mason to accompany you while nine armed guards stand behind you, the man's freedom to consider the offer may be technically intact and practically complicated."

Luceran looked toward Avarren.

The chancellor did not rescue him.

"I paid them."

"I know."

"Well."

"Very well. Which is why I suspect several of my remaining laborers will present themselves at your gate by dawn."

"Then they want to go."

"Probably."

Moncler paused.

"And I want compensation for the delay."

There it was.

Luceran almost laughed.

Even being morally correct had become a petition.

"What do you want?"

"Priority access to royal stone contracts when Edselsto begins ordering decorative work."

"No."

"Then repayment of my lost deposits."

"Submit the figures."

Moncler bowed.

Not victory.

Not defeat.

Government.

As he turned to leave, Luceran said:

"Moncler."

The lord paused.

"I should not have walked onto your site and assumed everyone could simply come because I said so."

Avarren looked at Luceran.

Moncler did too.

Luceran hated how difficult the next sentence felt.

"But I am still taking the men who agreed."

Moncler smiled despite himself.

"I suspected as much."

---

Jory Fell arrived at the north gate before dawn with one tool chest, two blankets, a change of clothes, three loaves of bread, a carving knife, a packet from his wife, and the expression of a man whose life had outrun him.

Derrin Voss arrived five minutes later carrying enough stone tools to lame a mule.

"You brought all of them?"

"I don't know what kind of stone."

"You think the mountain changes chisels?"

"Yes."

"It does not."

"You are a carpenter."

"Stone is stone."

Derrin stared at him.

"That is the stupidest thing you have ever said."

Around them, royal wagons assembled.

Not neatly.

Never neatly.

Guild crews arrived in clumps.

Royal Works men arrived offended that private tradesmen had reached the gate before them.

Two master carpenters argued about precedence while apprentices loaded saws.

A team of masons from the official Crown works office arrived with a wagon painted in royal colors and the demeanor of men who had spent the previous day being told they could not possibly be ready.

Jory watched them.

"I thought there were no royal carpenters."

Derrin snorted.

"There were always royal carpenters."

"Then why did the king hire us?"

"Because someone told him there weren't."

"That seems dangerous."

"You're learning government."

Nearby, Nerezza stood beneath a lantern arguing with three quartermasters.

Avarren had not slept.

Harrowmere was already mounted.

Rhyse moved among the guards.

Montegrain appeared with two horses and a servant who looked personally betrayed by the hour.

Frederick carried sealed household packets from one wagon to another.

Lord Edmund Moncler's unfinished west gallery sat somewhere behind them in the sleeping city, open to the weather.

Ellyn Fell and the children had come to the gate.

Jory's daughter hugged him hard.

Ellyn handed him a wrapped parcel.

"What is it?"

"Food."

"The Crown feeds us."

"The Crown thought it had no carpenters yesterday."

He accepted the parcel.

Derrin's mother did not come.

She had said goodbye at home.

She had also sent three scarves and a jar of salve.

The eastern sky began to pale.

Then the palace doors opened.

Luceran came down the steps.

No grand departure robe.

No coronation mantle.

Riding coat. Sword. Gloves. Crown absent.

Avarren met him at the bottom.

"Last chance."

"To do what?"

"Sleep."

Luceran looked at the wagons.

At the masons.

At the carpenters.

At the guards.

At the servants.

At the clerks.

At the merchants already hovering near the edge of the square trying to discover what the royal convoy had forgotten so they could sell it farther north.

He had wanted a smaller court.

Somehow, before he had even left, it had grown.

He refused to notice the lesson.

"Open the gate."

The gate captain raised his arm.

Chains moved.

Timbers groaned.

Beyond the wall, the northern road disappeared between waking houses.

Luceran mounted.

Behind him, Jory Fell looked at Derrin Voss.

"Yesterday I was fitting windows."

Derrin adjusted the strap on his tool chest.

"Today you're rebuilding a royal castle."

"That is a stupid sentence."

"Still true."

The gates opened.

The king rode north.

And half the city began calculating how soon it needed to follow.
