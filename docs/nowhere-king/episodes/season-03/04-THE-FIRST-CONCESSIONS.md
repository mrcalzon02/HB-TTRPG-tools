# Season Three, Episode 04 — The First Concessions

The rider from Dols arrived with mud on both boots and a cracked wax cover around his letter. He had paid for a fresh horse at the southern road house out of the reeve's own allowance, and he wanted Etta to know that before anyone suggested he had stopped to drink.

Etta wrote the hour and the condition of the seal. She brought Yara to witness its opening, then read the address again. It was a reply to the royal evidence packet, not a grant of passage.

"His hand?" Yara asked.

"The reeve's clerk, with the reeve's mark beside it. We have his earlier receipt for comparison."

The letter would receive named, voluntary evidence concerning the returned soldier and the disputed royal order. It would not compel Jalen Orris to appear. It would not certify the dead man's identity, excuse the king's contradictory declarations, or bind another river bench. The reeve had copied the relevant entry numbers into the margin so that no one could mistake his offer for an invitation to erase the first denial.

There was a second sheet: the Dols road keeper had inspected the wall from his own side. Water had taken mortar from beneath the northern coping. Until his mason and the municipal watch accepted the repair, no second loaded civilian wagon would pass. The reeve's evidence letter did not change the road keeper's decision.

Etta entered the two sheets separately. A clerk sent for Tomas and found him at the yard counting the Crown's six unmoved grain sacks for the third time that week.

"Have we got a road?" he asked.

"We have an answer," Etta said.

"That wasn't the question."

"No," she said, "and the man who wrote it knew that."

Ysabet ordered the evidence letter copied for the king, leaving the original under Etta's seal. Yara kept the road keeper's report in the transport correspondence rather than attaching it to the physician's account. The distinction cost another hour of copying and made it impossible for an impatient courtier to wave one impressive packet and claim that Dols had surrendered every objection.

The rider accepted his receipt and asked for bread before he rode back. He had brought a useful answer, and the answer was no to nearly everything the Crown wanted.

---

At Dols, the reeve was listening to a quarrel about the same damaged wall. A miller wanted the second wagon admitted before the next rain, because his customers could not live on the honor of a sound inspection. The road keeper had brought a mason who could show exactly where a probing iron disappeared beneath the coping.

"If we let the first wheel over, the next merchant will demand the same favor," the keeper said.

"The first merchant will pay his toll," the miller answered.

"And when the stones go into the river, will his toll haul them out?"

The reeve refused to turn the question into a contest of loyalty to Luceran. Dols had lost money from the closed road. The watch had also lost men in the earlier trouble, and the households who paid for that watch had a right to know whether a repaired wall could hold a loaded cart.

He authorized the mason to mark the defective stretch in red chalk and produce a priced account for the town's own portion of the work. The Crown could offer materials or paid hands; neither offer would appoint the king's mason as the town's inspector. The road keeper retained the right to refuse a wheel until the work was seen.

Senna, who had remained in Dols to answer further questions if lawfully called, heard the decision from the public bench. Harven was already preparing to return by the lower road. Neither was required to sit through the other man's account. Senna could testify that Luceran admitted the earlier order; she could not tell the reeve which stones would hold a wagon. The reeve seemed almost grateful to have found a question with an answer that a mason could test.

His clerk asked whether they should write to Merehaven to recommend a matching inspection.

"Tell them what we have done, if they ask," the reeve said. "Do not make our judgment into theirs."

He knew the Crown might call this obstruction. He also knew that if a child went into the water under a cart because he had been flattered into haste, no court at Edselsto would stand beside the child's mother and explain his clever diplomacy.

---

Hesta Vale had brought the second drain block to the edge of the repaired rut without putting a rope around it. Lenn had packed the stones two days before, and the top looked sound. Hesta knew what lay below it because she had watched the wet earth come out.

Tomas came with a Crown clerk, a mason, and a proposal that began with the words *royal priority*. Hesta heard him out and then asked who would pay when priority meant her own customers waited.

"That is what we came to settle," Tomas said.

"Then do not start by telling me I have already settled it."

The proposed contract gave the Crown first call on her quarry carts for thirty days, at a fixed price per measured load. It also offered her the use of the repaired receiving-yard turn. In return she would keep a team available whenever the king sent for it. Hesta's brother had written the cost of that promise on a board: fodder, two men's wages, a hired wheelwright, and the loss of a private order if the royal summons came late.

"Your thirty days could eat my winter," she said.

Tomas did not pretend otherwise. He crossed out *first call* and asked what she would sell instead.

Hesta wanted first refusal on twelve specified Crown-side drain and gutter-stone loads, with a mason measuring each before acceptance, at a posted price that could not be lowered after the stone arrived. The Crown would have to offer each load to her first, but she could refuse within one daylight day; if she did, the Crown could buy elsewhere without penalty. The privilege would expire after thirty days or twelve accepted loads, whichever came first. She would provide a paid porter for each accepted haul and keep the repaired turn safe for ordinary traffic during the agreed working hours. A dispute over damage or measurement would go before two living assessors, one chosen by each party, with an independent mason if they disagreed.

"That is a preference," the Crown clerk said.

"It is," Hesta answered. "I would rather have a preference I can work than a royal promise that keeps my horses hungry."

She also demanded that the earlier four-season toll-free Crown-track right remain untouched. Tomas had never intended to remove it, but he wrote it out again. Bram, who still could not take a heavy wheel, had been keeping Hesta's tools and receipts. He noticed that the draft said the Crown would pay *on delivery*, not *on acceptance*. Hesta made Tomas choose. He wrote *on measured acceptance*, with a separate porter fee payable even if the mason rejected a block for reasons not caused by the porter.

The new draft would offend suppliers who had hoped to bid for all twelve loads. Hesta knew that. She had been one of those suppliers often enough.

---

Luceran heard the proposed preference at the small council table while rain rattled against a patched shutter. Damaris had brought the paymaster's book. The Jorvan advance was still twenty-four marks borrowed, not twenty-four marks earned. A portion had become the carter's conditional deposit, the paid rut repair, and wages. No east-gate surplus had yet been certified for transfer to the creditor, and the twenty-seven-mark cap remained exactly where the witnesses had put it.

"You want to sell a piece of our freedom to choose stone," Luceran said.

"Not sell," Tomas replied. "Trade. She holds her own carts and her own passing place. We have an unfinished drain."

"Then another quarryman will ask the same."

"Another quarryman can offer us something we need," Damaris said. "Hesta has already put her people and her stone into the road. We can count what she offers."

Rhyse disliked the idea of the Crown granting a private preference beside a road it had only just recovered the use of. A royal officer might mistake the contract for an order to push other carts aside. Ysabet suggested that the gate keeper retain ordinary control of traffic and that no preferential passage attach to Hesta's loads. Bessa's east-gate books would remain outside the bargain entirely.

Luceran read the amended words. Hesta's privilege would be public, limited to Crown-side drain and gutter stone, priced in advance, and enforceable by an ordinary claim for the cost of a wrongly refused offered load, not by seizure of the road. The Crown could still reject defective stone and buy elsewhere if Hesta declined. Hesta could still sell to other customers. No Dols permission was implied.

"It looks small," he said.

"It will not look small to the mason whose cart waits for an offer," Ysabet said.

He wanted to reject the humiliation of having a quarrywoman tell him what a royal order was worth. He also remembered the toll books at the east gate and the chair beside them that belonged to a creditor's man. The court could not afford to act as if independent people had ceased to possess choices.

"Offer it in the yard, with the other suppliers allowed to read it," he said. "No private grant."

Damaris made him approve the cost of copying and posting it. He did, with the irritation of a man who had discovered that public honesty required paying clerks.

The Dols letter lay beside the contract. Luceran had wanted to answer the reeve with a grand promise of protection. Ysabet reminded him that the reeve had requested evidence, not a king's feelings. They would send a dated acknowledgment of the offer and ask Jalen whether he wished the physicians' copies transmitted. No reply from Jalen would be treated as consent.

---

Jalen had placed Leora's letter beneath the wooden horse to keep the page from curling. He had begun three replies and disliked them all. The first sounded like a deposition. The second sounded as though he were asking a widow to comfort him. The third began with an apology for things he could not remember doing.

Yara found him crossing out the opening line.

"Could you hold the paper?" he asked. "Not the pen."

She held the corner while he wrote. His hand tired quickly, and the brace supported his shoulder rather than repairing the body beneath it. He wrote that he remembered being Jalen Orris before the wound, that he had no memory of a marriage to Leora, and that he would answer questions about his own life if she wished to write again. He would not pretend to be Corven. He did not ask her to believe that the body's history was unimportant.

At the end he added a memory of his sister breaking a loaf unevenly and taking the larger piece when she thought he had eaten. He stopped there. It was not a proof of anything Leora had asked; it was something he wanted another living person to know.

"Should I say I am sorry?" he asked Yara.

"For what?"

"For being here."

She did not answer quickly. "You may be sorry that she suffers. You needn't confess to making a choice you did not make."

He changed one sentence and left the rest. Yara witnessed only that he had written and sealed the letter himself, not that she agreed with his memories. Etta entered its dispatch as private correspondence addressed to Leora. It would take ordinary road time. It had not arrived when the courier left the lower gate.

Isera came to check the cloth beneath his brace. Jalen told her he would not send the examination to Dols that day. He wanted to read both physicians' copies again and might authorize a narrower extract later.

"Will that spoil the king's bargain?" he asked.

"No bargain with your body has been made," she said.

For once the answer relieved him. Miren brought fresh cloth and stayed while Isera adjusted it. They talked about the nuisance of damp bedding, not the fate of kingdoms. When Miren returned to the hall, she carried no message about Jalen's willingness to be seen.

---

The agreement with Hesta was read aloud beneath the receiving-yard awning. A Morcant supplier and two independent haulers stood close enough to hear it. One of them objected that the king had granted a preference without inviting a competing price. Hesta let him finish.

"The price is written there," she said. "You can read what I must do for it. I cannot take twelve loads without stone, carts and paid men. Nor can I make the king accept a bad block."

The supplier asked Ysabet whether the Crown would now refuse to inspect his stone. Ysabet pointed to the one-day refusal provision and the thirty-day limit. He did not like the answer. He did understand it.

Luceran signed in the presence of the Crown mason, Tomas, Hesta's brother, the paymaster's clerk and two independent witnesses. Hesta made her own mark after the terms were read back, including the preserved four-season toll privilege, fixed measurement, porter fees, expiry and remedy. A copy went to the yard board, one to Hesta, and one to Ysabet's ordinary contracts chest. The original did not go into the Jorvan toll ledger.

Hesta took the second block forward only after the mason had tested the rut with a rod. Lenn guided the rope. The block crossed the repaired turn, but the mason found a chipped corner that would leave a gap at the drain mouth. He refused it for the intended place. Hesta was angry for a moment, then made her brother mark the chip and ask whether the stone could be cut for a shorter course. The mason would decide after measuring the remaining work. It was not an accepted second block. The third remained untested.

The porter received his fee. The Crown owed no stone price for the rejected block. Hesta retained the right to offer the next conforming load under the new preference, and the supplier who had objected watched carefully enough to learn how the rule worked.

Bram entered the porter payment from his dry stool. The contract had not cured his injured leg. It had given him a reason to keep an accurate book, because the next argument would be about whose money had bought the day's work.

At the east gate Bessa's observer copied the market tally while a boy pasted the yard notice beside the ordinary toll schedule. He read the preference twice and asked whether it altered the gate's expenses.

"Not a penny," Bessa said. "If it does, they can come and show us the signed words."

The observer wrote that down in his own notebook. A creditor who had paid for access to numbers could not afford to let every royal promise become a charge against the same revenue.

---

That evening Damaris brought Luceran the first list of what reopening the great hall would actually require. The kitchen needed dry fuel, two more working hearths and a repaired drain before it could feed guests. The musicians would want wages in coin, not a promise of royal attention. The watch captain would not release men from the outer posts merely to stand beside candles in a handsome room. Guest chambers still had damp walls. The Sorelle factor had offered a modest company of players, provided their road allowance was paid whether the court danced or not.

"Then we cannot reopen," Luceran said.

"We can decide what we can afford to reopen," Damaris answered.

He looked at the figures and at the Dols letter, which had cost its own courier and yielded no grain passage. Hesta had won a small, public privilege that would irritate other suppliers. Jorvan had won a seat at his gate. Dols had won the right to examine his confession without forgiving him. None of them had agreed to love him, and none of them had given him a coalition.

Miren passed through with a bundle of clean cloth for the upper sickroom. He asked whether she would attend the reopening if it took place.

"As a guest?" she asked.

"However you wished."

"I would rather know whether the kitchens have enough hands to wash up afterward."

He almost laughed. He had hoped for something more tender, but it was a fair question and she had not asked him to stop speaking to her. She went on without offering an evening together.

At the eastern cistern, Luceran asked Edselsto which of the old hall hearths had once drawn best. The castle named a flue and a mason's alteration from a century earlier. He ordered a living chimney sweep to examine it before anyone lit a fire. The answer might save a day's work. It could not hire a cook or compel a guest to come.

He passed the family nave on his way upstairs. All seven candles were burning.

Outside, the yard notice stirred in the rain. A king had paid for one honest bargain, and the people who had not received its benefits were already reading the price.
