# Season Three, Episode 07 — Business Outside the Walls

The baker had rented the back room for the price of two loaves and the use of her little stove. Orlen Morcant had offered coin instead, which was why she had agreed to the arrangement in the first place. Bread did not pay for a new roof tile.

She had moved her flour sacks before the first guest arrived. The room was narrow, warm and rather too full of the smell of yeast. It contained one table, four chairs, and a bench made from a door that had outlived its hinges. There was no silver line in its walls. There was no reason there should have been.

Orlen came carrying his bracket quotation, folded in oilcloth against the rain. The two haulers arrived separately. One had lost half a day waiting for a team to clear the wet turn below Edselsto; the other had spent the morning arguing over a broken wheel pin and wanted payment before he took another Crown load uphill. Hesta Vale's brother brought a board on which the dimensions of the rejected drain block had been marked in charcoal.

The baker gave them their room and went back to kneading. She could hear their chairs through the partition, and sometimes the words, when someone grew angry enough. Nobody had sworn her to silence. Nobody had asked her to listen.

The first quarrel was about who should pay for the stove.

"The room was my proposal," Orlen said.

"And my men came on your invitation," one hauler answered.

"Then I shall pay for the room. You shall pay for your own bread."

It was a small distinction, but the baker liked it. A gentleman who paid for the thing he had promised was already preferable to a gentleman who promised her that the Crown would be grateful.

She set a clean tally by the hatch. Whatever happened in the room, she intended to know what was owed to her.

---

At the east gate, Bessa had the original toll book open beneath a weighted stone. Rain had softened the corners of the last three pages, and the clerk assigned to copy them was drying a second book by the brazier.

The House Jorvan observer stood where his agreement permitted him to stand. He had been careful about that since his first day. He could see the wheels being counted and the deductions for ordinary gate expenses entered before the surplus was reckoned. He could not take the toll, set the charge, or instruct Bessa's guards.

He recognized Orlen when the factor passed below with an empty mule. He recognized the two haulers later, although one had changed horses. He asked Bessa whether the men were carrying Crown orders.

"Not through this gate," she said.

"Do you know where they have gone?"

"Downhill."

"That is not what I asked."

"It is what I saw."

The observer wrote the passage of the mule and the two carts in his own small book. It was a record of movement, not an accusation. He knew the difference. A creditor's witness who began inventing secret transactions from every empty cart would soon be unwelcome even to his creditor.

A younger gate servant had heard that the king's stone supplier was meeting a Morcant factor in the village. By noon he was telling another porter that the two houses meant to break the royal bargain. Bessa heard him and called him back.

"Which bargain?"

"The stone preference."

"How many loads?"

The boy did not know.

"Twelve accepted Crown-side loads, offered first to Hesta within thirty days," Bessa said. "If you mean to repeat a story, learn what you are saying first."

She did not know what the men intended either. She had no wish to defend their honor without evidence. She had a greater objection to turning a gate into a machine for manufacturing certainty.

The Jorvan observer made no addition to his book. A rumor was not toll revenue.

---

By the time the Kaelmont road clerk arrived at the baker's, the first sheet of prices had already become useless.

Orlen had listed the cost of iron brackets at his workshop. One hauler had written the price of bringing them to the foot of the mountain. The other had priced a full team to the upper yard. None of those figures included the same journey, and the clerk could not compare them without choosing whose inconvenience to ignore.

Her name was Edda Kelm. She worked from the Kaelmont road office two valleys east and had spent the morning inspecting a washed culvert. She arrived with mud on her hem and a cord for measuring wheel width.

"I can give you the lawful charges," she said. "I cannot promise the road will carry what you put on it."

Hesta's brother turned his board around. "The second drain stone was chipped before the mason accepted it."

"Rejected," Orlen said.

"Rejected," the brother agreed. "The first was accepted. The third has not been tested. You may dislike my sister's preference without making it a purchase of twelve stones already delivered."

Edda asked for the length of the blocks and the axle span of the wagons. The first hauler named his team and the number of journeys he could make in dry weather. The second described the wet turn and the labor of unloading a cart to ease it around the bend. Edda marked the costs separately.

Orlen watched her erase the total he had hoped to show Tomas.

"That is a larger price than mine."

"It is a different price," she answered. "Yours stops where the mountain begins."

There was no pleasure in that correction. Edda wanted the Crown to understand the road because she was tired of receiving complaints for damage caused by loads no one had measured. She would not authorize widening on Kaelmont's behalf, and she would not sign for a crossing she had not inspected.

Hesta's brother wanted the preference honored. Orlen wanted the next open order to be priced fairly. The haulers wanted a journey that would not consume the horses before it earned their feed. Each had a good reason to be present, and none could supply what the others needed alone.

They agreed on one narrow thing. Edda would make a measured road note for the Kaelmont office. Orlen would revise his iron quotation to show delivery separately. The haulers would provide written charges for the upper turn. Hesta's brother would take the rejected stone dimensions back to the quarry and ask for the third block to be inspected before anyone counted it.

No contract was signed. The baker brought in more bread, and they paid for it.

---

Yara Fen had come down to the village for a courier, not a conspiracy.

A rider from the League was expected at the post-house with the next Dols correspondence. She had arranged to receive the sealed packet there because the rider's route did not include the castle, and because a messenger could not be expected to climb the mountain merely to satisfy a court's curiosity. She brought the receipt book herself.

The rider arrived wet, tired, and irritated by a loose saddle strap. His seal was intact. Yara paid the ordinary delivery fee, recorded the handover and offered him a hot drink before he set out again.

The packet contained no miracle. Dols remained willing to consider freely authorized evidence, and its road keeper still had not accepted the damaged wall. There was no military countersign, no permission for a second loaded crossing, and no answer that converted Corven's death into an agreed judgment. The correspondence did not contain Jalen's withheld medical observations. Yara had not sent them.

She read it twice before sealing her reply for the next outward rider.

Across the square she saw Orlen leaving the baker's with Edda Kelm. They stood beneath the eaves to keep a drawing dry while Edda pointed toward the upper road. Yara recognized the Morcant factor from the hall's recent accounts and recognized a road clerk's measuring cord. She did not know what they had said in the room.

At the post-house, a woman waiting for a parcel asked whether the castle had begun sending its bargains downhill because the walls could hear too much.

"People have always made bargains downhill," Yara said. "That is where most of the bread is."

She regretted the cleverness as soon as she said it. It would travel better than a careful answer. Still, the woman's question told her something useful: the court's new habit of asking what Edselsto had heard was changing what people assumed privacy meant.

Yara kept her packet closed. Jalen's refusal was not made less binding by the fact that a stranger might find it inconvenient. She had promised him that much, and she intended to be the person who kept the promise.

---

Alden Pell learned of the meeting from a groom who had heard of it from a porter who had heard Bessa correct the gate servant. By the time Alden repeated the story at a late meal, Hesta had supposedly sold her preference to Morcant, Jorvan had taken possession of the tolls, and a foreign secretary had been seen carrying the agreement.

His companions were less impressed than he expected.

"Did you see the agreement?" one asked.

"No. The business was conducted away from the listening ways."

"That is where my father conducts his business," another said. "He has an office in town."

Alden laughed, but he was still sore from Rhyse taking his name at the threshold. He wanted to recover the position of the man who knew what was happening in the castle. A tale about secrets outside its hearing served him better than another account of a boy carrying water.

Tavin passed behind the table with a tray. Alden stopped talking for a moment. He had apologized at the kitchen hatch, and Tavin had accepted the words. Neither knew what the other ought to do with that afterward.

"Is the lower road busy?" Alden asked him.

"I've been carrying plates."

It was a perfectly adequate answer. Alden nearly asked again, then let him go.

Later, Rhyse heard the rumor from a watch officer and requested the names of those who had actually seen anything. The chain ended with Bessa's gate and a post-house rider, neither of which had witnessed a bargain.

"Shall we send a man to the baker?" Dain asked.

"To buy bread?"

"To learn what they are doing."

"If they have broken a law, bring me the law and the evidence. If they have priced iron, let them price iron."

Rhyse did ask Dain to preserve the ordinary gate account. It might matter to a disputed delivery later. He would not turn a lawful road book into a register of political disloyalty.

He also asked whether the loose flag by Iven's threshold had been repaired. It had not. The mason was waiting on a bracket. Rhyse wrote that on the repair order and sent it to Tomas. A court could spend an afternoon inventing a hidden government while a servant still had to step over a broken stone.

---

Luceran had imagined that moving the court to Edselsto would make it easier to know whom to trust. The mountain had given him a smaller circle, a clearer household, and, for a time, the pleasant belief that the walls themselves could keep the rest of the world from surprising him.

Damaris placed Orlen's revised quotation before him beside Hesta's witnessed preference. Tomas had added the first road measurements. Bessa's account was separate. So was Yara's sealed foreign receipt, which she had not offered for general reading.

"Has Hesta complained?" Luceran asked.

"No," Damaris said. "Her brother attended. He has not surrendered her rights, and no one has asked him to."

"Then what have they agreed?"

"To put the price of a cart journey on the same page as the price of iron. It is a surprisingly useful agreement."

Luceran had already heard a version involving foreign influence and secret toll rights. He asked Edselsto whether it knew what had passed at the baker's.

"I did not hear those words," the castle replied.

The answer was neither evasive nor helpful. He could have ordered a messenger to question everyone who had attended. He could have asked who had been seen leaving the room and demanded that each explain why. For a moment the possibilities felt like ordinary diligence.

"Would they have met here if they had wanted my approval?" he asked.

"Not necessarily," Damaris said. "The room is paid for. The road clerk was already below the mountain. And there is no reason a hauler should seek your permission to argue about the price of oats."

He looked at the quotation again. Orlen's first number had been attractive because it was incomplete. The second was less pleasing and much more useful.

"Let Tomas receive the measured prices," Luceran said. "The existing preference stands exactly as witnessed. Anything not covered by it may be offered openly when there is money and a measured need. I will not promise the next work merely to quiet a rumor about this one."

Damaris nodded. "And the road?"

"Let Kaelmont's clerk report to her own office. We can ask for its answer, not borrow her authority."

He wanted to know more. He disliked not knowing more. Those feelings had become dangerous before, and he knew it.

Miren entered while Damaris was gathering the accounts. She had come to ask whether the kitchen's broken kettle had returned from the smith. Luceran told her that people were making bargains in the village without informing him.

"Yes," she said.

"You do not find it troubling?"

"I find it troubling when they cannot. You are a king, Luceran. You are not everybody's host."

It was not forgiveness. They were standing in the same room because they had work there, and because affection could survive in a form that no longer made his wishes decisive. He told her the kettle was still awaiting a new rivet. She thanked him for the answer and went to tell the cook.

When she had gone, Luceran resisted the impulse to ask the walls which way she had walked.

---

The next market afternoon brought no grand declaration. Orlen returned to the baker's with a clean copy of his revised figures. Edda had sent her road note to Kaelmont, not to the king, and had left a witnessed copy with the haulers so that neither could quietly change the measured turn. Hesta's brother brought word that the third drain block would be offered for inspection when the quarry team could move it safely. It was not yet accepted.

The baker charged for the room again.

Orlen protested that they had scarcely used the stove.

"You used the table," she said. "And I had to put my flour somewhere else."

He paid.

A messenger from Tomas arrived with a simple request for the full delivered quotation and a statement that Hesta's preference remained in force. Orlen read it aloud. One hauler looked disappointed that no work had been promised. The other said a refusal to promise what had not been measured was better than a promise that would not pay.

Across the square, two men were discussing Jorvan's claim on the east-gate surplus. One believed the house now owned the gate. The other, whose cousin worked there, said Bessa would throw a man into the horse trough before she surrendered the toll book. Both were wrong in their own ways, and both would repeat the argument.

There were bargains being made outside Edselsto, and there would be more. Some would be generous, some predatory, some frightened, some merely competent. The walls could not hear them all. That did not make them enemies of the Crown.

Above the village, Pell Falbrecht's men inspected a lower service stair after a worker reported a new scrape of stone against stone beneath the old foundations. The men found a narrow settling crack beside a sealed descent, measured it, and barred the approach until a mason could return with a plumb line. They could not say whether the movement was recent or whether the rain had made an old flaw audible. The report went into Vess's ordinary works book, not the royal intelligence account. No one had yet linked it to the nave.

That evening Canon Merel checked the seven ancestral candles as he had done before. Their flames stood steady. He had no cause to announce a marvel.

At the baker's, the last guests left behind a few crumbs, a disputed figure for cartage, and enough coin for the next roof tile. She swept the floor and closed the shutters against the mountain rain.
