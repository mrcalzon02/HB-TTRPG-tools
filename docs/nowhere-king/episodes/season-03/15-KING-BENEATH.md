# Season Three, Episode 15 — King Beneath

The younger copyist had kept the mistake because she was afraid to admit it.

That was how Neris Vell found the reversed impression again, on the morning the Copyists' Hall was to send its evidence back to Edselsto. It lay between two clean sheets in a drying press, the bent enclosing line leaning the wrong way, the crooked hook turned inward where it should have turned out. A notation in Neris's own hand crossed the margin: *Transfer reversed. Not evidence of a second mark.*

“You were told to keep it with the other papers,” Neris said.

“I did keep it.”

“You hid it in the press.”

The girl, Lysa, was old enough to take wages for her copying and young enough to believe a mistake might end her employment. “Brother Odran wanted the right-hand version for his comparison. I thought he would see this one and think I had done the whole commission wrong.”

“He will see it. It is part of what happened.”

Neris opened the press and laid the mistaken sheet beside the two signed direct impressions. They were impressions of the same incised surface behind the upper-gallery linen cupboard. One had remained under Ysabet's castle seal; the other had come to the Hall in its own witnessed packet. Neither was a second ancient inscription, and neither was the work of a different stonecutter. The reversed copy was an error made after the first rubbing, not an ancient variant.

Lysa studied the three sheets. “It looks more like a word this way.”

“That is why it is dangerous.”

Neris took a small knife to the rough edge of the drying board, not to any of the papers. The board had warped in damp weather. She trimmed it so the sheets would lie flat when they were compared.

“You will carry the mistake yourself,” she said. “You will tell the witnesses how you made it. Then we will ask whether the correct sheets are any more legible.”

“Will I be paid for the journey?”

“Yes. The Hall has hired you for the day. The Crown has not.”

Lysa's shoulders eased. She had feared dismissal more than she had feared the work.

Outside, a carter was arguing with the Hall porter over a chest of damp account books. A book could be old and still need someone to carry it out of the rain. Neris wrapped the impressions in oilcloth, set the two seals where they could be seen without breaking them, and went to meet Brother Odran.

He had brought no revelation. He had brought three comparison leaves and a request that nobody call them originals.

---

The north anteroom at Edselsto smelled of clean linen, damp wool and the sour paste used to mend worn cloth.

Mistress Fera was counting sickroom covers when Ysabet arrived with the scholars. Fera had no intention of yielding the room simply because the visitors carried old writing.

“The north table is mine until the last count is entered,” she said. “You may have the little one by the shutter if you do not move the mending.”

Neris accepted. Brother Odran placed his packet on the little table. Lysa remained standing until Fera found her a stool with a sound leg.

Ysabet had arranged the comparison under the half-day of follow-up measurement already permitted after the cupboard discovery. The allowance paid the necessary living watch, the mason's inspection and the measured copying time. It did not pay for a week of lectures, the opening of a sealed stair, or a fresh expedition into the mountain. Damaris had entered the sums beside the unfinished receiving-yard gutter, the works wages still due and the linen reserve Fera was trying to stretch.

“You have until the noon bell for the upper passage,” Ysabet told them. “After that, the guard returns to his ordinary watch. Any further request comes with a named cost.”

Brother Odran nodded. “The Brotherhood pays for my journey and my books. I am not asking the king to purchase a conclusion.”

Elen Quorin arrived carrying the corrected elevation of the northern wall, rolled around a straight piece of timber. Rain had spoiled the earlier measurements. She had since taken them again from fixed points, and the difference mattered. On the first drawing, the hidden recess appeared to extend beyond the wall's plausible depth. On the corrected one, it could be an older face enclosed by later work.

Pell Falbrecht met her at the door. “It *could* fit,” he said. “That is not the same as saying it was always there.”

“I have never said it was.”

“Good. I have enough people telling me what old stone must mean.”

Rhyse came in behind him with the duty roll from the west service room. The watch would escort the scholars to the cupboard, but the room beside the hall would remain a place where living guards ate, dried cloaks and kept the upper-passage roster. Sera Sorelle's petition had not made either room vacant. Nor had the king's refusal given the scholars a claim to them.

Fera finished counting a bundle, tied it and put a finger on the figure in her book. “If the stone has a name, I hope it can mend a sheet.”

“No,” Elen said. “But if we make a false name, someone may order your room cleared to look for more.”

Fera looked up. “Then measure carefully.”

---

The cupboard was opened by the carpenter who had first found the incised mark, not by a scholar eager to be first inside.

Bren lifted the old fixed lath panel only as far as Pell permitted. The guarded recess beyond it remained unentered. Rhyse's men held the passage clear for the necessary linen work, and the lower service stair was still separately barred. Nothing about the morning's scholarship made its steps safe.

Neris held the signed impression against her drawing. Elen set her measured elevation beside it. They did not touch the mark with a new rubbing cloth; the two original direct impressions already preserved its handedness. Pell would not permit fresh charcoal to be worked into the grooves merely to give an argument another sheet of paper.

“Four points,” Elen said.

She indicated the cupboard's fixed hinge scar, a mortar joint beneath the lintel, the edge of the old lath seating and the small offset at the stone's right shoulder. Each was visible without crossing the threshold. Neris named them in the order recorded on the earlier drawing. Lysa checked their order against the Hall's sealed impression without being shown Elen's elevation.

“The second point is lower on my sheet,” Lysa said.

“Because your sheet was laid against a board that bowed,” Neris answered. “How much?”

Lysa held a straightedge against it. “Less than a finger's breadth at the edge.”

“And at the incised line?”

“Less again.”

Elen moved the drawing by that small amount. The apparent interruption of the third stroke no longer sat exactly on the masonry joint. It still appeared interrupted; the correction had not repaired it into a complete letter.

Brother Odran watched from the safe side of Pell's chalk line. “The hook might be a terminal sign.”

“It might be a mason's return,” Pell said. “A man making a boundary mark would turn his tool that way.”

“And the three strokes?”

“Three cuts. Perhaps a count.”

“Then why the enclosing line?”

“Because he wanted a line around them.”

Odran smiled faintly. “You have given me a reading as uncertain as mine.”

“I have given you a reason not to call yours certain.”

Elen asked Lysa to turn the reversed transfer face down. Without its seductive wrong-handed shape, the remaining marks were less like the familiar signs in Odran's first comparison leaf. The resemblance did not vanish, but it became less complete.

Neris recorded that result before anyone proposed a word. She had seen a copyist persuade herself that the best-looking version must be the oldest. The stone had not been obliged to agree.

At the end of the permitted inspection Pell replaced the panel without closing the evidence register. The recess remained guarded and inaccessible. Elen took her elevation away with no claim that the wall was newly grown, and Rhyse sent his second guard back to the west service room at the agreed bell.

The work had produced a correction measured in less than a finger's breadth. It had also removed one of the easiest arguments for a grand translation.

---

Brother Odran's comparison leaves were older than his journey, but not as old as the men who had written the marks he wished to compare.

He laid them on Fera's small table after the noon meal. One was a copy from a boundary charter kept at the Brotherhood's house, in which a bent line marked a jurisdiction *under* another jurisdiction. One was a later teaching leaf whose marginal hand placed a crown-like sign beside a phrase that might mean a ruler *below*, a ruler *subject to*, or a ruler *in the lower place*. The third was a fair copy of an index made by a previous brother, listing where such signs had appeared in other books.

The index was useful. It was not a fourth witness.

Neris asked him to say that aloud while Ysabet wrote.

“The index repeats the teaching leaf,” Odran said. “Its maker gives no independent source for the phrase. I cannot count the same copied gloss twice.”

Lysa, who had been ready to admire the neat index, looked disappointed. Odran saw her face.

“A fine copy may save a thought,” he told her. “It cannot give that thought a second birthplace.”

Canon Merel came from the family nave carrying a different book: an old order of commemorations in which the words for a dead ruler and a living ruler were distinguished by where the scribe placed a mark. He had not brought it to prove that the cupboard figure was a memorial. He had brought it because Odran's proposed downward sign could, in some hands, mark the dead rather than a place beneath a floor.

“The living king below the crown,” Merel said, touching the first leaf, “might mean a vassal. The king beneath the earth might mean a burial. The king beneath judgment might mean an oath. You are taking the most alarming sense because we have a barred stair.”

“The stair is not in my comparison,” Odran replied.

“It is in every listener's mind.”

Neris separated the sheets into three piles: the observed cupboard mark, the dated or attributed reference leaves, and the unsigned lower-return sketch that Ysabet had recovered from an old catalogue. The sketch had once been annotated *lower return, uncertain place*. A later hand had guessed at a location beneath the old buttery. Neither hand had named a witness, and no one had inspected an original wall for it.

Odran asked to see it, then left it in its own pile.

“The bent enclosure is similar,” he said.

“On an unsigned drawing of an unlocated surface,” Ysabet answered.

“Precisely. I would use it to choose where to look in an archive, not to claim where the stone is.”

Merel turned to the teaching leaf. “Why do you read the crown-like sign as a king rather than a lord?”

“Because in the charter's related hand the larger stroke denotes sovereign rank.”

“That is a different hand.”

“It is. And the sign may have shifted.”

“Then you cannot simply join them.”

“I cannot simply join them,” Odran agreed. “But I can ask whether a scribe of that family of hands would have joined them.”

Neris proposed a test that did not require a new wall or a fresh miracle. Each reader would mark, on a separate slip, which strokes of the cupboard impression corresponded to which strokes on the reference leaves, and which correspondences failed. They would do so before hearing the others' proposed words. Lysa would keep the slips folded, and Ysabet would open them with the witnesses present.

Pell, who had returned to collect his measure, said he would mark what he believed to be tool returns and ordinary cutting habits. Elen would mark what could survive a change of viewing angle. Merel would mark liturgical usages he could attest from his own book. Odran would mark the grammar of his copied signs. Neris would mark the forms, the copy errors and the places where one source merely repeated another.

It took longer than a confident pronouncement would have taken. The room was quieter for it.

When the slips were opened, three readers agreed that the crooked hook belonged to the same incised composition as the bent line. None agreed that it proved a word. Elen and Neris agreed that the third short stroke could not be restored from the reversed transfer; Pell and Odran disagreed about whether its interruption was intentional. Merel found a plausible funerary use for the downward form. Odran found a plausible territorial one. Neris found no secure sign corresponding to a complete personal name.

Lysa set the slips in their separate piles. “Then what have we learned?”

“That the wrong sheet was doing more work than the right one,” Elen said.

“And that some disagreements can be written precisely enough to survive us,” Merel answered.

---

Luceran heard the proposed phrase before he saw the record.

He was in the smaller accounts chamber with Damaris, listening to a report on the receiving-yard gutter. The last section still admitted water, and a workman's interrupted morning had not been erased by the festival sponsors' promise to consider the claim. Damaris was explaining why the next paid length of gutter could not be replaced by the same men who were already owed for other work.

Ysabet entered with the sealed scholarly slips.

“Brother Odran has a provisional phrase,” she said. “He asks that it be entered under his name, with the objections beside it.”

Luceran put down the gutter account. “What phrase?”

“King Beneath.”

For a moment he imagined a name engraved below the castle, a buried sovereign from his ancestors' time, an answer to the dark candle. The words were so well suited to the mountain that he nearly mistook their fitness for evidence.

“Has he read it from the stone?”

“No. He has proposed it by comparing the mark with several copied usages. The sign for king is disputed. The downward relation is disputed. The two direct impressions come from one wall. The old lower sketch remains without provenance.”

“Why call it that, then?”

“Because scholarship needs names for proposals it means to test,” Ysabet said. “A proposal is not a royal proclamation.”

Damaris closed the gutter book but kept a finger between its pages. “If this becomes a public discovery, I will have petitioners asking for guards, ladders, masons, candles, copyists and a new stair before I have paid the men fixing the roof.”

Luceran wanted to say that an ancestral inscription was more important than a leaking gutter. He could hear the argument forming and knew how easily he could make it by ordering somebody else to bear the cost.

“Can the scholars disagree in the record?” he asked.

“They have already done so.”

“Then let me hear the disagreement.”

Miren was in the corridor when he left the chamber. She had come to speak with Fera about sickroom linen and had seen the scholars pass with their bundles.

“I heard a title,” she said. “Not an answer.”

“I am going to hear why they use it.”

“You were very quick to stand when Ysabet spoke.”

“Would you have stayed seated?”

“No. I would have wanted to know too.”

Her candor made it harder for him to defend himself against an accusation she had not made.

“The last time I wanted to know something badly enough,” he said, “I made the castle tell me what it had heard of you.”

“And you learned words you had no right to demand.”

“I have not done that today.”

“I know. That matters. It does not make this room a place where you may purchase certainty by giving the scholars more money than they have asked for.”

He looked at her. “Do you think I am doing that?”

“I think you have not yet decided.”

She did not take his arm. She walked with him as far as the anteroom, then turned toward Fera's work. The physically disconnected withdrawing room remained hers to use without a silver listening branch. She had not forgiven the stolen conversation, the murder of Corven or the false denial that followed it. Her willingness to speak plainly was not an invitation to treat the old injuries as finished.

Luceran entered the scholarly hearing without her.

---

Brother Odran did not stand as if delivering an oracle. He stood like a man prepared to have his reading crossed out.

“The phrase I propose,” he told the king, “is *King Beneath*. It is not a translation I can defend as certain. It is the shortest Englishing of one possible joining of a sovereign sign and a downward relation in our comparison leaves.”

Merel objected to the joining. “Your sovereign sign may be a lesser lord, a dead king, or the Crown as an office. Your downward relation may describe submission rather than place. The stone gives us neither a complete verb nor a witness who can speak for its maker.”

Elen objected to the setting. “The corrected elevation allows an older face to have been covered by the cupboard work. It does not place that face below anything except the current gallery floor, and it does not place it in the barred stair.”

Pell objected to the carving. “I have seen marks that tell masons where a load returns. The hook may be nothing more exalted than that. The third stroke may have been lost when the edge was chipped, or it may have ended where we see it.”

Neris objected to the witnesses. “We have two careful direct impressions, but only one original surface. We have one reversed mistake, not a rival ancient spelling. We have comparison books with some known copy relationships, not a second discovered inscription. The lower-return sketch is an inquiry lead without a certified place.”

Odran heard them all without changing his proposed phrase.

“I do not claim they are wrong,” he said. “I claim that if a reader must begin somewhere, this reading has a form that can be tested against future, independently located writing. It may fail. A failure would be knowledge.”

Luceran asked whether the words might refer to a person.

“They might,” Odran said.

“A king?”

“They might refer to a king. Or to an office, a burial, a place below a ruler, a subordinate ruler, or a ritual station. I will not pretend the marks have chosen among them.”

“Could it be the reason the first candle died?”

Merel answered before Odran. “We have a candle that went dark and a mark behind a cupboard. Neither has testified to the other.”

There was no thunder in the room. A laundress carried a bundle past the door, and one of Rhyse's men moved aside to let her through. The ordinary sound made the question feel less like prophecy and more like a dangerous instruction waiting for someone to mistake it for proof.

Luceran turned to Ysabet. “How will you enter this?”

“Under the heading *Restricted comparative readings of the upper-gallery mark*. Odran's proposal will be named as his. The contrary readings will be named as theirs. Neris's custody account and Elen's elevation will remain attached but distinct. The old sketch will be described as uncertified. No copy will go to a herald or a public reader without a separate order and the reasons for it.”

“Will the king's seal appear?”

“On the order permitting the record to be kept and the existing access restrictions to stand. Not on the truth of the phrase.”

Luceran considered the distinction. He had once been relieved to find that Edselsto could answer questions faster than living people. Here five living people had spent half a day making an answer less convenient and more honest.

“Do that,” he said. “And pay the hours already authorized. No further opening of the recess. No new stair work on this reading alone.”

Damaris, who had followed him in, asked that the paid half-day and ordinary watch charges be entered against their proper accounts before the scholars departed. She would not make them disappear inside the east-gate receipts pledged under Othra Jorvan's twenty-four-mark advance and twenty-seven-mark repayment cap. Pell signed for the measured time. Lysa signed for the Hall's hire, not the Crown's. Odran's order would bear the cost of his travel.

The royal seal went onto a narrow instruction about custody and access. It did not go onto a claim that there was a king beneath the castle.

---

By evening, the restricted record had three parts that no one could honestly collapse into one.

There was the physical account: a guarded recess behind a cupboard, an incised bent line, three short strokes, a crooked hook, two sealed impressions of one surface, a corrected elevation, four checkable reference points, and a copy error preserved as an error. There was the comparative account: Odran's *King Beneath*, Merel's competing funerary and oath usages, Pell's mason's explanation, Elen's warning about placement, and Neris's list of where the sources depended upon one another. There was the unfinished account: the anonymous lower-return sketch, the unentered recess, the separately barred lower stair and all the questions nobody had yet earned the right to answer.

Neris asked Lysa to read the headings aloud before they sealed the Hall's working copy.

“Does that mean I am forgiven?” Lysa asked when she reached the reversed sheet.

“It means your mistake has been accounted for,” Neris said. “You still owe me better work tomorrow.”

Lysa grinned. “That is worse.”

“It is employment.”

At the village post-house, Edda Kelm found no reply from Kaelmont to her disclosed conflict and request for recusal. She would not pretend silence was permission to judge Orlen Morcant's higher, unaccepted iron quotation. Orlen had circulated corrected hauler charges; Hesta Vale still had one accepted drain block, one failed block and a third uninspected, along with her bounded first refusal and older Crown-track toll right. None of those accounts could be settled by a newly fashionable phrase.

Sera Sorelle, meanwhile, received the chamberlain's notice that equal hall-date notices were being prepared for interested houses. It named no available evening. She folded it beside the king's refusal and the unpaid remainder of the awning-rope account. She had not withdrawn her anger simply because the court had found a way to write a fair notice.

In the receiving yard the gutter still leaked. Six grain sacks remained north and six at Edselsto, with none newly delivered to the hungry posts. The separate rights of Dols, Merehaven and Teth had not merged into a royal permission. Eighteen patrol mounts had not returned. The third great-hall hearth remained cold. The workers' wages, the watch's duties and the family's private wounds were not diminished by a scholar's new title.

Canon Merel entered the family nave after the evening service. He had been arguing all day about what marks could mean. Here he did not argue. He counted the ancestral candles and wrote down what his eyes could attest.

The first was dark, its wick untouched. Six were burning.

He closed the book and left the record of the phrase where it belonged: among the scholars, under seal, with room for someone to prove them wrong.
