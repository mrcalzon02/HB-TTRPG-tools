# Season Three, Episode 18 — Default Intelligence

Pell Falbrecht had drawn the same curve four times, and each time the rain found a different way to cross it.

The receiving-yard drain was a shallow cut in a long table of boards. A cup of water poured at its upper end should have followed the channel into a waiting bowl. Instead it collected at the join where the lower stone course would meet the roof gutter, then crept across the table and wet the accounts underneath. Pell snatched the papers away.

"That is the trouble with a model," he told his apprentice. "It behaves like a model until it behaves like a roof."

A thin wooden gauge, cut to the angle of the original receiving-yard turn, ought to have been in his tool chest. Without it, he would have to measure the turn again in the yard, and the men there were already owed wages for other work. He had looked in the chest twice. He had not accused anyone of taking it.

Luceran entered through the connected east works passage with Ysabet and a page carrying the morning letters. Pell was about to explain the missing gauge when the king looked up at the ceiling.

"Edselsto. Do you remember where they put the wooden pattern for this drain?"

Pell's apprentice stopped with the water cup in his hand.

The castle answered from the stone over the passage, not from inside Pell's mind.

"I heard someone say that a narrow wooden thing had been placed behind the third upright of the measuring-room shelves. The voice may have been Master Pell's."

"Which day?" Ysabet asked.

"I do not know. There were men working in that passage when I heard it."

"Was the passage connected then?"

"Yes."

Ysabet turned the survey book toward the king. Vess's dated mark showed that the east measuring room had been on the restored listening way before the most recent roof work. The answer was possible. It was not yet a finding.

Pell put down his knife. "The third upright has a space behind it. I used to keep the older templates there, before we moved the chest."

He went himself. The apprentice followed with a lantern, and Luceran followed because he could not bear to wait. They returned with a thin curved strip of wood, dusty along one edge and bearing Pell's own knife nick at the narrow end.

"That is mine," Pell said. "And I am grateful to have it."

Luceran smiled before he could stop himself.

"How much time have we saved?"

"Perhaps a man's measuring hour, if the timber has not moved. The gauge will still need checking against the actual stones. It is not a drain, Your Grace."

The king looked at the strip. He had asked a question without sending a runner to the shelves, without interrupting a worker's meal, without waiting for a clerk to search the old daybook. The answer had been useful.

Pell returned to his wet model. He had been trying to tell the king that the lower-course joint still needed a new funded order. Luceran had already begun thinking of another question.

---

In the kitchen passage, Tavin carried two empty linen baskets past the black ear notice fixed to the south-gallery turn. The sign marked where the silver listening way ended. He knew the boundary because he had helped carry water to the workers who cut the junction, and because people now sometimes asked him whether a room was safe to speak in.

Fera met him at the pantry door.

"Did you find the two covers?"

"Not on the upper shelf."

"Did you look under the clean aprons?"

"Yes."

She did not ask Edselsto. She asked the laundress, who had folded the covers after the last wash and was eating bread at the back table. The laundress remembered that one had a torn corner. Fera remembered lending it to a fever patient after a night of chills. Tavin remembered carrying the other to the narrow sickroom when the roof dripped. Together they accounted for both. Neither was in the clean store, and neither was stolen.

A junior household clerk, standing by the pantry with a wax tablet, said, "His Grace has begun asking the house for lost things. We could save ourselves some walking."

Fera took the tablet from him and looked at the line he had already scratched: *two covers missing*.

"They are not missing," she said. "They are in use. If you tell the steward they are missing, he may send someone to hunt for them and frighten a sick woman out of her bed."

"The castle might have heard where they went."

"It might have heard a basket carried down a connected corridor. It would not have felt the fever or decided that a patient needed warmth. Put the tablet away until you have asked the people who handled the cloth."

The clerk reddened. He had been trying to make himself useful to a court that now admired quick answers. Tavin knew that feeling.

"Shall I bring the torn one for mending when the patient is well?" he asked.

"When the patient is well and can spare it," Fera answered. "Until then, find me the price of two more. Do not take them out of somebody else's bed."

The laundress finished her bread. She had a child to collect from the baker's wife before dusk, and she would not stay late because a clerk preferred a supernatural inventory. Fera let her go on time.

The clerk rubbed the words from his wax tablet. He wrote instead: *Two covers accounted for in sickroom use. Fresh purchase not yet funded.*

Tavin took the empty baskets back to the wash, pleased that a truthful answer could still require several people.

---

At the morning council Luceran had Pell's recovered gauge laid beside the account books.

He had not meant to make a display of it. The little strip of wood looked so modest on the table that it invited a story about how easily the castle had found it. By the time Damaris arrived, the page had already told two clerks that Edselsto had saved the drain.

"It found a pattern," Damaris said, after Pell corrected the tale. "We have not bought Hesta's stone."

"Hesta's third block is sound for the lower course."

"Conditionally suitable. It is hers. She has offered to hold it for two market days without storage charge. The hold is running, and I have no written funded order for it."

"Then the pattern may help us decide."

"It may help Pell measure. It does not pay the carter, the setter, the quarry or the men whose roof wages are already overdue."

Luceran accepted the correction aloud. Then he asked the ceiling whether the east-gate receipts had improved enough to free a little money.

"I have heard accounts read in the connected chamber," Edselsto said. "I have heard the number twenty-four and the number twenty-seven. I cannot tell you what Bessa has counted at the gate today."

The house-paid Jorvan observer, present only to witness the morning figures brought from the toll house, shifted in his chair.

"Perhaps the house could remember yesterday's total," he suggested.

"Bessa holds the original," Damaris said. "You witness what she enters. Neither you nor Edselsto collects it. And the east-gate net is pledged under a separate agreement. Even an excellent total would not make it unpledged money."

Luceran looked down at the books. The twenty-four-mark advance and twenty-seven-mark repayment cap remained exactly where they had been. The observer was not authorized to turn a remembered number into a royal appropriation.

"Ask Bessa for the signed tally when it is ready," Luceran said.

"I have," Ysabet answered. "The runner has gone."

He disliked the waiting more now that he had experienced the recovered gauge. A few moments earlier the castle had saved him the trouble of finding a man. The absence of a voice from the gate felt like a defect in the morning.

Pell took the gauge back before a clerk could mistake it for an order. Damaris had Tomas enter the actual hour saved, once the fit was checked, rather than an imagined saving against the entire roof. A partial wage disbursement had already been made; the remainder was still due. The leaky gutter remained above them, indifferent to the court's delight.

Rhyse was changing the lower watch when a metallic scrape sounded through the connected approach.

Edselsto spoke promptly: "A blade has been drawn near the west passage."

Two Doorwardens moved toward the sound. Oren Bale, his wounded hand still stiff in the cold, followed more slowly with the watch book. The source was a carpenter trimming a door pin with a short saw. He had unwrapped it from cloth because the pin would not fit its socket. The work order lay on the bench beside him.

The carpenter looked from the guards to the saw.

"Am I under arrest for a hinge?"

"No," Rhyse said. "You are being asked to show the tool. You have done so."

Oren wrote the warning and the inspection, naming the carpenter and the lawful work. The blade alarm was a report of sound, not a verdict about intent. The carpenter returned to the pin, though he worked with a different expression while two armed men stood watching.

Luceran arrived in time to hear the explanation.

"That was quick," he said.

"So was the challenge," Rhyse replied. "And the release must be quick too. A harmless man should not pay for the time it takes us to feel certain."

"Would you rather it had said nothing?"

"I would rather it say exactly what it heard. That is the rule we made after the knife."

Oren closed the book and flexed his fingers. He had not forgotten the blade that cut him, nor the pay that his wife Lysa had had to demand while he recovered. The castle's warning had once helped save the king's life. It had not paid a household.

Luceran asked whether Edselsto could tell how many men were on the west service watch.

"I heard three voices at the last bell," the castle said. "I do not know whether all three remained."

Rhyse laid the living roll on the table. One of the three had gone to relieve the outer gate; another was a messenger who had spoken there but never held a watch. Two named guards now occupied the west room, with their relief due at the next bell.

"Ask me for a count," Rhyse said. "Or ask the corporal who made it. The house can tell you what passed through a connected place. It cannot muster a man by hearing his voice."

Luceran looked at Oren's careful entry. The castle's sentence was at the top. The carpenter's answer and the guards' finding occupied most of the page.

He wondered how many pages might be saved if the first sentence were usually right.

Senn Daro was copying public hall notices when Sera Sorelle came to inspect the board where possible dates would eventually be posted.

She had brought a small purse and a folded receipt. One partial payment against the awning-rope assessment had been made; the remaining sponsor balance and the separate worker claim had not vanished. Her musicians wanted to know whether there would be a hall night before winter closed the upper road. Sera wanted to tell them something more useful than *perhaps*.

A courtier beside the board said the king could ask Edselsto whether a hall date had been promised. It had heard the earlier discussion, surely.

Sera looked at him. "If you can get a date out of a stone, you may hire the players yourself."

The courtier laughed, uncertain whether he was being insulted.

Senn had copied the equal possible notices. He knew that no date had been booked. He also knew the danger of turning a preliminary sheet into something saleable: his own attempt to sell a treasury inventory had cost him sealed access and a week's wages. His sister's apprenticeship had survived the punishment, but he had not forgotten the difference between a document someone wanted and a document someone was entitled to have.

"His Grace may ask what words were spoken," Senn said. "The board cannot post a memory as a booking."

Sera unfolded the receipt. "Then write that there is no date, and write it where the musicians can read it. I have already had to explain the rope money three times."

Senn asked Ysabet's permission before adding a plain notice beneath the unbooked alternatives. It stated that the hall dates remained possible, equal and unassigned; no performance or precedence was granted by the partial rope payment. Sera read it twice. She disliked it, but she could carry it to the musicians without deceiving them.

Later Luceran came through the connected hall gallery and asked Edselsto whether he had once said that Sera should have the next supper.

"I heard you speak of making a place for music," Edselsto replied. "I cannot distinguish a wish from an appointment unless the words distinguish them."

Luceran remembered the conversation. He had been lonely, and Sera had been angry. He had wanted to repair a friendship by offering a room that other people would have to heat, clean and pay to keep dry.

Sera held up the notice.

"Your Grace, I do not need the house to remember that you wanted music. I need you to decide whether you can afford it, and then let me say yes or no."

He did not appoint a date. He did not ask her to forget the unpaid rope balance. She left with a truthful notice and the difficult task of telling her players that hope was not a wage.

Edda Kelm had waited at the post-house long enough to know the sound of every cart that stopped outside.

The clerk brought her two ordinary packets and no Kaelmont answer to her disclosed attachment and request for recusal. Orlen Morcant's revised iron quotation remained higher than the first and unaccepted. His corrected hauler charges were in circulation, not a purchase.

Edda signed for the packets she was authorized to receive and did not ask the clerk to open another person's bag in search of a reply.

"I am beginning to think the road eats letters," she said.

"It eats wheels," the clerk answered. "Letters mostly wait for the next horse."

She smiled. She could have stayed at Edselsto, watching for an answer from the connected galleries, where people would tell her that the house remembered every footfall. Instead she had come to the post-house herself. She wanted the silence to be an actual absence of reply, not a guess borrowed from stone.

Orlen was waiting outside with a cartman who had come to dispute the charge for a return journey. The corrected sheet already accounted for that error; Orlen showed him the two columns and let him take a copy to the other haulers. Edda listened without adjudicating the Crown's price.

"Any news?" Orlen asked after the cartman left.

"None from Kaelmont."

"Will you still meet me at the baker's room?"

"Not to choose iron for the Crown."

"I know."

She took his offered hand briefly, in the public road where the post-house clerk could see them. Neither pretended that a private attachment had solved the institutional conflict. The clerk returned to his letters. He had no reason to report their touch to the castle, and the castle had no listening way here.

Miren was mending a sleeve in the south-gallery withdrawing room when Luceran knocked.

She invited him in after setting her needle safely into the cloth. The silver junction outside remained cut. The room was physically deaf to Edselsto, but it was not empty of memory. Luceran had once used the house's hearing to take words she had not given him, and a disconnected wall did not make that choice disappear.

"You found a piece of wood," she said.

"I did."

"Three people have told me that the castle repaired the drain."

"It did not. Pell found the gauge after Edselsto remembered where it might be."

"That is a different story."

"I know. I have been correcting it all morning."

She turned the sleeve right side out. "Have you?"

He sat without asking the house for a chair. It was an absurd thought, but he had nearly looked up when he entered.

"It answered before Pell had finished explaining," he said. "And for a moment I thought how much easier everything might be if I could ask first."

"Everything?"

"Lost tools. Old words. Who was in a passage. Where a clerk left a packet."

"Whose packet?"

He heard the question beneath the question.

"I have not asked it to hear this room."

"You have not asked it today. That is not the same as my trusting you with the next day."

He could not argue. He had no new evidence that would make her wrong.

"I wanted to tell you because I can feel the habit beginning," he said. "The waiting for a messenger seems slower after one useful answer."

Miren folded the sleeve and laid it beside the others.

"I know a woman who can tell you where every garment in this household has been," she said. "She is the laundress. When she is ill, you will discover that you have relied on her for years without learning her name."

"I know her name."

"Then learn when she needs to leave for her child. You will learn more about the house that way than by asking a ceiling where her baskets went."

He smiled, but she did not smile back immediately.

"You want the castle to spare you the small inconvenience of needing people," she said. "And sometimes it can. The danger is that you will stop noticing when the inconvenience is a person saying something you do not wish to hear."

He reached toward her hand, then stopped. She saw the movement and did not offer her own.

"The village path is still there," he said. "Maera has the weather decision. I have not hired a horse or escort. I have not put your name down."

"Good."

"Would you like to walk to the cart turn with me when the weather allows?"

"I will decide when I know what else I must do that day."

He accepted the answer without making it a promise. She returned to the sleeve. Their intimacy did not resume because he had confessed a temptation; she had her own hours to spend.

Outside the disconnected room, a page waited with a question about the hall board. Luceran could have asked the castle what Sera wanted. Instead he sent the page to Sera.

He felt virtuous for doing it, and disliked himself a little for needing to feel virtuous.

---

Bessa's signed east-gate tally arrived after the midday meal, damp at one corner where the runner had sheltered it beneath his cloak.

The Jorvan observer's witnessing mark was beside the figures, not above Bessa's name. He had seen the count. He had not collected the toll or directed the carts. The net was still subject to the twenty-four-mark advance and twenty-seven-mark cap under the agreed market-day terms. No line in the tally released it for the roof.

Ysabet set the paper before Luceran in the connected map gallery. He asked Edselsto whether it remembered the same number from an earlier reading.

"I remember a clerk saying that six sacks remained at Edselsto," the castle answered. "I cannot tell whether that is the number you seek."

"It is not," Ysabet said.

The six sacks at Edselsto and six already north belonged to a separate grain account. The castle had offered a true fragment from a different question. It had not lied; it had not supplied the requested toll figure either.

Luceran looked at the wet tally. Bessa had sent him an answer with a witness, a source and a route of custody. It was less graceful than the castle's voice and more useful for a lawful payment.

"Enter the gate total from this," he said.

Ysabet waited until the clerk had written it. "And the castle's answer?"

"Do not enter it as a gate figure."

"Shall we record that it was asked?"

He hesitated. The temptation was not to erase an error but to avoid leaving evidence of how readily he had made one.

"Yes. Put the question and the answer in the inquiry book. It may help us remember which questions it cannot answer."

The clerk wrote the two lines. He was the same young man Fera had corrected in the kitchen. This time he marked the castle's words as *heard recollection, wrong account*, and copied Bessa's tally as *witnessed current receipt*. The distinction cost him more ink than a single confident number.

At the next bell, Pell returned the wooden gauge to its chest after checking it against the actual receiving-yard turn. It had saved a little labor. The drain still required a purchased lower-course stone and paid setting; Hesta's two-market-day hold was not extended by the discovery. Damaris's wage roll still carried unpaid names. Rhyse's guarded recess and barred lower stair remained closed. No new silver branch had been laid.

Luceran passed the map-gallery arch on his way to the family nave.

"Edselsto," he said, without thinking, "has Merel finished his candle count?"

"I heard his steps in the connected approach. I cannot see the flames from here."

Luceran stopped. He had asked before he had even reached the door.

Inside the nave, Merel was finishing his own count. The first ancestral candle remained dark and untouched. Six burned. He told the king so because he had looked, not because the house had remembered a footfall.

Luceran thanked him. He did not ask for an explanation of the dark wick or a name for the disputed words *King Beneath*. The upper recess remained guarded, the lower stair barred, and neither had yielded a new answer.

That evening, in the small book where he kept questions for the next council, he wrote beside the receiving-yard entry: *Ask Edselsto whether it remembers the work.*

He considered the sentence and added beneath it: *Then ask Pell what is there now.*

The second line was a safeguard. The first had come more easily.

In the east measuring room the gauge hung on its proper hook. Pell's apprentice could find it without asking anyone. The king had not noticed that part of the day's improvement, although it was the part most likely to last.
