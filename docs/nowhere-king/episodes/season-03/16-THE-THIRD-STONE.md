# Season Three, Episode 16 — The Third Stone

Hesta Vale had turned the third drain block over before the sun reached the quarry face.

She did not do it herself. Lenn and two hired men raised it with a timber lever, rolled it against a bed of straw and lowered it onto its other side while she watched the corners. The stone was nearly the size of a winter chest, and its dressed face showed the pale parallel scratches of her brother's iron. One of the men wanted to hurry. The morning was wet and he had another cart to load.

"If you chip it," Hesta said, "you may hurry all the way home without your day's pay."

He set the lever down carefully.

The first trial block had been accepted. The second had failed. The third had waited beneath an awning through the festival and the court's argument about rooms and musicians, as if a king's attention might change its grain. Hesta had paid for the quarrymen's time, the cutting iron and the straw beneath it. Waiting had a price even when nobody entered it in a Crown book.

Lenn wiped the rain from a shallow line on the lower edge. "Pell will see that."

"Then he will see it. I won't turn the mark toward the ground."

"It may be nothing."

"That is what he is paid to decide. Not what you are paid to hide."

The stone had been shaped for a drain face at Edselsto's receiving yard. The old gutter above that yard still admitted water. The stone would not mend the gutter by itself, and Hesta was tired of hearing a useful piece of rock spoken of as if it could repair an entire roof.

She had a written first refusal for Crown-side drain stone during a limited term: thirty days, or twelve accepted loads, under its actual conditions. She also held four seasons of toll-free use of the Crown track for her family's quarry hauling, subject to military closure. Neither right obliged the Crown to buy a bad block. Neither entitled an officer to take a sound one without paying.

A boy came up the path with the castle's seal on a narrow folded letter. Hesta let him warm his hands at the quarry brazier before she opened it.

Pell Falbrecht and the mason's measure would arrive at the second bell. The Crown would pay the previously agreed trial inspection, with the day entered against works, not against east-gate tolls. No purchase was promised. The letter was signed by Ysabet.

Lenn read it over her shoulder. "They've learned to write the disappointment first."

"They've learned I read the whole letter."

Hesta sent the boy back with an acknowledgment and told the men to leave the block exactly where it stood. If the Crown wanted a verdict, it could come and look at the same stone she had been looking at.

---

The receiving-yard gutter poured onto a plank that was supposed to keep the water away from the door.

It did not. A worker had cut a shallow channel beside the plank to stop the overflow reaching the stacked lime, and the channel carried the water directly toward the place where the next cart would stand. Pell Falbrecht stepped across it with his measuring rod and cursed without raising his voice.

Tomas, who had spent part of the festival repairing damage caused by benches set too soon, was waiting beside the yard account. His lost morning was still written there. He had not been paid twice merely because the court had agreed that a sponsor should answer for the interruption.

"We can lift the plank again," Tomas said.

"And leave the water to find the mortar beneath it," Pell answered. "No. The drain face must be true before we set anything above it."

Damaris had come to see the work before the quarry inspection. She carried a narrow book and no promise of new money.

"How many men must leave this yard to inspect a stone?" she asked.

"Pell and one mason. The quarry supplies its own lifting hands. I can stay."

"Then you stay. The roof workers are owed their hours whether the stone passes or fails."

Tomas looked at the book. "Are those hours still in it?"

"They are. A number in a book is not a coin in your hand."

He seemed to appreciate that she had not attempted to console him.

Rhyse passed through the yard on his way to the upper-gallery watch. The cupboard recess remained guarded, the lower service stair separately barred. His men could not be borrowed to lift quarry stone because someone had discovered an alarming phrase in a wall. He offered Pell a pair of spare wooden wedges from the ordinary works store and refused a request for two guards to accompany the inspection.

"A mason can tell whether a stone is sound," he said. "A soldier with a spear cannot improve the answer."

Damaris entered the wedges against works stock. Nothing about the morning would be charged to the net east-gate surplus pledged under Othra Jorvan's twenty-four-mark advance and twenty-seven-mark capped repayment. The house-paid observer at that gate had a right to witness tallies, not to finance repairs or command the watch.

At the yard door Luceran found them studying the water.

"Will the third stone finish it?" he asked.

Pell looked up. "It may help finish the drain face. It will not finish the gutter. Nor will it pay Tomas."

Luceran had come prepared to hear a measurement. Instead he heard three separate unfinished things.

"Go and measure it," he said. "And tell me which of the three I can actually put right."

---

Hesta's men did not straighten their backs when Pell arrived. They were working, and the king was not there.

Pell had brought a square, a plumb line, a hammer small enough to hear a stone rather than break it, and the mason named Berenne who had taken the original receiving-yard dimensions. Berenne was a woman with a scar across the heel of one hand and a habit of writing the figures herself before allowing anyone to repeat them.

She measured the block twice. The first measure agreed with the quarry's mark. The second found the dressed face a little too proud along one edge.

Lenn folded his arms. "The bed will take that."

"The bed is not a place to hide bad cutting," Berenne said.

"It is a place to level a stone."

"Not this much of one."

Hesta let them argue until they began naming the same corner. Then she asked Pell to show her the point at which the block would be refused. He drew a short chalk line along the offending edge.

"That much must come away. If the sound changes beneath it, the block fails. If the face remains sound, I will mark it suitable for a drain face. Suitable is not purchased."

Lenn wanted to cut it himself. Hesta gave him the chisel and made him wait until Berenne had written the original dimensions. The quarry's loss would be measured as carefully as the Crown's possible gain.

The first chips came away clean. At the last finger's breadth a narrow pale seam appeared and ran inward toward the corner.

Lenn stopped.

Pell bent to look. Berenne wetted the seam and watched the water darken one side of it before the other. It was not a crack that divided the whole block, but it was not the harmless surface scratch Lenn had hoped.

"How much more?" Hesta asked.

"Enough that it may no longer fit the face we measured," Berenne said.

Lenn put the chisel down. "Then the block is lost."

"Not yet," Pell said. "We know what is wrong. That is different."

They set a straightedge against the sound inner face and measured the remaining depth. Berenne compared it with the corrected yard drawing, taking care to distinguish the drain face from the gutter above it. The sound portion was still broad enough for one lower course, but not for the higher place originally intended. It would need a separate cap and a different order of setting. More work, not a miracle.

Pell made his judgment in front of Hesta and both hired men. "The third block passes as a sound lower-course stone after this dressing. It does not pass for the upper face we first asked for. I will not call the lower course complete until the adjoining pieces are measured and laid. No load has been delivered or accepted under the first-refusal count."

Berenne wrote the result and made Lenn look at the new figures before he put his mark beside them. Hesta kept a copy. She had lost stone and labor, but she had not lost the whole block.

"Will you buy it?" she asked Pell.

"That is not my office."

"Then your office has finished its work. Mine has not."

She covered the block against the next shower. The inspection had produced a usable stone and a more expensive repair than anyone had wanted.

---

Edda Kelm had gone to the post-house for a letter that did not come.

The keeper showed her the day's Kaelmont packet before it was opened for ordinary delivery. Her office mark was not among the addressed seals. He would not promise a second packet by evening.

"You have asked me three times this week," he said.

"And you have answered me three times without pretending to be Kaelmont. I am grateful."

He gave her a place near the fire to dry her cuffs. Orlen Morcant arrived with two haulers and the corrected charges he had already circulated. He was not surprised to find Edda there. That did not mean he had arranged it.

"The quarry inspection?" he asked.

"Underway. I have no report."

"Nor have I. My iron quote is still in Damaris's hands."

"Your higher iron quote," she said.

"That is the one."

One hauler wanted Edda to say whether Orlen's revised rate would make the Crown's road work cheaper than Hesta's stone. She refused the comparison. She had disclosed her personal attachment to Orlen and requested recusal from Kaelmont's relevant official work. Until her office answered, she would not turn an unanswered request into permission to recommend his goods.

"I can tell you what the post-house charged for this room," she said. "I cannot tell you what a foreign office has decided by being silent."

The hauler laughed, then stopped when he saw she meant it.

Orlen asked the keeper for a small table. He and the haulers laid out their distances, waiting hours and cart capacity without asking Edda to certify any of it. She watched long enough to notice one charge had been entered for a return journey that the driver had made with a paying load.

"That line is yours to explain," she told Orlen, and went back to the fire.

He crossed it out after the driver confirmed the journey. Later, when the others had gone, he brought her the corrected sheet.

"You could have let me discover that tomorrow."

"I could have. You would have lost the evening telling everyone you were right."

"I was right about the iron."

"Perhaps. Your sums are not your affection, Orlen. I can doubt one without denying the other."

He put his hand beside hers on the table, not over it. She turned her palm and touched his fingers for a moment. The keeper could see them if he looked up. Neither pretended the gesture was invisible or that it amounted to a treaty.

"I will write again if the post is late beyond the ordinary interval," Edda said. "Not to demand the answer I want. To ask whether they received the disclosure."

"And if they remove you?"

"Then I will know what my office costs me. I would rather know that than have you think I secretly bought your contract."

Orlen folded the sheet. His quotation remained unaccepted when he left, and the absence of a Kaelmont letter remained only an absence.

---

The trial report reached Damaris while she was dividing coins for the next works pay chest.

There was enough unpledged money already assigned to works to pay the witnessed inspection and a portion of the stopped roof workers' arrears. There was not enough in that same purse to buy Hesta's dressed block, haul it, pay the additional setting labor and keep the next due wages whole. A royal order could make the account say otherwise. It could not make the chest heavier.

Ysabet read Pell's judgment aloud in the smaller accounts chamber. Luceran listened without interrupting until she reached the words *suitable for a lower course, not accepted as a load*.

"Then we have the stone," he said.

"We have found that she owns a usable stone," Damaris replied.

"Could we pay for it next market day?"

"Perhaps. We have not counted that day's unpledged receipts, and I will not borrow from Jorvan's pledged gate net to make a perhaps sound like an order."

"Can I ask Hesta to hold it?"

"You may ask. She may refuse. She has already paid for two failed uses of her men's time: one failed block and the trimming of this one. Her first refusal does not require her to extend credit."

Ysabet turned the report over. "There is another cost. Pell cannot certify the finished drain from a stone alone. The adjoining face must be measured again after the cut. The gutter above still needs work."

Luceran stared at the figures. The phrase *King Beneath* had occupied his thoughts since the scholarly hearing. It was easier to imagine an ancient answer beneath his house than to account for a finger's breadth of stone removed from the wrong edge.

"Bring Hesta here," he said.

Damaris looked at him.

"Not to command her," he added. "To let her name the terms herself."

"Then bring the workers too," Ysabet said. "They will be asked to lay it."

Luceran nodded. He had wanted to announce that the third stone was sound. Instead he had called a meeting at which he would have to explain why soundness was not enough.

---

Sera Sorelle arrived before Hesta, carrying a small purse and a complaint.

She had received the equal-notice letter about possible hall dates. It named no date and reserved no room. She thought the wording unnecessarily cold. Ysabet answered that the same wording would go to the other interested houses and that no house could be promised the west service room while guards used it.

"I know what I was refused," Sera said. "You needn't refuse it again."

She set the purse on the table. It was a payment against the assessed awning-rope balance, not the whole amount. She wanted a receipt naming the portion paid and the portion still in dispute, including the separate claim for the roof workers' lost morning.

Damaris counted the coins in her sight. "This will reduce the sponsor debt. It does not settle the workers' claim."

"I have not said it did."

"Nor does it purchase a hall date."

Sera gave her a look. "I have not asked it to."

Luceran had once known how to turn Sera's irritation into a joke. He could not find a harmless one now. She had paid something she owed and had still come prepared to defend herself against the suspicion that she was buying favor.

"I should have told you earlier that a hearing was all I could promise," he said.

"You did tell me. You did not tell me how many people would hear the request before I could withdraw it."

He had no good answer. She took the receipt and left without asking him to walk with her.

When Hesta entered, the room had already acquired the uncomfortable honesty of a place where coins had been counted.

She brought Lenn and her copy of the inspection. Tomas came from the receiving yard with Berenne. Damaris set the works chest open far enough for them to see the separated pay packets, not to invite them to touch the money. Ysabet placed the quarry report beside the wage roll.

Luceran began by asking Hesta her price for the third block in its new lower-course shape. She named the stone, the additional dressing, carriage and a fair allowance for the men who would wait while the setting bed was prepared. She did not ask the Crown to pay for the failed second block again. She did not offer the third at the price of a block it no longer was.

Tomas asked who would repair the gutter while the new drain course was being set.

"That is a different line," Berenne said, and drew it on the plan.

Damaris read the sums back. The total exceeded the unpledged works coin left after the already due wages and the agreed inspection. No one challenged her count. The difference was not a dispute about arithmetic.

"I can order it," Luceran said, and heard the room go still.

"You can order men to work," Hesta answered. "You cannot order my brother's quarry to eat the cost until you remember us."

Lenn looked alarmed at her plainness. She did not withdraw it.

Luceran felt the old heat of being contradicted before his own officers. He had not forgotten how easily an insult could become an excuse to demand obedience. He looked at Tomas's hands, stained with wet mortar, and at the packets that were already owed to him and the other workers.

"Pay the due portion of the roof wages from the assigned works coin," he told Damaris. "Pay the trial inspection as witnessed. Do not purchase the stone today."

Tomas did not thank him. He asked which men would be paid and which would still be waiting. Damaris named them from the roll and let him check. One of the men who had worked the festival interruption would receive his recorded works hours; the separate sponsor claim remained a claim against the sponsors, not a second wage charged to the workers. Other arrears remained outstanding.

Hesta listened. Then she gave the king a condition.

"I will keep this block covered for two market days. I will not charge the Crown for those two days of standing, because the inspection changed its use and I want the sale. After that I may offer it elsewhere. If you send an order, it must name the stone, the new dimensions, the price, the carriage, the setting men and the coin that pays them. My first refusal and my track right remain as written whether you buy it or not."

"Two market days," Luceran repeated.

"Not two days of your choosing after the rain stops."

Ysabet wrote the offer. Damaris would not call it a purchase order. Hesta and Lenn marked their copy; Berenne marked the measurements; Tomas witnessed the wage division but did not certify a finished drain. The king sealed a narrow acknowledgment of the trial result and the proposed deadline. It bound the Crown to the inspection payment already due and preserved Hesta's written offer. It did not transfer title to the stone.

The price of a promise had become visible enough that nobody could pretend it was paid.

---

Miren found Luceran after the workers had gone.

He was standing in the receiving yard beneath the bad gutter, watching water pass the plank and enter the channel Tomas had cut. He had no cloak over his head. She offered him one, and he took it without treating the gesture as an answer to anything else.

"I thought I was going to do a good thing," he said.

"You paid some men who were owed money."

"And left the drain open."

"You did not open it today."

He looked at the water. "Hesta thinks I want her to work for nothing."

"She thinks you might. You nearly ordered it."

"I stopped."

"Yes."

The word did not become praise merely because he wanted it to. Miren stood beside him while a cart passed outside the gate. The driver took the dry edge of the track without waiting for a royal direction. There were still roads the castle could not hear and decisions no listening stone could make for them.

"I asked Edselsto nothing about the quarry," Luceran said.

"Would it have known whether the seam ran through the block?"

"Not from the map gallery."

"Then Pell had to go. Hesta had to let them cut. Berenne had to measure. Tomas had to ask who would pay him. Those are not failures of the castle."

He smiled unhappily. "You have learned to hear an argument I haven't made."

"I have heard you make it before."

She did not take his hand. The cloak had been enough tenderness for the moment.

Rhyse crossed the yard with the evening watch roll. He reported the upper-gallery recess secure, the lower stair still barred and the west service room occupied by its ordinary men. A household servant had asked whether the scholars' new phrase meant there would be another night inspection. Rhyse had told him there was no such order. The disputed words remained in Ysabet's restricted record, not in a proclamation.

At the village post-house Edda found no late Kaelmont packet. Orlen's higher quotation still waited for an ordinary Crown decision. Sera's partial payment was entered against the awning rope, with the rest disputed or due. Hesta's third stone lay beneath its quarry awning, inspected but not sold. One block from the trial was accepted, another had failed, and this third had a new possible use but no accepted load or completed purchase. Her thirty-day or twelve-accepted-load first refusal and four-season toll privilege had not changed.

Damaris closed the works book with wages still outstanding, the roof and gutter unfinished, and Jorvan's pledged east-gate receipts untouched. Six sacks remained north and six at Edselsto; no foreign road right had appeared. Eighteen patrol mounts remained withdrawn from Crown service. If Luceran wished to ride beyond the walls for pleasure or inquiry, he would have to learn whose horses, land, forage and guards such a ride would cost.

Canon Merel went through the family nave after the last ordinary service. He counted the ancestral lights with his own eyes, entered the count and did not ask the castle to interpret it.

The first candle was dark. Six still burned.

At the quarry Lenn lifted the corner of the awning to check the third stone one last time. The pale seam was visible where the bad edge had been cut away. He laid his palm against the sound face, then put the cloth back.

For two market days it belonged to Hesta and might become the Crown's. On the third, if no one brought the promised coin, it would still belong to her.

That was what a promise weighed when nobody could command the stone to move.
