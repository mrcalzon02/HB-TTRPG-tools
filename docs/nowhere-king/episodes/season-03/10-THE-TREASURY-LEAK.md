# Season Three, Episode 10 — The Treasury Leak

The pay chest opened at the first bell, and Fera arrived before the clerk had found his spectacles.

She had kept the signed voucher flat beneath a piece of board all night. The corners were still damp from the sickroom when she laid it on his table.

The clerk read the household officer's name, checked Damaris's signature, and counted out the wages owed for the extra hours she had worked when frightened visitors crowded the passages after the candle went dark. He counted twice, not because he distrusted Fera, but because a man who made a mistake at the pay chest had to explain it in writing.

Fera did not put the coins away immediately.

“Is that all of it?”

“The hours allowed on the voucher.”

“Then yes,” she said. “It is.”

The clerk showed her the entry, and she put her mark beside it. The money went into a little purse whose drawstring had been repaired with thread of a different color. Her sister was due the hours she had covered. Fera could now pay her.

The clerk reached for the next paper. Fera remained.

“The covers,” she said.

“What covers?”

“The two I put on the sickroom list. The ones I have mended until there is more seam than cloth.”

He found the list beneath an account for the upper hall. No purchase had been authorized. The note said only that replacements were needed.

“I can put it before the steward.”

“You already have.”

He looked at her then. She had been paid for yesterday's work and was asking about tomorrow's.

“Will you tell her I asked again?”

“I will write that you came.”

“Write why.”

He took a clean corner of the sheet and did so. Fera watched him form the words. A name, a date, two worn covers. No new expense had yet been incurred. It was a small enough matter to disappear among larger ones.

When Damaris arrived, she found the clerk's spectacles beside three open books and Fera's note on top.

“Was the voucher honored?”

“Yes, my lady.”

“Good. Which purse bore it?”

“The household sickroom, as you directed.”

“And the nave watch?”

“Still in its own book.”

“Keep it there.”

Damaris turned the pages. The recent hall supper had been properly paid for: thirty-six mouths, two hearths made usable, a third left cold, three guest rooms made dry, linen and firewood consumed. The first candle's extinction had then demanded living guards, witnesses, stopped work, more charcoal and more wages. Private household challenges had detained servants who ought to have been at their tasks. The gate book contained a disputed hour of a hauler's time. The works book contained men forbidden to descend an unsafe stair.

None of these expenses was theft. That was what made them difficult to find. Each had begun with someone doing a defensible thing, or a frightened thing, or a courteous thing, and then expecting a different purse to pay for it.

Damaris wrote four headings on a scrap: hall, gate, nave, works. Beneath them she wrote a fifth: sickroom.

The clerk regarded the scrap with apprehension.

“Is there a shortage?”

“There is a difference between a shortage and a promise made against money already promised elsewhere.”

“Which have we?”

“We shall know when every man who says he was paid has been paid, and every man who says he is owed has been heard.”

She sent for Tomas, then for Bessa's original gate book. She did not send for Edselsto. A castle that could hear through a marked wall would not tell her which purse had lawful title to a coin.

---

At the east gate, Bessa had already refused the Jorvan observer's request to take the corrected tally away for copying.

“You may see it here,” she said. “You may compare it with the original. You may have a witnessed extract. The book remains in my keeping.”

“I am paid to see that the deductions are honest.”

“And I am sworn to see that the record survives your visit.”

The observer was neither a thief nor a fool. He had a house to answer to, and a loan whose repayment depended on what the gate actually earned. He accepted the bench Bessa offered and asked her to turn back three pages.

Othra Jorvan's advance had been twenty-four marks. Repayment was capped at twenty-seven, taken from the east gate's net surplus after ordinary expenses for twelve market days. If that failed to satisfy the cap, no more than half the net could be taken for four further market days. Thereafter the house had an ordinary petition, not a hand upon the toll chest. The second market gate was not pledged. The observer could witness the account, not collect a penny or command a guard.

Bessa repeated the terms without looking at the copy.

The observer tapped the entry for the morning of the exchanged cloaks.

“A guard stood here an hour longer than he might have done.”

“He stood his assigned watch. His pay was already due.”

“Then why is there another charge beside it?”

“Because someone copied the delay into the expenses before we settled what it cost.”

“And does it cost the gate?”

“Not merely because it happened at the gate.”

Damaris arrived while Bessa was showing him the difference between a watchman's ordinary duty and a noble visitor's private question. The steward examined the original entry, the corrected copy, the yard keeper's receipt and Dain's account of the delay.

“No one has yet established an extra gate expense,” Damaris said. “The watch was on duty. We will not diminish the pledged net by calling a household's curiosity a toll charge.”

The observer drew his finger back from the page. “My house will welcome that.”

“Your house will welcome an accurate book. If the next true gate repair reduces the net, you will see that as well.”

The younger hauler stood outside the office, holding his cap. He had lost the hour in which he might have taken iron uphill at Orlen's quoted rate. Orlen had offered to hold the price until the next market day if the carrier paid for another night's shelter. The carrier had refused. The price was not a load, and an offered load was not a signed carriage contract.

“Am I to be paid?” he asked when admitted.

“What do you say you lost?” Damaris asked.

“An hour, and the work I might have done in it.”

“Those are not the same claim.”

He flushed. “They felt much alike.”

Bessa showed him the watch record. It gave the hour of detention, the officer who ordered it, and the release after the covered-yard keeper identified the two men and their receipts. It did not certify a promised load or an agreed freight price.

“I can hear a claim for the time a royal guard detained you without sufficient authority,” Damaris said. “I cannot give you the profit of a journey no one had contracted.”

“Then I must pay the yard keeper for a second night?”

“You must decide whether to stay. I will not pretend I can make yesterday's cart leave today.”

He stared at the account, where his lost hour had become ink in a book he did not own.

“Will the guard be punished?”

“The captain will answer for his watch. Your payment will not depend on his humiliation.”

Bessa summoned the keeper and the other hauler to attest the time. Damaris ordered a narrow petition entered for the customary hire of one working hour, to be paid from the Crown's household-watch allowance if the witnesses agreed. She did not call it a toll expense or a compensation for imagined freight.

The observer asked to see the petition.

“You may see that the gate was not charged,” Bessa said. “You have no right to the man's private petition.”

He considered that, then nodded. It was the first satisfactory answer he had received all morning.

---

Hesta Vale had rented the baker's room for another hour because it was cheaper than arguing in the rain.

Her brother had brought a measured block, too heavy for the table and too small to satisfy the men who wanted grand stone for a grand gate. Orlen Morcant had brought a revised delivered-price quotation. Two haulers had brought the accounts of what a cart actually consumed between the quarry, the receiving yard and the east road. Edda Kelm, the Kaelmont clerk, had come with figures from a different road that the weather might close before any of them used it.

Hesta placed the papers beside the stone.

“Tell me which price you mean,” she said to Orlen.

“The price delivered.”

“To where?”

“The Crown yard.”

“By whose cart?”

“That is included.”

“Whose shelter?”

“Not included if the yard is closed when the load arrives.”

“And if the lower rut takes a wheel?”

“The carrier bears ordinary road damage, unless the road keeper has given a warranty.”

“He has not.”

Orlen spread his hands. “Then I have given you a price, not a miracle.”

Hesta smiled in spite of herself. He was a merchant who preferred an expensive truth to a cheap promise that would ruin his next bargain.

Her existing Crown-side first-refusal agreement ran thirty days and covered twelve accepted drain-block loads, witnessed and paid under its own terms. One trial block had been accepted. A second had failed inspection. The third remained untested. Her older four-season toll-free Crown-track right for quarry hauling, subject to military closure, was separate. Neither privilege made the Crown buy unaccepted stone, and neither let a stranger displace her lawful first offer merely by calling a quote urgent.

Her brother tapped the failed block. “We can cut the next smaller.”

“We can,” Hesta said. “We will not call the failed one accepted.”

Orlen pointed to his figures. “Nor will I say my iron reaches the yard without shelter, time and feed. If you compare my delivered price to stone lying in a quarry, you will think one of us is cheating.”

“Perhaps both of you are,” one hauler said.

“Then you may carry the goods for nothing and expose us.”

The hauler laughed. The room warmed for a moment.

Damaris entered with Tomas and listened before she spoke. She asked which costs were fixed by existing agreement, which depended on inspection, and which remained only offers. Hesta answered the first. Orlen answered the second. The haulers answered the third, frequently correcting both.

When Damaris asked whether a new hall supper would increase the need for fuel and cloth, Orlen said it would. When she asked whether deferring the upper-gallery hangings would leave the hall unsafe, Hesta said no, though the bare walls would be noticed. Tomas said the third hearth was cold for a reason and would remain unused until the necessary work was measured and paid.

“What of the roof?” Hesta asked.

“The receiving-yard bay is not a finished winter roof,” Tomas said. “The gutter and drain are still due.”

“Then don't spend its money on a feast.”

Damaris looked at the stone. “That is the answer everyone gives until the feast is theirs.”

Hesta did not deny it. Her family had been glad of the hall supper. Her brother had eaten there. An open court brought work, contracts and faces she might persuade. She wanted the hall to prosper, but she also wanted a cart to pass a sound road.

“Tell the king whose supper he is buying,” she said. “He may still want it.”

Damaris took the quotations, leaving Hesta's original agreement where it lay. No new order had been placed.

---

Luceran had expected a discussion of theft.

Damaris brought him five books and no thief.

The king sat at the end of the table with Rhyse, Ysabet, Tomas and the steward. Outside the window, workers crossed the yard carrying timber past the unfinished receiving roof. The stone below the barred stair had not been certified safe. Six candles still burned in the family nave. The first remained dark.

“Is the treasury leaking?” Luceran asked.

“Not through a hole in the wall,” Damaris said.

“That would at least be easy to find.”

“It would be easy to see. Finding the money afterward would be another matter.”

She set the books down in their proper order. The east-gate account belonged to the gate and to Jorvan's bounded repayment terms. The works account carried the stopped masons' wages, the bruised worker's lost labor, charcoal for the waiting brazier and the cost of another dry-night measurement. The nave carried its own watch. The sickroom had paid Fera's signed voucher that morning but still lacked sound covers. The hall had consumed the linen and fuel of its earlier supper and was preparing to consume more.

“None of this is a fraud,” she said. “That does not make it free.”

Luceran turned the page listing the hall's expected guests. Several houses had been told another supper was likely. No formal invitations had gone out, but servants had begun reserving time. The third hearth remained cold. The upper-gallery hangings were still deferred.

“You would close the hall?”

“I would stop promising another great supper until the roof, linen and owed wages are covered. Two hearths remain usable. Three guest rooms remain dry. We can receive envoys and feed the household without dressing every meal as a triumph.”

“Those houses will say we are afraid.”

“They may. They will also say we are poor. If we feed them now and fail to pay a worker, they will say both and be right about one.”

Rhyse asked about the extra watch after the candle failed. Damaris separated guards stationed for the nave from the ordinary east-gate watch and from the unauthorized private challenges. He accepted the distinction and said Dain would put each delay against the officer who ordered it. That did not mean every delayed person had a payable claim.

“The hauler has witnesses,” Damaris said. “One hour of improper detention, not the profit of a journey that never had a contract. I propose the customary working-hour hire from the household-watch allowance, not the gate surplus.”

Ysabet read the petition. “If we pay him, the next man will ask.”

“Then we shall hear him,” Damaris said. “A precedent for hearing a claim is not a promise to grant every claim.”

Luceran looked at the figure for the proposed supper. It was not an enormous sum against the kingdom's needs. That was part of the humiliation. He could not solve a northern grain shortage by refusing a dinner. He could, however, pay people already owed and keep the sickroom from receiving its next patient on a worn cover.

“What if I order the supper and the repairs?”

“Then I must show you which other payment waits.”

“Choose one.”

“The stopped workers' wages, the sickroom cloth, or the next receiving-yard drain work. I will not take from Jorvan's pledged net, Hesta's separate rights, the promised battle compensation or a foreign deposit whose title is not ours.”

He wanted to ask Edselsto whether it remembered another treasury. He knew how the question would sound. The castle had once found a sealed chamber; living hands had opened it, and living records had separated Crown plate from other people's goods. A remembered room could not pay a wage until someone established what belonged to whom.

Tomas slid the roof drawing toward him. “The yard will be wet again before winter if we leave that gutter.”

Luceran had already sent men into the north without enough mounted protection. Six sacks of grain remained at Edselsto and six north; the larger northern delivery was not complete. Dols had not granted military passage. Merehaven had not released a second advance. Teth's ferry remained the owner's to value and challenge. He could not spend those jurisdictions into obedience.

He took up the pen.

“No second hall supper this turn,” he said. “The upper-gallery hangings remain deferred. Use the uncommitted household hospitality allowance first for the sickroom covers, the lawful wage arrears and the next measured roof-and-drain work. The third hearth stays cold until inspected. Keep the guest rooms and two hearths in service.”

Damaris waited.

“Pay the hauler's witnessed hour from the household watch account if his petition is proved. Let the books show why. Do not turn the payment into a freight contract.”

“And Hesta's stone?”

“Inspect what is offered under the existing first-refusal agreement. Do not pay for the second block as though it passed. Do not buy the third before it is tested.”

“Orlen?”

“His delivered quotation remains a quotation. Compare it with the other actual delivered costs. No award today.”

“Jorvan?”

“His twenty-four-mark advance and twenty-seven-mark cap stand as written. Nothing more pledged. Nothing taken from another gate.”

Damaris set a clean sheet beneath his hand. She had not come to be reassured; she had come for an order she could administer.

Ysabet read the wording aloud. It named the purses, the deferrals and the payments. It did not say the Crown had become wealthy by refusing hospitality. Luceran sealed it anyway.

From the courtyard came the sound of a hammer striking timber. No one at the table mistook it for a treasury opening.

---

The announcement went badly in precisely the ordinary ways Damaris had expected.

A household steward complained that his lady had already chosen a gown for the next supper. A kitchen master objected that a smaller table did not make his stores cheaper if visitors continued arriving without notice. A guest who had hoped to meet the Kaelmont clerk accused the court of hiding behind thrift to avoid hearing petitions.

Damaris did not ask them to admire the decision. She told the kitchen master how many meals were still authorized, the chamberlain which rooms remained available, and the disappointed guest where the public petition hours would be held. No one received an extra room or a new contract in exchange for taking the news well.

At the sickroom, Fera received two sound covers from the household stores after a cloth merchant's existing price was checked against the stock book. They were plain and not enough to renew the whole linen reserve. She spread one over the bed, pulled it tight and tested the seam with both hands.

Yara came to see whether the voucher had been honored.

“It was paid,” Fera said, touching her purse. “And these arrived.”

“Good.”

“Do not tell Jalen the whole sickroom has been repaired.”

“I would not.”

“Tell him the covers came. He asked about my pay, not the bed.”

“Then I will tell him both, and which was which.”

Fera smiled. It was not gratitude for a royal miracle. It was relief that two small promises had finally become objects she could use.

At the works yard, Pell Falbrecht read the order aloud to the men held off the lower stair. Their waiting wages were entered as wages, not as a gift. The man with the bruised heel would return to suitable work only with the physician's approval. The independent mason's second reading still required a dry night and a checked reference point. The barred passage remained barred.

One of the workers asked whether the king's refusal to give another supper meant there was money for a new stair.

“No,” Pell said. “It means you are paid for not being sent down an unsafe one.”

“That is a peculiar kind of progress.”

“It is better than a funeral charged to the wrong book.”

At the gate, the younger hauler's witnesses agreed on the hour. The covered-yard keeper had no interest in inventing a detention that kept his own yard occupied. Bessa entered the testimony; Damaris approved payment at the customary hire for the lost working hour. The hauler accepted the coins without surrendering his right to complain that he had missed a larger opportunity. The entry expressly refused payment for an uncontracted load. The guard's watch record remained intact for Rhyse to review.

The Jorvan observer saw the corrected gate net and the separate household payment, then signed that he had witnessed the entries, not that he approved every royal decision. Bessa returned the original book to its locked chest.

Hesta learned of the decision from Tomas, who brought the order for inspection of the third drain block. She read the wording twice.

“The first refusal stands?”

“It does.”

“The failed stone stays failed?”

“It does.”

“And the next is tested before anyone buys it?”

“Yes.”

“Then I shall send my brother to the yard, not the feast.”

Orlen, hearing that no iron order had been awarded, asked for his quotation back so he could correct the shelter charge before the next market. Damaris let him copy it, but retained the original with its date. His prices might change; the Crown's record of what had been offered would not.

By evening, the household had spent money and looked less splendid. The roof was still unfinished. The north was still hungry. Yet the people who had worked, waited or kept watch had begun receiving what the books said was owed.

---

Miren found Luceran in the corridor outside the family nave. He had not entered. A pair of living guards stood at the door, and the canon's assistant was carrying fresh oil for the ordinary lamps, not for the seven ancestral candles.

“I hear you have offended half the guests,” she said.

“Only the half who expected supper.”

“The other half will be offended when they hear why.”

He showed her the sealed order. She read it without praising him.

“Damaris has made you choose a small thing.”

“She made me choose what it costs.”

“Those are not the same.”

He leaned against the stone. “I wanted a court that did not make every favor a contest. Now I cannot give a dinner without borrowing from the sickroom.”

“You can give a dinner,” Miren said. “You have decided not to give this one.”

“That is a comfort.”

“It was not meant as one.”

They listened to the guards exchange a quiet word. One was relieved at the end of his watch by another whose name was written in Dain's book. The change was visible, human and imperfect.

Luceran had hoped the sealed order would please Miren. He had begun to recognize the hope as a kind of demand and did not voice it. Her refusal to make a public virtue of his thrift was familiar enough to hurt.

“Did you come to read the order?” he asked.

“No. I came because the kitchen sent too much bread to the south gallery, and I thought you might eat some before it went hard.”

“Is that a royal invitation?”

“It is bread.”

He laughed, then stopped when he saw she was smiling.

They walked together as far as the gallery turn. She did not take his hand. He did not ask why. The private room remained physically deaf, and her answer about marriage remained her own. Neither a paid voucher nor a canceled supper altered what he had once commanded Edselsto to repeat.

At the nave door, Canon Merel received the assistant's lamp oil and sent him back to his ordinary work. The first ancestral wick remained black. Six flames burned steadily beside it. Merel entered the hour in his own book, not because he understood the failure, but because he had seen it.

Below, Damaris locked the books after writing the day's disbursements. The gate had its own net. The household had paid a detained man for an hour and a sickroom attendant for her work. The works yard still owed a roof, a drain and an honest reading of the stair. Hesta had a right to offer stone and no right to have bad stone accepted. Orlen had offered a price and won no contract. Jorvan had a bounded claim, not the keys to the kingdom.

There was no hidden thief to drag before the court. There were only decisions, and the little streams of cost that ran from them.

Damaris put the king's sealed order between the day's account and the next day's blank page.

Tomorrow someone would ask for a favor. Tomorrow the books would have to answer.
