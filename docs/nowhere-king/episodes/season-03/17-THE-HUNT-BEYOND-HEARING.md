# Season Three, Episode 17 — The Hunt Beyond Hearing

The stable boy had put the saddle away before Luceran asked for it.

It hung on the third peg from the door, the girth looped over the pommel and a strip of damp straw caught beneath one buckle. The boy was brushing a bay mare that had carried a messenger down from the north track before dawn. Her flanks were dark with drying sweat. When the king entered, the boy stopped brushing and reached for the saddle.

"Leave it," Luceran said. "She has worked."

"She can go again after feed, Your Grace."

"That was not a request."

The boy lowered his hand. He had made the offer out of habit, and Luceran could see how easily a king might mistake that habit for a horse's willingness.

At the far end of the stable a farrier was examining a cracked shoe. Two other stalls were empty. The emptiness looked, at first, like available room. Rhyse, arriving with the duty roll beneath his arm, knew why it was there.

"Eighteen patrol mounts remain withdrawn," he said. "The men have not been given eighteen replacements because you are bored of the walls."

"I said I wanted to ride."

"You said yesterday that you wanted a hunt."

Luceran rested his fingers on the worn saddle. "I remember what I said."

"The messenger mare is not a hunter. The farrier has the other fit horse under his hand. The remainder of the service animals are assigned, lame, or not ours to take. I can put you on a horse for a necessary journey. I cannot conjure a hunt out of the watch roster."

Outside, water dropped from the stable roof into a barrel. The repaired half of the gutter carried it neatly; the unrepaired half missed the barrel and made mud against the wall. Luceran had come to the stable hoping to hear something that did not sound like an account.

"Who hunts these hills?" he asked.

"The people who know them. And some nobles who think knowing the name of a hill is the same thing."

"Could we ask one of them?"

"We could. We could also ask whose ground they cross, who owns the dogs, who brings the food, who takes a broken ankle home, and whether the reeve wishes a line of riders across the village woodcutters' path."

Luceran looked at the bay mare. Her ears had turned toward the farrier's hammer.

"Then I will ask," he said.

Rhyse did not move aside. "Ask as a man seeking passage, or as a king commanding a road?"

Luceran considered the question longer than the captain expected.

"I am not certain yet."

"Then do not send a groom to saddle anything until you are."

---

Bessa had already counted the east-gate morning carts when Ysabet found her at the toll house.

A house-paid observer sat at the far end of the bench, where he could see the figures entered without touching the coins. Bessa had put the original tally between herself and the observer. The king's arrival did not change which hands held it.

"Will you want the track closed?" she asked after Luceran explained the proposed ride.

"No. I wanted to know where the track goes."

"Then you need the reeve. I can tell you what passes the gate and which cartmen paid. I cannot give away a path through somebody's coppice."

A charcoal burner waiting with two sacks nodded without asking permission. His cart had come from the lower pines, where the village cut fuelwood in strips so the hillside would grow again. He described a path that climbed beyond the last cart turning and bent toward a bare shoulder of rock. In dry weather it was a useful walking way. In rain, the middle section took water across its slope. Horses broke the edge when they tried to avoid the wet stones.

"Good hunting?" Luceran asked.

"For a man on his feet who knows where to stand. Bad for riders who want to be seen riding."

The observer looked up from the tally. "A royal party could take the upper track."

Bessa kept writing. "An upper track on whose permission?"

"The Crown has roads."

"The Crown has the road I am paid to count. The upper way is not on this tally."

Luceran almost smiled. The observer had made the same mistake he had made in the stable: seeing a line on the landscape and imagining it belonged to the person who could most easily order others onto it.

The charcoal burner named a fallen beech beside the path and a spring below it. He warned that the spring was shallow after a hard freeze and that the upper shoulder was open to weather. His knowledge came from carrying sacks over the route, not from a map.

Ysabet wrote the landmarks down without calling them a survey.

"Who is cutting there tomorrow?" she asked.

"Two families, if the rain lets them. One has already paid for a mule to fetch the wood."

"If a royal hunt frightens the mule?"

"Then somebody will have to find the wood and pay for the mule's wasted day."

Luceran asked whether the burner would come to the castle and speak to the reeve with them. The man shook his head. He had a cart to deliver. He would come if his time were paid and his load did not spoil while he waited.

"That seems fair," Luceran said.

"It seems ordinary," Bessa replied.

The observer's pen scratched on his own sheet. He had witnessed a toll count and heard a conversation about a hill. Neither made him keeper of the hill.

---

The reeve met them in the village hall, where a woman was repairing a basket by the window and two woodcutters waited for a dispute over a cart's broken pin to be heard.

The king had sent notice rather than appearing at the door with mounted men. That had given the reeve time to bring the person who actually walked the upper path. Her name was Maera, a widow who cut brush for the baker's ovens and guided visiting drovers when the weather permitted. She arrived with mud to the knee and no bow.

"I was told you wanted a hunt," she said.

"I wanted to see the ridge."

"Then you want to walk."

"I had imagined riding."

"I know. I have seen riders imagine it."

The basket-mender laughed. Luceran took the laugh without deciding it was insolence.

Maera set three small stones on the table. The first stood for the gate where the cart road ended. The second was the fallen beech. The third was the shoulder beyond the last cut. She placed a broken twig between the second and third stones.

"Water crosses here. The stone beneath is sound, but the edge is soft. You may take one horse through it if you walk the animal. A party of riders will make a new ditch. A packhorse with a full load will be worse."

"What game is there?"

"Enough to live. Not enough to entertain a court without driving it away from the people who depend on it."

The reeve did not claim ownership of every deer. He explained the village's customary use of the coppice, the woodcutting days, and the need to keep the cart turn open. The Crown's old rights farther up the mountain had not been measured into this discussion. Nobody in the room could certify a royal hunting right on the upper shoulder simply because a map showed it within sight of Edselsto.

"We could give passage for an inspection," the reeve said. "A single daylight visit with a guide. We cannot grant you an exclusive hunt today. If you wish to pursue game, bring the old grants and the people who hold the adjoining land, and let them answer."

Luceran examined the three stones. "Would a foot party disturb the cutting?"

"Not if it waits until the mule has come down, leaves the gate free, and does not take the cutters' dry shelter. The guide chooses whether the weather is safe. A royal seal does not keep the path from washing out."

Maera folded her hands. "And I will not guide you for the honor of it. I have oven brush to gather. If I spend the day with you, the baker must buy from someone else."

"Name your wage."

"First tell me who is coming. One king on foot is one thing. Twelve armed men, a physician, three horses and a cook are another."

Luceran could not tell her. He had not asked his household.

The reeve gathered the stones back into a row. "Then there is nothing to refuse yet. Come with a number, and we can make an answer that means something."

Outside, the cart-pin dispute was still waiting. The reeve called its two owners forward. A king's unfinished request did not suspend the village's other work.

---

Damaris spread three separate books on the accounts-chamber table and would not let Luceran combine them.

The first held the assigned works coin. The inspection of Hesta Vale's third stone had been paid from it, along with a portion of the roof wages already due. The remaining wage names were still there. Hesta's two-market-day hold was running, and no order had bought her lower-course stone.

The second held the east-gate receipts and the obligations against their net. Othra Jorvan's twenty-four-mark advance and capped twenty-seven-mark repayment had not become a convenient purse merely because the king wanted to leave the castle. Bessa still counted the tolls; the observer still witnessed without collecting or commanding.

The third was the smaller household book. It had no room for an expedition, but a modest personal outing might be paid from the king's own uncommitted allowance if he chose to spend it instead of keeping it for hospitality.

"You may pay a guide, bread, dry cloth, a messenger and a hired animal from here," Damaris said, tapping the third book. "Not from the works chest. Not from the pledged gate net. But there is not enough for a grand hunt, and I will not call the allowance inexhaustible."

"How much for a horse?"

"Whose horse?"

"I do not know."

"Then I do not know the price. Ask a person who has one to hire. If you want an animal capable of carrying you and returning over wet ground, the farrier must inspect it. If you want a spare, that is another animal and another keeper."

Ysabet had made a separate note of the reeve's conditions. The village would consider a small daylight passage; no exclusive right to game, no cutting across the coppice while the mule was at work, no commandeering of shelter. Maera would need her day's lost earnings covered. Weather could cancel the journey after the bread had been bought.

"And guards?" Luceran asked.

Rhyse was waiting by the door.

"The upper-gallery recess remains guarded. The lower service stair remains barred. The west service room still holds its watch. I cannot take those men to see you enjoy a hill."

"I did not ask you to empty the castle."

"You asked whether you could have guards. Those are the men I have. The eighteen withdrawn patrol mounts have not made the watch lighter. I will not put a tired off-duty man on a dangerous path merely because his name is not written in the next watch column."

"Could I hire village men?"

"You may ask whether competent men are free, whether they are willing, and what they will do. A hired escort is not a royal patrol. He may turn back for weather. He must know where his duty ends. If you want me to approve the security arrangement, I must see the route and the names."

Damaris turned another page. "Medical provision?"

Fera's sickroom linen was already short. There was no reason to strip a patient's cover to dress a king's scraped knee. Ysabet proposed a small separately purchased bandage roll and a named person able to tend an ordinary injury; anything worse would require a way to carry the injured person back. Maera knew where a handcart could reach and where it could not. The household had no authority to assume that a village cart would wait without hire.

Luceran looked at the figures until they stopped resembling the effortless pleasure he had imagined.

"Could I go alone?"

Rhyse's expression did not soften. "You could leave without telling me. You could also fall into a washout where no one expects to find you. Neither would make you free of the people who must search."

"What would you advise?"

"Begin with a meeting at the cart turn. Let Maera walk you to the beech on a dry day if the reeve permits. Do not call it a hunt. Do not promise that a full party will follow. Learn what the ground can bear before you make it bear you."

The king set the household book between them. "Then price that. One daylight inspection, not a hunt."

Damaris took up her pen. It was a smaller order than he had wanted, and for the first time it was an order she could begin to price.

---

Miren was in the south-gallery withdrawing room when Luceran came to tell her.

He knocked. She opened the door herself. No silver line connected the room to the castle's listening ways; the cut junction had not been repaired, and he had not asked for it to be. That did not make the room empty of other people. A seamstress was sitting beside the window with a length of mending. Miren finished giving her instructions before she invited him in.

"I heard you were planning to hunt," she said.

"News walks where Edselsto cannot."

"People have legs."

"I have discovered the same."

She put the mending aside. The seamstress left of her own accord, taking her needle case, and Miren did not ask her to swear silence.

"I am not going to hunt," Luceran said. "Not yet. I may walk a village path with a guide."

"Why?"

He had rehearsed an answer about air and exercise. It would have been true as far as it went.

"I want to stand somewhere the castle cannot answer me."

"You can do that here."

"Here I know I am being careful."

"And on the hill you would not be?"

"I thought perhaps I could forget for a little while."

Miren watched him. He had learned to ask permission for the room and still sometimes spoke as if her presence were the reward for learning.

"Did you want me to come?" she asked.

"Yes."

"To talk about what?"

"I don't know. That may be what I want most."

She went to the shutter and opened it a hand's breadth. The village roofs were visible below the pines, each one with smoke, chores and a person who would notice a royal party crossing the lane.

"The castle's hearing is not the only thing that makes a conversation difficult," she said. "A guide hears. A guard hears. A village woman carrying brush hears. Even if nobody repeats a word, I may not wish to say it."

"I would not command you."

"You have not asked whether I want the journey. You have asked whether I want to be away from the castle with you."

Luceran felt the distinction strike before he understood all of it.

"I would like you there," he said. "I will not put your name on the party."

"Good."

"Would you meet me at the village, if you chose?"

"Perhaps. But not as the object of a hunt."

He laughed once, surprised by the sharpness of the phrase.

"I have made it sound worse than it was."

"You made it sound like a thing arranged for your comfort. That is different."

She did not promise to meet him. She did not forgive the stolen conversation in the map gallery or answer the question of marriage. When he left, she remained in the disconnected room with her own work and her own view of the valley.

Luceran was grateful for the invitation she had not made. He was beginning to understand how much freedom there was in a refusal that required no excuse.

---

Maera returned to the village hall the following afternoon with a strip of cord, a weathered walking staff and a boy who knew the cart gate.

She had walked the lower approach before breakfast. The first section was sound. The water crossing above the fallen beech was not. She would not take horses through it that day, and she would not promise that tomorrow would be better.

The reeve had written a narrow permission, subject to the customary village use. It named a daylight passage from the cart turn to the fallen beech and back, no hunting, no exclusive closure, no fire, no cutting, and no crossing beyond the agreed landmark. The woodcutters and their mule retained the track until their load was down. The guide could refuse to proceed in bad weather without forfeiting her agreed cancellation payment. The king's men would leave any damaged gate as they found it or pay for its repair, with the village witnessing the condition before and after.

The document did not pretend the reeve could grant land beyond the village's customary reach. Nor did it authorize passage into Dols or any foreign jurisdiction. A second request would require another answer.

Ysabet read it aloud to Luceran and the reeve. Maera corrected the description of the water crossing. The boy corrected the location of the gate latch, which was sound but stiff and must not be forced. The reeve corrected the hour at which the mule was expected to return.

"Who pays me if the weather turns before the king leaves the castle?" Maera asked.

"The household allowance," Damaris answered. "The agreed cancellation portion will be set aside before the day. You will not have to chase a royal steward after a storm."

"And if he turns back at the beech?"

"You have still guided him. Your full day is due."

Maera looked at Luceran. "I will tell you when to turn. I will not ask whether the mountain is insulting your crown."

"That is one of the reasons I wish to go with you."

The reeve held out the parchment. Luceran could have sealed it as a royal grant and made its words look grander. Instead he marked his acceptance beneath the village's conditions. Ysabet and the reeve each kept a copy. Damaris entered the reserved guide payment against the household allowance, not against works or the gate pledge. The weather decision remained Maera's. A named hired escort and a suitable animal had not yet been secured; without them, Rhyse would not approve the outing beyond the village cart turn.

The king asked whether the permission would still be good if he walked only to the gate and spoke with the reeve there.

"Yes," the reeve said. "The village can hear you at the gate without losing its day to you."

That was the first part of the journey he could actually make.

---

At dusk Luceran walked as far as the outer yard, where the castle road fell toward the village and the black pines gathered on either side.

He did not call for a horse. A servant carried the sealed village copy back to Ysabet. Rhyse took the evening watch without surrendering a man to the proposed excursion. Somewhere below, the charcoal burner was delivering the sacks he had refused to abandon for a king's convenience.

The king had a lawful path available to him, conditional on weather and other people's work. He had a guide whose wages would be paid even if he disappointed himself. He did not yet have a hunt, a horse, a security party or Miren's company. The path would not carry him into the mountain's unknown places, and he had learned nothing about the guarded recess or the barred stair by arranging it.

He thought of asking Edselsto whether the road was safe. It might have remembered a sound from a connected room or an old report brought through a listening way. It could not feel Maera's boot sink into the wet edge above the beech. It could not speak for the reeve, price a hired horse, or decide what Miren would choose.

He asked it nothing.

In the works chamber, Damaris had not moved the money owed to Hesta Vale. Her two-market-day offer remained open and unsatisfied; the third block remained at the quarry, its lower-course use measured but not bought. Other roof wages remained due. Sera's rope payment was still partial. Orlen's iron quote was still unaccepted, and no answer had arrived from Kaelmont for Edda. The pledged east-gate net remained under Jorvan's separate terms. The court had not repaired its gutter by arranging a walk.

In the family nave, Merel made his ordinary count. The first ancestral candle was dark. Six still burned. No new light failed because a king had asked to go beyond the castle's hearing.

Below the walls, Maera hung her wet cloak beside the baker's oven and told her son she had been hired to guide a king, weather permitting. The boy asked whether she was frightened.

"Of the king? Not especially."

"Of the mountain?"

"When the rain comes across the shoulder, yes."

"Then why go?"

"Because I know when to turn back. And because this time they have promised to pay me for saying so."

The king had wanted a place beyond the reach of a listening house. He had found a road that belonged to other lives, and the price of being permitted to use it.
