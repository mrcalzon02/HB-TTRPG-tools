# Season Three, Episode 03 — Proof of Life

The Jorvan observer arrived at the east gate before the first market cart. Bessa had given him a stool, a board for his own figures and a place beneath the arch where the rain would not run down his collar. She had not given him the keeper's chair.

He laid his house's copy of the agreement beside the morning tally and asked where the twenty-four marks were entered.

"In the paymaster's book," Bessa said. "This is the toll book."

"But the tolls repay the advance."

"The surplus does. After the gate is kept standing and the watch is paid. If you put the loan among the tolls, you will count the same money twice."

He was young enough to be offended by correction and trained well enough to check it. Bessa showed him the prior day's closing sum, the keeper's duplicate and the separate watch charge for a broken lantern. He copied the numbers. He did not touch the original.

A carter waiting beneath the arch wanted to know why a stranger was writing down his wheel fee. Bessa explained the observer's limited authority, and the carter asked whether Jorvan would now decide which carts passed.

"No," Bessa said.

The observer looked up. "I was about to say the same."

"Then we have saved you the trouble."

At noon a messenger asked whether the gate could certify that a man who had been seen dead was now alive. The request had come from a minor household frightened by the rumors surrounding Corven Sareth. Its servant had carried a written description of a face and wanted the keeper's seal on it.

Bessa read the paper and handed it back. "I can tell you which man paid to bring that cart through. I cannot swear that a face belongs to the name you have written, much less what happened to him last winter."

The messenger persisted. A royal gate, he argued, ought to be able to give a royal answer.

"A royal gate knows its hinges," Bessa said. "Ask the physician about breath. Ask the man about his name. Ask his own household what it remembers. Do not ask me to make one answer out of three different questions."

The Jorvan observer wrote nothing of the exchange in the toll figures. He did, however, remember it.

---

Jalen Orris was learning to move his chair without calling for the carpenter. The new brace held when he leaned toward the writing board. It did not make the lower part of his ruined body less terrible to look at, nor restore any strength to it. He could turn a page, pull the board nearer and place the carved wooden horse beyond the edge of his sleeve without assistance. That was sufficient work for one morning.

Master Isera arrived with the Crown physician and a sealed request from Ysabet. The request asked whether Jalen would permit a second, limited examination of his present condition, witnessed by a person of his choosing, so that future visitors could distinguish what the physicians had actually observed from what the Crown claimed about him.

"Who wants it?" he asked.

"The household needs a record that can be challenged," Isera said. "Dols may ask for one. That does not give Dols command over you here."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then we record that you refused. I will still change the cloth beneath your shoulder."

He thought about that. He had been carried home as a soldier once, and entered among the dead. Later he had been given another man's body and expected to be grateful for the duty it made possible. Everyone who wished to use his story had a reason to call it extraordinary. Very few had asked whether he wanted to spend another morning being extraordinary.

"Yara may witness," he said. "No audience. No household ladies invited to see a miracle. And I want the old discharge searched for whether I agree or not."

Rhyse, standing near the door, answered before the physicians could. "The search is already ordered. It is not payment for this."

Isera asked permission before lifting the cloth. She and the Crown physician separately examined what could be examined: present warmth, breath, speech, the supported shoulders, the persistence of movement despite the catastrophic injury, and the condition of the skin where the brace rested. They made separate notes. Neither pronounced him Corven Sareth. Neither claimed that an examination of a breathing body could establish the consent of the man whose body it had been.

When Isera asked what name should stand on the record, Jalen said his own.

"And the other?" she asked.

"Write that people knew the body as Corven's. That is something they can testify to. It isn't something I remember being."

Yara entered the exact distinction. She had carried three river warrants and seen what happened when a signature was mistaken for authority. This time she would not permit a physician's careful words to be turned into a diplomatic appointment.

Jalen made her read the entry aloud before he allowed his mark beside it. He could withdraw from further questioning. The two physicians could certify only their own observations. His mark acknowledged that he had heard the entry, not that every explanation offered for his existence was true.

"Now," he said, "could someone bring me breakfast? I have given the kingdom enough of my morning."

Senna and Harven reached Dols in the rain, two days after the east-gate counting began. The lower road had held them at a flooded ford for half a day. Their Crown escort had parted from them at the agreed road house, and the captain's final receipt stated only that the witnesses and their two sealed packets had passed beyond his protection without interference.

The reeve received Senna's packet first. She identified the chamber in which Luceran had spoken, the people present, the original instruction she had been shown, and the words of the admission she heard. Her copy was a witnessed comparison, not the original order. Ysabet still held that at Edselsto.

Harven laid down his separate account of the miller's-lane accident. He had seen the stone fall and Jalen continue speaking afterward. He had not seen the earlier attack on Corven, and would not let the reeve's clerk write his name beneath a sentence saying otherwise.

The reeve listened without congratulating either of them. He had wanted an answer from the Crown and had received one that made his work harder. The king's admission directly contradicted the denial already entered in the Dols register. It did not tell the reeve who else had carried the order, who had seen the death, or what the foreign benches would conclude about a body subsequently occupied by another man.

"May I send the king's confession to Merehaven?" the clerk asked.

"You may send notice that Dols has received it," the reeve said. "Not an instruction about what Merehaven must believe. They have their own warrant and their own hearing."

He ordered the two packets entered under separate numbers and kept the earlier denial in its original place, dated as received. Erasing it would make the contradiction less intelligible, not less shameful. Senna was paid her earned road allowance. Harven asked that the miller's apprentice's injuries remain in the accident account, where the boy's family could seek the costs without being dragged into an accusation about an attack they had not witnessed.

A priest's messenger was waiting outside with a proposed oath by which any claimant to a returned life might be made to swear that he had not been replaced. The reeve read the formula and folded it.

"Would a frightened man tell us the truth because the words are solemn?"

"He might fear to lie."

"He might fear to answer. We need not confuse the two."

Instead the reeve authorized a narrow reply: Dols would receive named, voluntary testimony and separately witnessed medical observations for consideration. It would not certify a person's former identity from appearance, memory questions, royal attestation or a single ritual. Any witness could decline a question outside personal knowledge. The original records would remain with their custodians, and no answer from Dols would bind Merehaven or Teth. This was a method for hearing evidence, not recognition of Corven, absolution of Luceran or permission to move a grain wagon.

The reply left with a paid rider. It had not reached Edselsto.

Leora's representative returned with a soaked cloak and a report that had cost more in copying fees than he had expected. He had inspected the village-watch book in the presence of its keeper, seen the damaged walking stick and compared the attendants' separately dated accounts. The originals had remained where they belonged.

He set his inventory beside the letters Leora had already preserved. She recognized her husband's handwriting on the older pages without pretending that recognition could tell her what had happened on the road. The representative had brought no body, no eyewitness to a killing and no lawful declaration of death.

"The injured attendant asked about his lodging," he said. "I had no authority to settle it."

"Did he change his account when you could not pay?"

"No. He was angry, but he kept to what he remembered."

"Then write that too."

Her mother called from the adjoining room. The little girl had put the ribbon horse on the bed and was explaining that the animal had come to inspect the patient's breakfast. Leora went to help settle the pillows before returning to the papers. Her mother's breathing was worse in damp weather. No question of Corven's fate made that duty less real.

The representative asked whether he should now seek a public judgment.

"From whom?" Leora said. "The watch can say what it saw. Dols can say what it received. Neither can tell me what Corven said to me in our first winter, and neither has seen his death."

She had already written to Jalen under his own name. She did not know whether her letter had arrived, and she did not know of Luceran's admission at Edselsto. She instructed her steward to keep the representative's report with the original marriage letters and the witnessed reply to her private question, each under its own date. A new fact must not be allowed to alter the ink of an older one.

Her niece came to ask whether the ribbon horse might be given a proper place at the table. Leora said yes, provided it did not eat the patient's bread. The girl laughed, and for a moment Leora was grateful that someone in the house could ask a question and be satisfied by the answer.

---

Luceran had expected the council to ask whether Jalen was truly alive. Instead Ysabet brought three papers and laid them on the small table.

The first was Isera's observation, the second the Crown physician's separate account, and the third Jalen's witnessed permission to be examined on that occasion only. The words were not identical. Where one physician had described a continuing pulse, the other had described the bodily condition that made ordinary survival impossible. Neither had certified a recovered Corven Sareth.

"That is why there are two," Ysabet said when Luceran frowned. "Not so that one may be made to agree with the other."

Damaris wanted to know whether the proposed safeguards would require a paid witness for every servant returning from an errand. She had enough expense already: a creditor at the east gate, a replacement cart still to hire, an unfinished gutter and a road the king could not command open.

"No," Rhyse said. "Only when the Crown asks a person to establish a disputed identity, service, commission or bodily condition for an official purpose. Ordinary work must remain ordinary."

Yara objected to the word *establish*. She had seen three foreign benches refuse to accept the same recommendation, and she knew that even an honest witness could establish only the things witnessed.

"Record," she said. "We can record who was questioned, by whom, what they chose to answer, and what source was shown. A bench elsewhere decides what it makes of the record."

Miren had listened from the far end of the table. She had not come to advise Luceran about forgiveness. She had come because she had helped Jalen into the chair and had heard how he wanted to use it.

"If you call the questions voluntary and then put him before a hall full of people who have come to see the king's miracle, you will have made refusal a public humiliation."

Luceran looked at the empty place where Jalen might have sat. The king had imagined that a visible man who could speak would be the strongest possible answer to the accusations against him. It was true that Jalen could speak. It was also true that he had refused to be used that way.

Ysabet drafted a household order while they argued. A person under Crown care could decline an identity interview or any particular question without loss of lodging, medical treatment, earned wages or protection. If the Crown sought a disputed statement for official use, two named living witnesses would attest the circumstances, not the truth of memories they had not shared. Physicians would certify their own observations. Original letters, service rolls, watch entries and medical notes would remain in separate custody. No household officer could treat a bodily resemblance, remembered detail, oath or royal seal as a substitute for another jurisdiction's commission or a dead person's consent.

The order did not create a universal law, a magical test or a foreign judgment. It governed the king's own servants and what they could claim in his name.

Luceran signed it. He was irritated by the number of ways a man could tell the truth and still fail to prove what a king wanted proved. Damaris reminded him that the same limits protected the toll books from Jorvan's overreach.

"Does this help Dols?" he asked.

"It may make our next answer worth reading," Yara said. "That is not the same as making them accept it."

Miren left with the physicians' copies to see that Jalen received his own. Luceran did not ask her to stay. He could have made a command out of the wish. He had learned how little such a command would be worth.

---

Tomas found the replacement carter at a shed below the village, inspecting a horse with a rubbed shoulder. The man had already lost a day's hire while deciding whether the Crown's money would arrive. He would not send his team north on the promise that Dols might repair a wall.

"The coin is counted," Tomas said.

"Then count what it costs to keep my horses standing."

They agreed to a limited deposit and one paid day of readiness after notice that a Dols-side inspection had actually been accepted. Further waiting would require a fresh price. Tomas wrote the conditions before the carter took the deposit. The team remained at its own shed, fed and available by arrangement, not harnessed beside six sacks as a public display of progress.

At the quarry road Hesta Vale was supervising Lenn and her brother as they packed the soft rut with broken stone. She had other customers waiting for her blocks. The Crown's accepted drain stone had earned a price; the second block still stood aside and the third had not been tested.

"Will the new carter use my passing place?" she asked.

"If Dols opens a civilian interval, and if the loads fit."

"Then he can wait for the same measurement as everyone else."

Tomas showed her the written terms. Her four-season toll privilege was intact, as was the right to challenge damage caused by an ordered Crown trial. Lenn had been paid for the morning's work. Bram, still unable to guide a heavy wheel, kept the count of tools and loads from the dry side of the yard.

Hesta's brother wanted to try the second stone again before the rain returned. She refused. The rut needed another day's settling, and a hurried failure would break more than a wheel.

The king had borrowed twenty-four marks. A portion had now become a deposit, wages and a patch of stone in the mud. None of it had turned the unaccepted Dols wall into a bridge.

---

Leora's letter reached Edselsto in the evening under an ordinary road-house receipt. Etta entered the carrier, hour and intact seal, then gave it to Yara because the outside named Jalen Orris and no royal office. She did not open it for a useful summary.

Jalen asked Yara to stay while he read. He could make out the writing slowly, resting the paper on the board he had chosen. Leora had asked whether he would accept a letter from her and whether he could tell her something he remembered of his own life without prompting. She had not called him her husband.

He sat with the page for a long while. At last he said, "I remember the smell of wet wool in the barracks. I remember a man called Orris who owed me three pennies. He was my father. I remember my sister taking the larger piece of bread when she thought I had already eaten."

Yara did not write the memories down. He had not asked her to.

"Would you answer?" she asked.

"Yes. But not tonight. I want to write it myself, and I want it to be a letter, not a statement for the court."

She folded Leora's page back into his keeping. His own earlier letter had only a first road-house mark; it had not reached his sister. No one could promise that the woman at its old address would know him, or that Leora would find comfort in an answer from a stranger living in her husband's body.

Luceran heard of the letter's arrival only as a receipt. He asked no one to copy it. Later, passing the eastern cistern, he asked Edselsto whether the old outlet had been built for winter water. The castle answered with the former stone course and the name of a long-dead mason. The damp timber was still drying. Living workers would have to decide whether the channel could safely be opened.

It was a correct answer to a narrow question, and Luceran felt the old relief. A human answer, by contrast, might arrive late, contradict an earlier answer or refuse to come at all.

The Dols reeve's limited reply was still on the road. No river court had accepted a new commission. No second grain wagon had crossed. In the family nave, all seven candles burned.
