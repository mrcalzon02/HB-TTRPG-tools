# Season Three, Episode 02 — The Price of a Seat

The messenger who returned from the Jorvan counting house had not brought money. He brought a receipt for the king's invitation, the name of a woman empowered to answer it, and a request that the gate books be kept where they were.

Etta read the receipt twice before she entered it. It was the third morning since the royal letter had gone out. The receiving clerk had written the hour and the condition of the seal, and had added that Mistress Othra Jorvan would travel with her own notary and two guards. Nothing in the answer promised a loan.

"Will she come to the great table?" Luceran asked.

"If the road holds," Damaris said. "She is coming to the gate first."

He did not like that. He had offered an audience with a king, and she had answered with a demand to look at his accounts. Damaris made no effort to soothe him.

"The tolls are at the gate. So are the men who collect them. She would be a fool to examine them here and a greater fool to accept our copies without seeing the originals."

Ysabet had prepared two sets of seals: one for the visiting party's safe conduct, another for the custody of the rolls. Rhyse's guard order protected the visitors from interference but did not let their servants enter the watch rooms. A creditor could be invited without becoming a member of the household.

Beyond the window, rain worked its way along the receiving-yard gutter. A carpenter shouted at the men moving timber from the wet side of the shed. Luceran could hear the labor through the closed shutters.

"Have we heard from Dols?"

"Not from Senna or Harven," Etta said. "Nor from the reeve."

He almost asked her whether the witnesses were deliberately delaying. She had a courier book, not a window into another man's intentions. He signed the safe conduct and told her to enter the answer exactly as received.

There were still six sacks at Edselsto. The other six remained counted at the northern posts. The road between them had not improved because a noble house had agreed to send a woman to look at figures.

---

Hesta Vale had her mule's second shoe at last. She had paid for it with the impatience of a woman who had already lost more time than the smith could repay. Her brother waited beside a sled carrying the three smaller stones he had measured. Tomas had brought the Crown's written terms and a purse for one paid porter.

Hesta made him read the line concerning her four seasons of toll-free Crown-track use aloud. Then she asked who would answer if a wheel broke at the passing place.

"The Crown for damage caused by its ordered trial," Tomas said, "after the wheel and the place are inspected by both parties. Not for an old split you bring with you."

"That is a sentence a carter can live with."

Bram stood at the side of the yard, his injured fingers wrapped in a clean strip. He could count loads but could not safely guide a loaded wheel against a stone lip. Hesta had brought Lenn, the replacement porter, and insisted he receive the first day's wage before the cart moved. Tomas counted the coins into Lenn's palm while the Crown mason measured the first block.

The stone passed the receiving-yard turn with a finger's breadth to spare. On the second block, the sled's left runner sank into a soft rut. Hesta stopped the mule before her brother could urge it forward. The mason took the measure again, this time with the sled sitting where the weight had actually carried it.

"That one will wait," he said.

Her brother looked at the block as though the stone had betrayed him. Hesta told him that stone was better at keeping promises than men, provided no one made the promises on its behalf.

The mason accepted the first block for the yard's drain work and entered the weight. The second remained on the sled for a later correction; the third had not been tested. No one claimed that Dols had accepted a wall, a crossing, or even these stones. Lenn received the balance of his trial wage, and Tomas signed the measured result in the presence of Hesta's brother.

"The king will want to know whether we can carry grain back the other way," Tomas said.

"The king may know we have moved one stone around a bend," Hesta replied. "The rest costs another day's work."

She turned the mule toward her quarry. The paid trial had established a useful limit, not a road that could be written open by royal order.

---

Mistress Othra Jorvan arrived with mud to her knees and no court gown in her baggage. She was neither the eldest nor the richest member of her house. Her authority lay in a letter bearing the house seal and naming the two market gates, the greatest advance she might make, and the term beyond which she could promise nothing without fresh instructions.

Ysabet read the delegation beside the gate keeper, Bessa, and Jorvan's notary. Each marked the same date. Othra allowed the letter to be copied, then took it back. She had not been sent to give away her family's right to refuse.

The gate books smelled of leather, soot and the damp that came through the east arch in heavy rain. Bessa had carried them herself from the locked chest. She would not let Othra turn a page until she had shown the broken corner on the second week's tally and the stitch that kept it attached.

"That is not an alteration," Bessa said. "The cartmen knocked the book from the ledge. I had it sewn before the ink could be lost."

Othra examined the stitching, then asked for the duplicate tally kept by the other gate officer. The numbers agreed. She made a small mark on her own sheet and moved on.

The third week's book included a four-penny refund of an improperly collected wheel fee. Bessa showed Hesta Vale's signed acknowledgment beside the correction. Othra did not call the refund a weakness. She asked instead how many such refunds might be entered after the week had closed, and who was empowered to authorize them.

"By petition and witnessed correction," Bessa said. "Not by rubbing out a sum because a lord dislikes it."

Othra's mouth moved at one corner. "Then we agree about rubbing things out."

She was less pleased by the night-watch charges. Two men had been paid for an additional watch after a damaged hinge left the east postern vulnerable. Their wages were proper, but they reduced what could be promised to a lender. Othra asked whether the Crown could order those wages deferred.

"It could order the men to leave," Bessa answered. "Then your clerk may come and collect tolls from whoever holds the gate."

Damaris, who had remained silent through most of the inspection, set a finger beside the charge. "The watch is paid before surplus exists. So are the gate's necessary repairs and the clerks who make these books possible."

"Then you offer us the remainder of an uncertain sum," Othra said.

"We offer you the chance to see exactly how uncertain."

The factor closed the book without taking it from Bessa's hands. For the first time that day, she seemed interested in the Crown's refusal as much as its need.

---

Jalen had asked the carpenter to bring the brace before noon. It was fitted with two plain pegs and a strap that could be loosened without another person's permission. Isera watched him transfer his weight, then asked him to do it again after resting. His body did not heal because the furniture had improved. His shoulders could nevertheless be spared a little of their daily work.

Miren had brought a thicker strip of cloth for the edge. She did not ask whether he wished to be shown at the royal hearing. He had heard enough of the arrangements to ask first.

"Will they put me at the table to prove the king tells the truth now?"

"Not if you do not want it," she said.

"Good. I should like him to learn that without seeing me do it."

He was not smiling. Miren sat on the stool while the carpenter adjusted the strap. Jalen kept his hands on the chair arms until the work was done. Then he reached for the carved horse the Dols apprentice had given him and placed it on the writing board where he could see it.

Yara came with the ordinary courier ledger. Jalen's private letter to the possible address of his sister had been handed on at the first road house; there was a dated receipt and nothing more. No answer had returned. Leora's separately dispatched letter had not reached Edselsto. Yara's reports to Merehaven and Teth had no confirmed receipt either, and she would not borrow a foreign warrant to hurry a private letter.

"Does the road house know what is inside?" Jalen asked.

"It knows who paid for the carriage and where the letter was sent."

"That will do."

After Yara left, Miren checked the cloth once more. Jalen studied her face with a soldier's old habit of watching what people withheld.

"You needn't tell him I was grateful," he said.

"I wasn't going to."

"You are kind to me."

"That is not a message to him either."

He looked at the horse, then at the chair that finally let him sit without being held upright. The distinction pleased him. Miren could help a man for his own sake and still refuse the king. He could be grateful without becoming anyone's argument.

---

At the afternoon audience Othra laid out two sheets, not one. The first was the tally of what the east market gate had yielded after ordinary expenses in the last three reckonings. The second named the money her house would risk and the means by which it expected to be repaid.

Luceran looked for the sum first. It was smaller than he had hoped.

"Twenty-four silver marks will not reopen the Dols crossing."

"No," Othra said. "They may secure a carter when a lawful crossing exists. I am not lending you a bridge."

Damaris had already examined the figure. It could meet the quoted deposit for a replacement team, pay the porter for the necessary Crown-side work and preserve enough for a portion of the receiving-yard drain. It could not feed the northern posts indefinitely. Nor would it restore the eighteen withdrawn patrol mounts.

Othra wanted twenty-seven marks repaid from the east gate's surplus over twelve market days. She demanded a house observer beside the gate tallies, able to witness the counting and retain a duplicate, and a written right to seek the unpaid balance if the period ended short. Her first draft had placed her claim ahead of the watch and repair charges.

"No," Damaris said.

Othra turned to the king. "I have already removed the second gate."

"And you shall not have the first gate's guards dismissed to pay you," Luceran said.

The factor studied him. He could not tell whether she admired the answer or was measuring how much longer he could afford it.

Ysabet set out the objection more precisely. The original rolls would remain with Bessa and the other gate keeper. The night watch, ordinary gate wages, essential hinge and wall repairs, and witnessed corrections would be paid before any Jorvan claim. Othra's observer could count and attest but could neither command the guard nor collect a toll. The loan would create no claim on the second gate, Hesta's road privilege, or the three river jurisdictions.

"And if the king changes his mind after taking the coin?" Othra asked.

"Then he will have broken a witnessed royal undertaking," Ysabet answered. "You may bring the copies to the gate reeve and to the royal court, and tell every other lender what the Crown's seal is worth. We cannot make him virtuous. We can make the breach legible."

Luceran felt the heat rise in his face. A king accustomed to giving orders had just been offered the threat of his own signature.

Othra asked for an additional term: if the twelve market days did not discharge the debt, her house would have first claim on half the remaining net surplus for four further market days, up to the unpaid balance only. No right to seize the gate, no continuing levy afterward. Damaris calculated the burden while the chamber waited. The clause was ugly, but it was bounded. A longer failure would leave a claim in court rather than a private Jorvan tax.

"Your house will have a seat at our gate," Luceran said.

"A stool," Othra corrected. "At the counting ledge. My clerk will pay for his meals. Your clerk will have to work while someone watches him."

Bessa, who had been asked to stand as a witness, spoke before anyone could silence her. "Then put in writing that he may not hold up the queue to settle an argument."

Othra considered that and nodded. "Agreed."

The king looked at Damaris. She did not smile. She did not advise him to pretend the price was lower.

"Let the words be written," he said.

---

Othra did not go straight to supper. She walked the east gate with Bessa and asked where the observer might sit without blocking carts or hearing the guard's private orders. Bessa showed her a narrow ledge under the arch, dry only when the wind came from the south.

"Your clerk will hate it," Bessa said.

"He will be paid to hate it."

Othra's younger brother had once believed lending to kings was the easiest kind of trade. A king could always tax someone, he had said. Then a provincial governor had refused a collection order, and the family had spent two winters arguing over whose seal was older. Othra had not forgotten the cost of that lesson. She did not need Luceran to be a good man. She needed his people to be able to count and his promises to survive the hour after he made them.

Damaris joined them with the revised sheet. The night-watch charge was protected. The extra four-market-day remedy was limited to half the net surplus, and the house observer's seat ended with the debt. The Crown would pay its own duplicate clerk and the witnesses' fee; Jorvan would pay its observer. There was no automatic renewal.

"Why did you come yourself?" Damaris asked.

"Because I was told your king had learned to tell the truth after it became expensive."

"And you wanted to see whether that made him safer to lend to?"

"I wanted to see whether anyone around him would stop him making a promise he could not keep."

Damaris folded the paper. "There are several of us. It has not always been enough."

Othra looked through the arch at the carts turning in the mud. The castle rose above them, black and magnificent. Its towers could not make a wet wheel turn or a disputed toll lawful.

"Then I shall lend against what you can count," she said, "and leave the king's conscience out of the security."

Neither woman mistook that for friendship. It was a useful beginning between people who expected to disagree again.

---

The agreement was signed at the gate, where the books and the people responsible for them were present. Etta read the terms aloud. Ysabet checked each seal. Bessa and the second gate keeper witnessed the original toll custody, while Othra's notary attested the counted coin and the limited delegation under which she acted. Rhyse stood near the door with living guards, not with an order for Edselsto to judge anyone's honesty.

Othra opened a small iron-bound coffer. The paymaster counted twenty-four silver marks, examined them, and entered the sum as a Jorvan advance, not royal revenue. The repayment was capped at twenty-seven marks: first claim on the east market gate's surplus for twelve market days after ordinary watch, repair, clerk and correction charges; if short, half of the remaining net surplus for no more than four further market days, and only up to the unpaid balance. After that, any unpaid claim would require an ordinary petition. The observer could watch the tally, never take the tolls or command the gate. The second gate remained unpledged.

Luceran signed last. He understood the insult of permitting a creditor's man to sit beside his own keeper. He also understood the greater insult of promising grain he could not move and calling the promise sovereign will.

Tomas took the paymaster's witnessed note and went to seek the available replacement carter. The coin had arrived; the horses had not. No Dols wall had been accepted, no northern passage opened, and no second Merehaven advance granted. Hesta's one-block trial remained exactly that. The receiving-yard drain could now be paid for in part, not declared repaired.

After the witnesses dispersed, Luceran found Miren at the small table. She was sewing the remaining cloth for Jalen's chair.

"We have borrowed," he told her.

"I heard the counting."

"It cost us a place at our own gate."

"Then keep the gate worth watching."

He asked whether she would stay when she finished the seam. She touched the cloth between her fingers, judging whether it would rub a wounded shoulder.

"I shall take this to Jalen," she said. "After that I shall sleep."

Luceran let her go. It was not a punishment she had devised for his borrowing. Her answer had its own reasons, and he was beginning, unwillingly, to understand the difference.

He passed the eastern cistern on his way to the family nave. The mason had left the damp timber stacked to dry; Edselsto could still remember the old mark, but it could not make the wood sound or the Jorvan debt disappear. When he asked the castle whether the eastern stone had always been so cold, it answered with the age of the wall and the old winter courses. It gave him something true and small, without a price and without an argument.

At the gate below, Bessa was clearing a place for a stranger's stool.

All seven candles burned.
