# Season Three, Episode 05 — Reopening Night

The first guest to enter the great hall was a chimney sweep.

He came down from the west flue black to the elbows and told the mason that the old pointing had opened above the throat. Edselsto had remembered the date of the repair, but not the present crack. Luceran asked the mason whether the castle might be mistaken.

"About what was done? Perhaps not. About what will hold another fire? I should prefer to look."

The east hearth was safe after its ironwork was reset. The west could be pointed and tested again before evening. The third would remain cold. The cook crossed two hot dishes from the supper she had never promised, then asked for bread from the village baker, dry wood, borrowed kettles and two additional pairs of hands for the washing afterward.

Damaris brought the household book. The Sorelle players required a road allowance before unloading and wages at the end of the performance. Three guest chambers had dried enough for use; the others would stay locked. The hall watch could be covered by paid additional duty, but no man would be withdrawn from the outer posts.

"We can seat thirty-six," she said. "We cannot restore the whole court with thirty-six suppers."

"Where will the money come from?"

She showed him the separate household reserve, not the Jorvan advance and not the east-gate surplus pledged to its repayment. The reserve had been kept for winter linen and minor roof work. Those needs would remain, but their money would be spent.

Luceran signed the limited allowance. The cook accepted the paper without thanking him. She had people to hire before the bread was ready.

---

Hesta Vale had declined the invitation. A quarrywoman could enjoy music as much as anyone, but her brother needed her at dawn to judge whether the chipped drain block could be cut to a shorter course.

Orlen, a Morcant factor who supplied iron brackets and sometimes hired a cart for stone, had not declined. He stood before the posted first-refusal agreement and read it aloud to Tomas, as though the words might become less objectionable if spoken slowly.

"Twelve accepted loads. Thirty days. She gets the first offer."

"And can refuse within one daylight day," Tomas said. "Then we may buy elsewhere."

"That isn't the same as being asked to bid."

"No," Hesta said. "It is what I bargained for."

Orlen did not call the contract fraudulent. He had seen its witnesses and knew the second block had been rejected by a living mason. His complaint was that a public preference could be perfectly lawful and still leave another supplier outside the first opportunity. He gave Tomas a written price for gutter brackets and demanded a dated receipt. If his iron was refused, he intended to learn what the Crown paid instead.

Hesta's brother later met Orlen at the village baker's bench. They argued about charcoal, then agreed that the next Crown purchase ought to be discussed by several suppliers before anyone accepted a private offer. Two other haulers were invited to meet them after market day.

There was no conspiracy in the arrangement. There was a bakery with a table, a grievance, and four people who would rather know the price before somebody else set it.

---

Jalen heard the musicians testing their strings below his chamber. A window stood between him and the hall, and for once that was an advantage. Nobody could point to him as the king's returned miracle if he was not in the room.

Yara brought the physicians' two separate observations and a proposed request from Dols. A clerk had softened the language of the request until it sounded almost like an invitation to help.

"Does it still ask for the whole examination?" Jalen said.

"Yes."

"Then I still refuse."

He had read the observations. He would consider particular questions later, if he chose, but he would not send the entire record or permit it to become an answer to Corven's identity. Yara wrote his decision in his presence. His private reply to Leora had left Edselsto without a delivery acknowledgment. The earlier letter seeking his sister and original service papers still had only its first road-house receipt. Neither silence told him what the recipients thought.

Miren came to check the cloth beneath his shoulder. She had arranged another attendant for the evening and meant to attend the supper as a guest.

"Go," he said when she hesitated. "I can hear the music without seeing everyone dance."

"That sounds suspiciously like an order."

"Advice, then. The sort you can refuse."

Yara opened his window a little. The players found the right note together, lost it, and laughed. Jalen smiled at a sound that had nothing to do with his body.

---

The Sorelle company arrived in a wagon with a loose wheel. Its leader, Neris, inspected the room offered to her musicians before she permitted the instruments inside. She refused the damp chamber and chose a smaller dry one. The paymaster counted the road allowance before a witness. Neris counted it again.

"I have played for men who promised twice this much," she told a younger player. "A promise will not mend your fiddle."

The hall looked handsome in lamplight without looking new. Fresh rushes covered worn stone, autumn branches softened the pillars, and a curtain concealed the cold hearth. One bucket stood beneath a roof leak beyond the guests' tables. The bread was warm and the village brewer's casks were good.

At the east gate Bessa collected the musicians' ordinary wheel fee. Jorvan's house-paid observer copied the figure from his allotted stool. He neither inspected invitations nor asked to approve the entertainment. His authority extended to witnessed tallying, not the pleasures of a royal household.

Etta checked arrivals by name against the limited invitations. A Pell cousin wanted a room for his widowed aunt and learned that the dry chambers were already promised. He found a village lodging through an acquaintance instead. Orlen came in a borrowed collar and spent the first course discussing iron with a Kaelmont road clerk.

Luceran entered to find people talking over one another about horses, sick relatives, the price of wool and the merits of the brewer's ale. For a few breaths nobody noticed him. He was astonished by how much he had missed that noise.

Then Neris struck the opening measure, and the guests turned toward their king.

---

There was genuine pleasure in the hall. A visiting factor made Damaris laugh by losing count in a dance. Bram, still unable to guide a heavy wheel, came in a borrowed coat and sat where he could rest his injured leg. The Sorelle players refused a request for a victory march because they had brought no trumpet and had promised three dances, not a pageant of royal glory.

Miren arrived after her sickroom duties, wearing no jewel that could be mistaken for a gift from Luceran. She praised the bread when they met beside the cups.

"I had hoped you would enjoy yourself," he said.

"I am."

She went to speak with Yara, and later danced with her in a country measure that made them both laugh at their missteps. Luceran felt the old jealousy and recognized it as his own. She was not making a public statement about him. She was enjoying an evening with someone she trusted.

He danced instead with an elderly guest who had once corrected his table manners when his parents were alive. She made him lead properly and informed him that the hall had been swept better than she expected. He laughed, and for a moment the room seemed a place where he could be both king and ordinary man.

Orlen watched the dancing with his Kaelmont acquaintance. The clerk wanted the mountain road inspected before another heavy wagon attempted the wet turn. Orlen wanted the next gutter-iron offer considered without another preference granted in advance. They had separate reasons to speak again.

No one left the evening forgiven of every grievance. Several left having enjoyed it.

---

A young nobleman noticed the narrow service door beside the hall. He had heard that Edselsto sometimes yielded at a named Doorwarden's command and sometimes required its living keeper to turn the latch. He decided the castle preferred noble voices and offered a silver penny to anyone who could prove it.

A kitchen boy carrying empty jugs was urged to try. He looked at the coin, then at the waiting guests. He had been sent for water, not hired to entertain them.

Rhyse crossed the room.

"The passage is under the Doorwardens," he said. "Let him do his work."

"It is only a game."

"Then play it without risking a servant's place."

The nobleman pocketed his coin. A Doorwarden opened the passage by its ordinary latch. The door had proved nothing about breeding, intention or truth. The boy went for water, though the laughter followed him down the stair.

A younger Pell cousin whispered that next time they ought to try one of the silver-marked listening ways. Rhyse heard and told his lieutenant to keep the existing threshold rules. He could stop one humiliation; he could not stop the court from remembering an interesting possibility.

Outside, Orlen and the Kaelmont clerk continued their conversation by the timber stacks. Their voices were beyond the hall's ordinary hearing. Neither had asked the castle for permission to talk.

Luceran later asked Edselsto when the west stair had last been repaired. It supplied an old steward's date. He sent a living guard to inspect a loose tread reported by a guest. The house's answer was useful. It was not a substitute for looking.

---

After midnight, the washerwomen gathered cloth while the hired kitchen hands carried away greasy pans. Neris received the agreed wages in front of a witness and paid her players before they slept. A borrowed kettle had split at its handle and would need repair. The west hearth required another daylight inspection. The hall guards had earned rest before their next turn.

Damaris entered the charges. The supper had been paid from the household reserve; winter linen and minor roof repairs now had less money behind them. The Jorvan loan remained a debt on its original terms. The east-gate tally had not been turned into a banquet purse. No additional drain block had been accepted. No Dols mason had approved the damaged wall.

"Was it worth it?" Luceran asked.

"Ask the people who were paid," Damaris answered. "Then ask the people whose repairs must wait."

Miren had returned to the sickroom before the last dance. Jalen had heard the music without being exhibited. Orlen and the road clerk had found useful business outside the hall. The king could not claim any of them had become his possession because he had offered a pleasant evening.

At the family nave all seven ancestral candles burned. Luceran stood listening to the distant scrape of benches being moved for cleaning. For several hours the hall had been beautiful and full of living people.

Tomorrow those people would still have their own work, their own desires, and their own prices.
