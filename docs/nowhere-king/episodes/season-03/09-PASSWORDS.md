# Season Three, Episode 09 — Passwords

The trouble with a password, Dain discovered, was that everyone wanted to know it.

By breakfast, a visiting cousin had been asked his name twice between the east gate and the hall. The porter sent to fetch a witness had missed the unloading of two carts. A guard at the service turn had begun asking returning kitchen hands what they had eaten that morning.

Rhyse found the guard facing Tavin across a bucket of hot water.

“What did you eat?” the guard repeated.

“Bread,” Tavin said.

“What sort?”

“The sort that goes hard when you leave it standing to answer questions.”

The bucket was heavy. The boy held the iron handle in both hands because it was hot. Rhyse asked who had sent him and who would receive the water. The guard knew both answers: the cook and Merel's assistant, at the outer nave door.

“Have you reason to think he is not Tavin?”

“After the candle, sir—”

“After the candle, one wick went out. It did not give you leave to hold the kitchens hostage.”

He let Tavin go and took the guard aside. The man had not invented his question. Alden Pell had told him the houses wanted proof that people returning through Edselsto were themselves, and that a diligent guard ought to be ready.

“Alden Pell does not command this turn,” Rhyse said.

“He said a good guard would have an answer.”

“A good guard would know whose order he was obeying.”

Tavin did not thank Rhyse. Rhyse was learning that a boy made repeatedly into an object of amusement might prefer not to be rescued in public every morning.

Alden appeared with a folded paper. Two noble households, he explained, had devised questions for returning visitors. One asked the name of a dead nurse; the other required a man to expose an old wound. Alden had written them with care. His eagerness was real, and so was his fear of welcoming an impostor.

“A man might hear a nurse's name from a cousin,” Rhyse said. “A scar proves a scar. Neither gives a stranger authority to strip a guest in the passage.”

“Would you let anyone through?”

“I would have the officer who invited him answer for the invitation. I would compare the name to the entry and ask for a living witness who knows him. If something disagrees, we keep him somewhere decent until it can be settled. That is work, not certainty.”

“And if the witness lies?”

“Then we have a lying witness whose name we know.”

Alden took the paper back. He had hoped to be praised for anticipating danger and instead discovered he had proposed work he would never be required to perform himself.

Dain opened a page in the watch book for delayed entries, who had ordered them and whose labor was lost. The first line concerned the cousin. The second concerned the porter.

“That is a fine way to make enemies,” Alden said.

“Writing down whose idea it was?” Rhyse asked.

“Yes.”

“Then the book will have a useful purpose.”

Yara found Jalen with his correspondence spread across a board on his chair. His handwriting had changed after his injury. The lines took more effort than they once had, but the words belonged to him.

“Someone has suggested a test,” she said.

“For the candle?”

“For you.”

He set the letter down. “A test of what?”

“Whether you are Jalen.”

“That is a larger question than they think.”

A household visitor had heard that families were devising private challenges. If Jalen supplied a childhood answer known to Leora, the visitor argued, a copy might be sent to Dols and spare everyone the indignity of doubt. The visitor had no authority from Leora, had not seen Jalen's restricted medical observations and possessed no acknowledgment that Jalen's own reply had reached her.

“Who would hold the answer?” Jalen asked.

“A clerk, presumably.”

“Which clerk?”

“He did not say.”

“And if Leora remembers it differently?”

“Then they will have invented a dispute they did not possess yesterday.”

He had spent too long being spoken of as a casualty, a body and a question of recognition. His sister's possible doubt did not offend him. The notion that strangers could demand pieces of their childhood did.

“I want her to know I am alive,” he said. “I want her to know what happened. That is not the same as wanting a room full of men to know what we called each other when we were small.”

“Shall I send a refusal?”

“Send nothing new. My letter has not been acknowledged. If she wishes to ask me something, she may ask me.”

“She may never ask.”

“She may not believe me when I answer.”

Yara did not comfort him with a lie. “Then you will have the trouble of persuading her.”

“I know.”

He looked toward the folded draft. An official could be met with argument. His sister might read every word and still mourn the brother she remembered. Yara saw him shift against the chair brace, trying not to show discomfort.

“The physicians' pages?” he asked.

“Remain restricted by your consent. Dols may submit particular questions. It has not received the whole record.”

“Good. I do not ask you to make everyone believe me.”

He put down the pen he had lifted. Instead of adding to the letter, he asked whether the attendant who had stayed late when the nave filled with frightened visitors had been paid. Yara did not know. She promised to find out, which was a promise she could actually keep.

Miren met Luceran outside the south-gallery withdrawing room. He waited on the public side of the door whose silver connection had been physically removed after he ordered Edselsto to repeat her private conversation.

His courtesy irritated her, because she knew he hoped she would notice it. He had learned to leave some doors closed. He had not learned to stop hoping she would reward him for it.

“Rhyse says the houses are setting private questions,” Luceran began.

“They have been frightened since the nave.”

“Some were frightened before.”

“Some have reason.”

“A question known only to husband and wife might be useful.”

“It might teach the servants which marriages have secrets worth stealing.”

“I did not say we should use it.”

“You came to ask whether I thought it useful.”

“I came because you might see what I am missing.”

That was nearer the truth. Advice could contradict him, and it could be withheld.

“You know what you would have asked me a year ago,” she said.

“A question no one else could answer.”

“And if I refused?”

He took his time. “I might have told myself refusal proved something.”

“It would have proved that I refused.”

He laughed unhappily. “That is what Rhyse said about the scar.”

“Rhyse is often right about doors.”

“Less often about hearts.”

“Only because he does not pretend to command them.”

The remark hurt more than she intended. She regretted the cruelty without withdrawing the truth.

“I am not asking you for a private answer,” he said.

“Then ask me what you are asking.”

“Should I forbid the tests?”

“No. A mother may question a stranger who comes to take her child. An envoy may distrust a broken seal. A lover may ask something remembered by two people. You cannot forbid fear. You can prevent your officers from making private questions into public tolls.”

Luceran remembered the stolen seal wax and practice impression from the earlier inquiry. Seals could be copied. Questions could be overheard. Witnesses could be bribed, mistaken or tired. Each safeguard had a cost, often borne by the person least able to refuse.

“The castle might remember a voice in a connected way,” he said.

“A voice. You know the difference.”

He did, and disliked it. Edselsto had warned him where a knife moved. It had not told him who sent the man or what he intended. Nor could its bounded hearing know an off-wall meeting or a conversation in this disconnected room.

“I will speak to Rhyse.”

“As a captain, not a magician.”

She opened the door. He did not follow. She was glad, and resented having to be glad.

“Luceran,” she said. He turned. “I came because I wanted to see you. That does not answer the other things.”

“I know.”

She believed he meant it. Believing him did not yet make him safe to trust.

Damaris found Bessa at the east gate disputing three rumors: the king had commanded every visitor to utter a secret name; the Doorwardens could distinguish living flesh from dead by touch; and no one could leave without a priest's permission. None was true.

The Jorvan observer asked whether the new precautions would reduce the pledged net. Damaris told him that actual gate costs belonged in the gate account, while hall and nave watch belonged elsewhere. His right to inspect deductions did not make him master of the guards.

“The twenty-four-mark advance, twenty-seven-mark ceiling, twelve market days and limited four-day tail remain,” she said. “We shall count real expenses, not rename them.”

Outside, the hauler who had paid for a covered yard still argued with his partner about taking iron uphill. The road was not closed, the lower stair was not the road, and neither man could afford many more idle nights. Orlen's revised quotation remained a quotation. Hesta's first-refusal right had not evaporated. The third drain block remained untested.

Dain sent a note recording the time lost to the new challenges. It contained no impostors apprehended; none had been proved. Rhyse's narrow instruction followed: guards were to record invitations, witnesses and delays, not compel servants to surrender family secrets in order to reach assigned working rooms. Households wishing private challenges could arrange them at their own expense.

“That will disappoint people,” Bessa said.

“Certainty would cost more,” Damaris answered.

She folded the note beside the unpaid workers' wages and the nave-watch hours. The independent mason's measurements had not yet reached her, and she would not purchase unmeasured bracing timber by wish. The upper-gallery hangings remained deferred; the winter roof and linen still needed money.

Above the gate, six candles burned beside the one that had gone dark. None of the new questions made the seventh flame return.

The second mason brought his own line, his own weights, and an apprentice instructed not to look at Vess's figures.

Vess met them at the barred lower stair before the yard had warmed. Pell Falbrecht waited with Tomas and the worker who had bruised his heel when the passage trembled. The man could bear weight now, but he did not bear it easily.

“Where shall I hang the line?” the mason asked.

“From stone we know has not moved,” Vess said.

The mason studied the landing. “Which stone is that?”

Vess had no answer he liked. They chose an iron staple two landings above and checked its mortar. The apprentice wrote down each reading before either master compared the figures. At the lower turn, the new line passed a finger's breadth from yesterday's mark.

“So the wall moved,” Pell said.

“Or our points differ,” the mason replied. “You asked me to measure. I cannot make your fear into a fact.”

The injured worker shifted his foot. “Can we go back to work?”

“Not down there,” Vess said.

“We were told another mason would settle it.”

“He has measured it,” Pell said, with more patience than he felt. “That is not the same as settling it.”

Tomas could put two men in the upper yard carrying clean stone. The man with the bruised heel might count pieces from a seat if the physician approved his return, but Pell would not pretend that counting was a mason's task merely to reduce wages owed for the stoppage. He told Tomas to enter the difference in the works account.

The visiting mason wanted another reading after a dry night, with a second reference point checked independently. Vess agreed. He disliked needing another man's doubt and liked even less the thought of sending a laborer down the stair because the Crown had already paid for an inspection.

“Will you sign the passage safe?” Pell asked.

“No.”

“Will you sign what you measured?”

The mason held out his hand for the book. “That I can do.”

Beyond the bar the stair remained dark. Nothing moved while they watched it. The workers would be paid for waiting; the stone had offered no answer in return.

At the east gate the trouble began with two cloaks drying beside the same stove.

One hauler's was faded brown. The other's was a brown that had once been blue. In the morning each took the nearer garment. Neither noticed until a guard demanded the name entered against the brown cloak's tally and received a different one.

“I've been myself since breakfast,” the hauler said. “I was myself when I paid for the yard.”

“You have another man's tally.”

“I have another man's cloak.”

The guard had heard Alden Pell's ideas about private challenges, though Rhyse had forbidden treating noble advice as a watch order. The man knew the distinction. He was frightened enough by the stories from the nave to wish for a test stronger than wet wool. He held both haulers to one side and sent for Bessa.

By the time she arrived, Orlen Morcant had come to ask why the iron carriage had not moved. Hesta's brother had come for an unrelated quarry measurement, and he objected when someone called the delay a failure of his family's first-refusal right. Two men began arguing over a cart that neither owned.

Bessa sent for the covered-yard keeper. He knew which hauler had paid for which place and had watched them unhitch their teams. He had not watched them change cloaks. The guard had seen the tallies in their hands but not their issue. Bessa set the receipts beside the gate book and corrected the copied entry without scraping away the original.

“Then you know which is which,” the guard said.

“I know who paid the yard keeper and who came through my gate,” Bessa answered. “I know men can put on the wrong coats. I do not know what lies in their souls, and neither do you.”

The younger hauler had lost the hour in which he might have taken a load at the quoted rate. Orlen offered to hold his iron price until the next market day if the carrier paid for another night's shelter. The carrier declined. A fixed price without a paid cart was not much of a favor. Hesta's brother refused to let either man call the stoppage a failure of the quarry's lawful preference.

The Jorvan observer watched Bessa enter the delay and its correction. He asked whether the guard's time would be charged against the pledged gate net.

“He was on gate duty,” she said. “The noble questions belong to whichever house asks them. We shall count the hours actually worked, not rename them.”

She could not yet say who would bear the hauler's lost hour. No delivery contract had been made; no royal road closure had been ordered. The loss existed without a convenient debtor.

The haulers exchanged cloaks in front of the guard, who looked as though he would have preferred a miracle.

Alden Pell had scarcely finished hearing about the gate when the steward of his cousin's guest chamber refused to admit him.

“Who taught you your letters?” the steward asked.

Alden stared. “What?”

“Your cousin says the answer will prove you.”

“My mother hired three tutors. Which one does he want?”

“The first.”

“The first could scarcely keep a sober morning. I remember the third.”

The steward had known Alden since childhood. That was what made the exchange intolerable. The man did not doubt him. He was obeying a cousin who had ordered everyone questioned and was now entertaining two guests inside.

“You know me,” Alden said.

“I do.”

“Then open the door.”

“If I do, he will say I have made his precaution worthless.”

Alden had written precautions like this himself. He had imagined an impostor defeated by a clever memory. He had not imagined a familiar servant who could lose his place for disobedience, or a guest who honestly could not recall which of three men had first taught him a letter.

The steward sent for the cousin. A servant waited with a tray of hot cups while the cousin took his time. By the time he appeared, the cups had cooled and the servant had missed the hour when she usually took bread to her mother in the village.

The cousin laughed at Alden's anger. “It was a jest.”

“It was your order.”

“A harmless one.”

Alden looked at the woman holding the tray. She was not smiling. He took a coin from his purse and offered it for the lost errand. She asked whether the cousin would permit her to leave. Alden turned to him, and for once the question he asked was not clever.

“Will you?”

The cousin waved her away. The steward opened the door.

Alden went in, but the triumph he had expected from devising a test had become a small, sour thing. He could not buy back the woman's hour. He could not make Tavin trust him by suddenly noticing that other people had work.

He told the steward not to ask that question again. The steward replied that the cousin, not Alden, had issued the instruction.

“Then I shall speak to him,” Alden said.

He had thought giving the correct answer would be the difficult part. He was beginning to learn how much trouble a person could cause by asking a question he would never have to answer himself.

Merel's assistant had made two copies of the nave testimony and spoiled a third.

He held the spoiled sheet toward the brazier, but the canon caught his wrist before the edge reached the coals.

“Why are you burning it?”

“I wrote that the rail shook for three breaths. In my first account I wrote two.”

“Then we have two accounts.”

“I was there. Surely I ought to know.”

Merel took the sheet from him. The boy had slept poorly and had been asked the same question by six visitors before the noon bell. In the morning he had remembered the tremor as a single blow; after hearing the mason's account, he had begun to remember a rumbling before it. Neither recollection was proof of what had occurred beneath the floor.

A lady from one of the houses waited outside with a clerk and a list of questions. She wanted the witnesses named in the household hall so that every family might test their answers separately. The clerk was not unkind. He had brought his own paper and offered to pay the assistant for his time, as if that disposed of the matter.

“May I speak with the boy?” the lady asked when Merel received her.

“You may ask me for his account.”

“I should prefer to hear it from his own mouth.”

“You have his written account. He will be here at the evening office, and he has already spoken under witness.”

“But if someone had taken his place—”

“Then you would need grounds to believe it. He has not disappeared between the vestry and this table.”

She looked past Merel to the assistant. “You need not fear a simple question.”

The boy flushed. Merel had been a young priest once, proud enough to answer every challenge and too inexperienced to recognize when an invitation had become a demand.

“He need not fear it,” Merel said. “He need not answer it either.”

The lady objected that the seven lights belonged to the royal family and the realm, not to a canon's private keeping. On that point he agreed with her. He showed her the public notice: the first candle had gone dark before witnesses; the other six burned; the wick had not been touched. The hour was given as the watch bell had been heard. Neither the names nor the unharmonized testimony were posted for the amusement of every frightened visitor.

“I cannot tell my household why it happened,” she said.

“Neither can I.”

“Then what use are your records?”

“If tomorrow someone claims we put out all seven ourselves, we can say what we saw. That is useful enough for today.”

When she had gone, the assistant asked whether the spoiled sheet belonged in the record. Merel laid it beneath the other two, with the hour of its writing. He would rather preserve an honest change of memory than teach a frightened young man to make his recollections match.

Yara found the attendant Jalen had asked about behind the sickroom, trying to wash a stain from the cuff of her gown.

Her name was Fera. She had stayed late when frightened visitors filled the passages after the candle went out, and had remained when the other attendant went to fetch a physician. The sickroom clerk said the nave should bear the extra hours because the news had caused the disturbance. The nave clerk said Fera had never worked in the nave. Both men had written nothing.

"I was paid for my ordinary watch," Fera said. "Not the rest."

"Who asked you to remain?"

"The household officer. He was standing here when the visitors came. He knows."

"Then we shall ask him."

Fera twisted the damp cuff. "My sister is taking my morning watch. I owe her the hours she covered last week. If this watch goes unpaid, I cannot ask her to take another."

Yara knew the embarrassed little smile with which the woman finished. It was the expression of someone who had been taught that requesting payment for work made the work less generous.

She brought Fera to Damaris instead of sending another note between clerks.

The steward had two books open and a third balanced upon a pile of folded accounts. She heard Fera's account, sent for the sickroom slate, and compared the name of the summoning officer with the watch entry. The hours matched. The officer's order had been proper. The failure lay between the books.

"She was summoned to the sickroom," Damaris said. "The sickroom belongs to the household. We cannot charge a nurse's wages to the temple because the trouble began at its door."

"Nor to the east gate," Yara said.

"Least of all to the east gate. Jorvan has a right to witness the deductions from that surplus, not to purchase the attendance of the wounded."

Fera watched the steward draw a line beneath the entry.

"Will I be paid?"

"Your hours are allowed. The pay chest opens tomorrow morning. I shall sign the voucher now."

Damaris calculated the sum from the agreed rate, sanded the writing and gave the paper to Fera. It was a promise with a date and a name, not money in her hand.

"There is another thing," Fera said, lingering. "The covers are wearing through. I have mended the same two twice."

"I know we are short of linen."

"I thought you ought to know what short means before somebody sends another guest to the sickroom."

Damaris looked up. The household could not spare money it did not have, but it could at least hear from the person who would be asked to lay a patient upon torn cloth.

"Put both covers on the sickroom list. I cannot promise replacements this week."

Fera nodded. At the door she asked whether the guards would question her again when she came to work.

"Not as a condition of reaching your assigned room," Yara said. "Rhyse has forbidden it without a named officer's authority."

"And if a guest asks?"

"You may name the officer who sent you. A guest who wants more may take his question to him."

Fera went to find her sister.

Jalen was awake when Yara returned, struggling to turn a page without letting the writing board slide from his lap.

"Was she paid?" he asked.

"She has a signed voucher for tomorrow."

"So not yet."

"Not yet."

He nodded. He had endured so many comforting assurances since his injury that the exactness of her answer seemed to please him.

"She said she could not afford another late watch," he said. "I thought perhaps they had forgotten."

"They disagreed about which room should pay."

"Did you settle it?"

"Damaris settled the account. Fera remembered the hours and who had summoned her."

"Then she proved who she was by remembering her work."

"No," Yara said. "She proved what work she did. We have been confusing those things all day."

Jalen looked down at the letter to Leora, still his own to finish. A guard's steps passed the sickroom door without stopping to demand a childhood name.

"Tomorrow," he said, "ask whether the voucher was honored."

"I will."

This time Yara wrote the promise down.

Ysabet found Luceran with Dain's watch book open beside the household accounts. He had reached the entry concerning two haulers and a pair of exchanged cloaks.

“Have we become so frightened that a man's coat is now his name?” he asked.

“Not by your order,” Ysabet said.

“Is that meant to comfort me?”

“It is meant to tell you which orders you can correct.”

Rhyse had already instructed his guards to record invitations, living witnesses and delays rather than demand household secrets. Dain's pages showed what the instruction had not prevented: a frightened guard could hold two men while seeking someone with authority to release them. The loss of an hour was written in one hand. The cost of the delay was not yet settled in any hand.

Luceran turned to the next entry. A kitchen boy had carried water to the nave under an assigned errand. A noble visitor had asked why he was not required to answer the same questions as a returning guest.

“Shall I command every house to stop?” Luceran asked.

“You may command your own watches,” Rhyse replied. “A mother questioning a stranger at her nursery door is not the same thing as a guard holding a porter who is expected at work. If you make them the same, the houses will either defy you or make a show of obeying.”

Luceran knew the first response well. The second had lately proved almost as costly.

Ysabet pointed to the account. “The house that insists upon a private challenge may pay for its own servants' time. That does not make every loss a royal debt. Nor does it let the watch turn a household request into a warrant.”

“And the hauler?”

“Bessa has recorded what she can attest. Whether the man has a claim for his lost hour is a question to be heard, not answered because you dislike the story.”

He had wanted an order that would make people recognize one another. Instead he could issue an order about what his own guards were permitted to do. It seemed a meagre return for being king.

“Write the watch instruction under my seal,” he said. “No challenge invented by a household shall become a condition of passage through a royal working gate without a named officer's authority. Keep the entries. Let the petitions come by the ordinary clerk.”

Rhyse inclined his head, satisfied with the narrowness of it. Ysabet was less pleased than she appeared. A signed order could be used against Luceran if he broke it; that was part of its value.

“The gate charges?” Luceran asked.

“Damaris will have the proper books,” Ysabet said. “She will not be grateful if you decide their sums in advance.”

He closed Dain's book. In the nave a candle remained dark. On the stair a mason would not sign a wall safe. At the gate two men had been delayed by coats that were not theirs. None of these facts had yielded the certainty people wanted. He could at least keep his own uncertainty from becoming another person's compulsory confession.
