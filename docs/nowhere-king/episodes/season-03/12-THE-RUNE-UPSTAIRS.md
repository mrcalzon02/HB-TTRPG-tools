# Season Three, Episode 12 — The Rune Upstairs

The first person to see the mark was a carpenter who had come to look at a rotten shelf.

Pell Falbrecht had sent him to the upper gallery because the men repairing the receiving-yard roof needed somewhere dry to sort the good nails from the bent ones. The hall supper had been canceled, the decorative hangings were still postponed, and the gallery's old linen cupboard had become a convenient place for the household to put things it did not want to pay to mend.

The carpenter, Bren, had been promised two hours of work. He spent the first half hour arguing with the cupboard.

“It's fixed to the wall,” he told Pell when the master came up.

“Then take the shelves out of it.”

“Not the cupboard. The wall behind it.”

Pell put his palm against the timber. The cupboard had been made with a false back of thin laths, fitted across an opening that did not appear in Vess's recent survey. A draft moved through the seam. Bren had removed one shelf and found a square of dark stone beyond.

Pell did not tell him to break the rest away.

He sent for Vess.

The surveyor arrived with his measuring cord, a folding rule, and a temper so poor that Pell knew he had been interrupted during an argument about the lower stair.

“That one is still barred,” Vess said before Pell could speak.

“I know. This is upstairs.”

“Then I have two troubles.”

He inspected the cupboard without touching the back. The laths were old enough to have taken the smell of the linen; the nails were not all of the same making. A panel had been removed once and put back. That did not tell him when, or by whom.

On the stone behind the panel someone had cut a figure about the width of a man's hand: a bent enclosing line, three short strokes, and a final little hook that seemed to turn away from the rest.

Bren had called it a letter. Pell thought it resembled a mason's setting mark. Vess said neither man had earned the right to call it anything yet.

“Can you get the panel off?” Pell asked.

“With wedges. Without striking the stone.”

“Can you say what's behind it?”

“No.”

“Can you say it's safe?”

“Not from here.”

Pell sighed. “You make a poor companion to a curious man.”

“I am an excellent companion to a living one.”

They left the cupboard standing and sent a runner for Rhyse. The carpenter was given another task and his time was entered against the gallery inspection, not concealed inside the roofers' wages. By noon, a household rumor said that a new room had appeared in Edselsto.

By noon, nobody had yet entered it.

---

Rhyse arrived with two Doorwardens and found three servants peering down the gallery from a respectable distance.

“Who told you there was a room?” he asked.

“The carpenter,” said one.

“I told Pell,” Bren protested from the far end, where he was sorting nails.

“Then somebody has a fine ear.”

Rhyse set a watchman at each end of the passage, not because he expected the cupboard to attack, but because the upper gallery was still a working route. A porter with a load of timber could do more harm to a man kneeling in a doorway than an ancestral curse whose existence nobody had established.

Vess and Pell eased the panel outward. Dust fell, and a small stone chip followed it. Pell caught the chip on his open palm and wrapped it in a scrap of clean cloth. It had come from the edge of the opening, not from the carved figure. He made Bren write that distinction in the works book.

Behind the cupboard was a short passage, perhaps two strides long, ending in a small chamber with a low stone bench and no visible window. The air was stale but not wet. A narrow opening high in the outer wall admitted enough light to show the far corner. The floor had no obvious gap. The ceiling was intact as far as they could see from the threshold.

Vess measured from the gallery side while Pell checked the jambs and the load above them. Neither entered. The outer wall should have allowed for a recess, but the depth of the space did not agree neatly with the dimensions Vess had taken from the courtyard. He had measured that wall during a rainstorm, with a scaffold in his way; he would not turn an imperfect old measurement into proof of impossible stone.

“This room isn't on your plan,” Rhyse said.

“It wasn't on my plan when I drew it.”

“Was it here then?”

“I did not see it then.”

“Those are different answers.”

“They are. Put both in the record.”

Rhyse ordered the opening guarded until a safe threshold and a proper route for measuring the exterior could be established. The lower service stair remained separately barred. Nobody would use this discovery as an excuse to pass beneath the castle, and nobody would ask a dead keeper to enter an unsafe place on a living man's behalf.

Tavin came up with water for the watch. He set the jug on the gallery table and looked at the open cupboard.

“Will I be asked to fetch things out of there?” he asked.

“Not unless it becomes a lawful kitchen store,” Rhyse said.

“Then I would prefer it stayed a mystery.”

One of the Doorwardens laughed. Rhyse did not. He had seen what happened when a nobleman's amusement became a servant's duty.

Tavin returned to the kitchens with his empty jug and a story that was smaller, and therefore more useful, than the one he had heard: a cupboard had been opened; a room had been seen; the door was guarded; nobody knew its age.

---

Ysabet did not permit the first drawing to be made on the back of a supper invitation.

The chamberlain offered her a stack of unused cards. She thanked him for the paper and refused it for the record.

“Those have already been handled by half the household,” she said. “I need sheets whose first use we can account for.”

The chamberlain asked whether the discovery justified new stationery.

“Two sheets, a clean board, and someone whose hand can be read afterward. Not a banquet.”

She summoned Neris Vell of the Copyists' Hall, who had been paid that week to check a set of worn boundary rolls. Neris had the square fingers and tired eyes of a woman who had spent more years copying other people's assertions than making her own. She was pleased to see an inscription and displeased to find a crowd waiting to watch her interpret it.

“I copy marks,” she told the gathered officers. “I do not make them tell the truth.”

Ysabet gave her the original works entry, the names of the men who had removed the panel, and a fresh leaf ruled into four parts: place, witness, method, and condition. Rhyse witnessed the transfer of the small wrapped chip into the works chest. Pell kept the key. Ysabet retained the seal, so neither could open it alone without making the act visible.

Neris set a lamp outside the opening. She would not carry a flame into the chamber until Pell had inspected the air and the ceiling. The mark was on the gallery-facing stone, accessible without crossing the threshold.

She drew it once by sight. Then she laid a thin sheet over the carving and worked charcoal lightly across it. The first impression showed the enclosing bend and two of the short strokes clearly. The third had a shallow gap that might be wear or a deliberate interruption. The hook was unmistakably crooked.

Neris marked the sheet's top edge before removing it.

A younger household copyist, appointed to make the second impression, took a fresh sheet and began at the bottom. Neris stopped him.

“Are you drawing what you see or what you expect?”

“What I see.”

“Then why have you made the hook point the other way?”

He held up his paper. He had turned the sheet over to compare it against her drawing and then copied the reverse as if it were the stone.

For an uncomfortable moment he looked ready to tear the page in half.

“Keep it,” Ysabet said.

“It is wrong.”

“Then write that it is wrong. A destroyed mistake can be made again by someone who never learned why.”

Neris wrote *reversed by transfer from back of sheet* across the copy and signed beneath it. The boy wrote his name beside hers. On a third sheet, under her eye, he made a fresh impression from the stone itself.

The two direct impressions agreed in the position of the hook and the interrupted stroke. That agreement proved that two hands could recover the same visible marks from the same surface. It did not prove who had cut them, what they meant, or when the room had been enclosed.

Neris insisted on that last sentence. Ysabet entered it in the book.

When the sheets were dry, one went under Ysabet's seal in the castle archive. The other was sealed for the Copyists' Hall, with Neris's name, the date, and a witnessed statement of where she had worked. Neither would be circulated as a royal proclamation. The mistaken reverse copy remained with the works entry, where a future reader could see how easily an apparent difference had been made.

“You will be accused of preserving an embarrassment,” Neris said.

“I have practice,” Ysabet replied.

---

Elen Quorin came up the hill after supper with mud on the hem of her riding coat and a bundle of survey stakes tied beneath her arm.

She was of House Quorin, though not of its senior branch, and had been measuring the northern roof line for Tomas. The Crown had not commissioned her to discover mysteries. Her fee covered stone and distances. She intended to keep it that way.

Vess showed her the gallery plan, the new measurements and the opening. Elen looked at the drawing before she looked at the mark.

“Your outer wall is too thick by half a foot,” she said.

“My outer wall was measured in rain.”

“Then perhaps it is the rain that has become supernatural.”

Pell grinned. Vess did not.

Elen set her stakes along the passage, sighted from the window at the end, and checked the courtyard return from two points that did not require a man to stand beneath the damaged gutter. She found that the wall was not as thick as Vess's first drawing suggested. The little chamber could fit within the corrected line, though its north corner remained difficult to account for.

“You have a room omitted from the recent plan,” she said. “Not a room proved to have appeared since the plan.”

“Exactly,” Vess said, with the air of a man receiving a compliment.

“Your first measurement was wrong.”

“Not a compliment, then.”

She inspected the carving without touching it. Neris showed her the direct impressions, the reversed copy, and the place entry. Elen laid the sheets side by side.

“Could it be a mason's mark?” Pell asked.

“It could. But why cut it on the face of a stone hidden behind a cupboard?”

“Because the cupboard came later.”

“Or the stone came from somewhere else,” Elen said. “Or the mark was once visible through an opening now covered. We do not know the order of those things.”

Neris pointed to the interrupted third stroke.

“A letter may break where the tool slipped.”

“A mason's sign may break where a line of mortar crosses it.”

“A religious device may break because the break is its meaning,” said Canon Merel, who had come up from the nave after the evening count.

Elen turned toward him. “Can you read it?”

“No.”

“Then we agree.”

“Not entirely. You think the uncertainty is a reason to leave it alone. I think it is a reason not to let the household turn it into a game.”

Merel had not come to claim a miracle. He had come because visitors were already asking whether the dark first candle had spoken through a wall. His assistant had heard the question twice at the nave door. Merel wanted a notice he could read without lying.

“The first candle remains dark,” he said. “The other six burn. I have no evidence that this mark belongs to any of them.”

“Put that on the notice,” Neris said.

Elen frowned at the sheets. “I would also like a measure of the cut's depth and the grain of the stone. Reused masonry is common in old houses. If the mark is older than the room, its position tells us very little.”

“Not with a chisel today,” Pell said. “The edge is already shedding.”

“Then with light and a fine rule, from outside the threshold.”

They agreed on that much. Elen would make an independent measured elevation. Neris would make a fresh drawing from her own impressions without looking at Elen's. Vess would enter the chamber's dimensions and the unresolved outer-wall discrepancy in the works book. Their records would be compared only after each was signed.

Pell still refused to certify the chamber for entry.

---

The trouble with two copies was that everyone wanted the one that would make a good story.

A steward from House Pell asked Ysabet for a likeness to show at a private supper. The supper had no royal authority and would not be held in Edselsto's great hall, but its guests would be delighted to speculate about an ancestral secret.

Ysabet offered him the public notice: *Unregistered upper-gallery recess discovered behind fixed shelving; access restricted pending inspection; incised mark recorded; origin and meaning unknown.*

“That is hardly worth showing,” he said.

“Then you will not need it.”

The steward complained that scholars were being granted a privilege denied to the houses whose money kept the court alive. Ysabet reminded him that Neris had been hired to make a copy, not to flatter a dinner guest, and that the House Quorin surveyor was measuring stone under an existing works commission.

The next petitioner was a man from the Brotherhood of the Living Ink. Brother Odran had heard of the mark and wanted to compare it with the Hall's old collections. He offered to take the impression away and return it after a week.

Neris said she would show him the sealed copy at the Hall under its rules. She would not surrender it into a traveling satchel.

“You distrust the Brotherhood?” Odran asked.

“I distrust roads, rain, unlisted hands and the habit of calling an unsigned copy an original.”

He considered this and asked whether he might at least see the description.

She read it aloud. Odran listened, then described three families of marks he had encountered: a mason's turning sign, a fragment of a household protective formula, and a partial character from a much older hand whose strokes were not all read in the same direction.

“Which is this?” Ysabet asked.

“I should like to know.”

“So should I.”

“Then may I compare the copy with the catalog?”

“Under the Hall's witness, with the uncertain lines marked as uncertain.”

Odran accepted. He had wanted a discovery that would justify a winter of scholarship. He had instead acquired a day's careful work and the possibility of being proved wrong in public. He was enough of a scholar to see that this was not nothing.

In the archive, Ysabet found an older works index with a charcoal sketch of a bent line and two short strokes. It had been filed decades earlier under *lower return, uncertain place*. The sheet bore no named maker, no trustworthy date of inspection, and no account of which wall had supplied the sketch. A later clerk had written *beneath the old buttery?* with a question mark.

Odran leaned over it.

“That resembles—”

“Stop there,” Ysabet said.

“I was going to say the enclosing bend.”

“You may say that. You may not say it came from an original stone below this castle.”

“Someone thought it did.”

“Someone wrote a question mark.”

She made a separate entry for the old sketch. Its uncertain origin was preserved, along with the fact that it had been in the archive before the cupboard was opened. It was not added to the two direct impressions as though it were a third witness. If an accessible lower stone could ever be located and measured safely, the sketch might help identify where to look. Until then it was a lead with a poor pedigree.

Odran copied the catalog reference, not the sketch itself, and asked permission to return with a second reader from a different tradition.

Ysabet said he could ask again when he had a name and a reason.

He went away irritated, but he did not go away empty-handed.

---

Damaris received the costs at the end of the second day.

There was a carpenter's interrupted work, two Doorwardens assigned to a guarded passage, paper and charcoal, a Copyists' Hall fee, Elen Quorin's additional measurements, and the question of whether the old panel should be repaired or replaced. None of these sums was large enough to rescue the kingdom. Together they were large enough to make the roof men complain that a carved wall had stolen their morning.

“The room has already cost more than the next length of gutter,” Tomas said.

“It has not been paid for yet,” Damaris corrected. “That is the trouble with costs that arrive before permission.”

Pell put the wrapped stone chip on the table, still sealed.

“I did not cut this off to create a mystery. It fell when the panel came away.”

“I believe you,” Damaris said. “Belief does not enter it as a free stone.”

Luceran sat with Rhyse, Ysabet and Miren at the smaller table that had replaced the intended hall supper. The king had asked to see the two direct impressions. Ysabet laid them out with the mistaken reverse copy between them, where nobody could mistake their history.

“The same mark,” Luceran said.

“The same visible surface, copied twice,” Neris corrected from the end of the table.

He looked at her, surprised by the interruption.

“Is that not the same thing?”

“Not if the stone was carried here from elsewhere. Not if the carving was cut last year. Not if a reader has supplied the missing line in her mind. Your Majesty has two copies of a mark, not two witnesses to its maker.”

Elen Quorin laid down her corrected plan. The room fitted within the outer wall better than the first survey had allowed. She had also found an old mortar seam beneath the cupboard's bottom rail, but could not date it from sight. Her drawing showed the awkward north corner without making it miraculous.

Luceran had been hoping, without admitting it, that Edselsto might have given him something clear at a time when so many other things required witnesses, money and disagreement.

“Could the house tell us when the room was made?” he asked.

Vess answered before anyone could turn toward the walls.

“Not in a manner I can measure. This passage has no approved listening survey, and we have not established what the house can perceive here. Even an answer would be a claim to test, not a date in my book.”

Rhyse said he would not authorize a new listening branch to settle the question. Miren, seated near the window, said nothing until Luceran looked at her.

“You have enough people who can say no,” she said. “That is what makes their answers worth hearing.”

He let the words remain between them. She had not forgiven him for taking speech from a private room. Nor had the terrible truth of Corven's death become less terrible because he was learning restraint. But he did not order Edselsto to repeat what any scholar had said in an unconnected passage, or to report what it had heard elsewhere.

Damaris closed the account.

“One paid day of copying and the necessary watch are allowed against the works inspection account. The short comparison of the signed measurements tomorrow may use one further half-day, entered against the same account; any wider scholarly catalog work must wait for a new limit. No charge goes against the east-gate net pledged to Jorvan. No hall hospitality money is restored. The roof and receiving-yard drain remain ahead of ornamental work.”

“And the third hearth?” Luceran asked.

“Cold.”

“The guest rooms?”

“Three dry ones. We are not discovering a fourth by calling a recess a chamber.”

Rhyse proposed keeping the gallery open to workers under escort and the small room closed. Pell wanted the old panel braced without covering the carving again. Elen required two external reference points to remain accessible for later measurement. Neris wanted her own name on any public account of the impressions.

Luceran approved those narrow terms. He did not proclaim an ancestral message. He did not grant a monopoly on interpretation to the Brotherhood, House Quorin or the temple. Ysabet would retain the custody record and allow supervised scholarly comparison by named readers; no one could take the originals away.

It was an unsatisfying royal command.

It was also one that could be carried out.

---

The following morning, Elen found Neris in the upper gallery with the two signed sheets laid on separate boards.

They had not seen each other's final drawings since the previous day. Elen brought her measured elevation; Neris brought the charcoal impression and a plain ink outline made without the elevation. Vess stood beside the outer reference stake. Rhyse's Doorwarden watched the passage. Pell remained near enough to stop anyone leaning against the weak jamb.

They compared the position of the enclosing bend first. It lay the same distance above the lower seam in both accounts. They compared the three short strokes. The middle one was shorter than the others. They compared the hooked end. Neris's outline had made it slightly too round; Elen's measured drawing had made it too square. The charcoal impression showed why both errors were tempting.

“Then neither of us has the perfect copy,” Neris said.

“No,” Elen replied. “But a third person can stand where we stood and tell us which of us is wrong.”

They made a little window in a fresh sheet, marked the top and left edges, and drew four measured points around the carving. It was not a new interpretation. It was a way for a future reader to put a different drawing back into the same place on the same wall.

Vess checked the points against his cord. Pell checked the soundness of the surface and refused Elen's request to scrape a flake from the incised line.

“Not until you can tell me what question that injury would answer,” he said.

Elen was annoyed. Then she wrote the refusal into her notes, with his reason. It would prevent a later reader from assuming that the absence of a stone sample meant nobody had thought of taking one.

Neris took the mistaken reverse copy from its sleeve and held it beside the true impressions.

“Do you know what troubles me most?” she asked.

“That someone may read the mark backwards?” Elen said.

“That they will read it forwards with equal confidence.”

They laughed, briefly, in the manner of two people who had spent a great deal of work earning permission not to know.

At the far end of the gallery, the chamberlain was measuring a space for the deferred hangings. He asked whether the new mark would have to be hidden when the cloth finally came.

“Not until the cloth is bought,” Tomas called from the stairs. “And not until the roof is sound.”

The chamberlain rolled his measuring tape and went away. The festival of masks being discussed in the village would require musicians, tailors, watch hours, food and a place for guests to stand. A carving had not provided any of them.

Elen sealed her elevation. Neris sealed her corrected outline. The two independent accounts, the direct impressions, the reversed error, the old uncertain archive sketch and the custody list were all separately named. The opening remained barred to casual entry, and the lower service stair remained barred to everyone.

That afternoon Ysabet filed the comparison under a heading she had chosen with care: *Upper gallery, unregistered recess: observed mark and measured position*.

Not *new royal chamber*. Not *ancestral warning*. Not *proof of a lower original*.

The file contained the hour of discovery, the men who had opened the panel, the two methods of copying, the admitted mistake, the dimensions, the guarded access and the limits of every claim. A future mason could challenge the wall measurements. A future scholar could challenge the interpretation. A future clerk could trace which sheet had traveled where.

Outside the archive, a messenger brought another packet from the village post-house. It was ordinary Kaelmont dispatch business, not an answer to Edda Kelm's still-pending disclosure. Orlen's corrected iron quotation remained unaccepted in Damaris's book. Hesta's third drain block still awaited the proper mason. The north road did not become sound because a court had found an interesting wall.

Canon Merel came to the archive door after the evening watch and asked whether Ysabet had found a name for the mark.

“No.”

“Have you found out whether it is old?”

“Not yet.”

“Then what have you found?”

She showed him the sealed sheets, each with its own date and witness.

“A way for the next person to prove us wrong.”

He considered that longer than he had considered any proposed reading.

In the family nave the first ancestral candle remained black and untouched. Six others burned, and Merel entered their light in his own book. He had no cause to connect them to the upper gallery, and he did not invent one.

High above, the unregistered chamber waited behind its guarded opening. It had given the court neither an answer nor a command. It had given them a place on a plan, a mark on stone, and a record sturdy enough to survive an argument.

For the first time, the argument would have something fixed to return to.
