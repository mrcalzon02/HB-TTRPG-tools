# Season Three, Episode 13 — Festival of Masks

Tessa had sewn three hundred and twelve small brass bells to other people's clothes and was beginning to hate the sound of money.

The bells were not real gold, though half the courtiers who had ordered them would insist upon calling them gilded. They came in a shallow wooden tray from a village metalworker who had made a fair profit from the order and was now refusing to supply another hundred without an advance. Tessa could hardly blame him. Her own apprentice had spent two evenings threading the little loops through dyed cloth, and there was still no coin in the box from which Tessa paid her.

Lady Sera Sorelle stood in the doorway of the workroom with a velvet mask in her hand.

“Can the ears be made longer?”

“They can,” Tessa said. “That will be a different mask.”

“It is for the same face.”

“The face is not what I charge for.”

Sera considered this, then laughed. She had the polished ease of a woman accustomed to being told yes, but she did not appear offended by the answer. She had brought the order for the first masks herself, rather than sending a servant to promise a sum her house had not yet paid.

“Two evenings,” Sera said. “A procession in the village, dancing beneath the old market awning, music from our company. The king need only be seen.”

“The king will need a road he can walk on.”

“We have one.”

“Half of it is being used to bring stone uphill.”

“Then we shall choose our hours.”

“And the bells?”

Sera put down the velvet mask. “My steward has the money for those.”

“Your steward has a promise for those. I have seen it. The metalworker has seen it. Neither of us can buy bread with it.”

Sera's smile diminished, though it did not vanish. She drew a purse from her sleeve and counted the advance herself. Tessa entered the amount beside the order and called her apprentice to witness it. The apprentice, who was called Ilen and was old enough to have a trade of her own one day, took particular care to look at the coins before she signed.

“Do you distrust my house?” Sera asked.

“I trust you to want a beautiful evening,” Tessa replied. “I trust my apprentice to want her wages. Both wishes can be true.”

Outside, a cart rattled over the wet stones toward the receiving yard. Its driver called for a porter who was supposed to be mending the roof. The porter called back that the roof had not been mended because the timber had arrived late. A masked festival would not make the road wider, the roof sounder, or the porter free.

Sera looked through the open door at the traffic.

“Then perhaps,” she said, “we should begin with what we can afford.”

---

Damaris did not bring a festival proposal to the king until she had found out which people were offering to pay for it.

The Sorelle company had played at the reopening supper, when the hall had been made handsome at the expense of winter linen and roof money. That supper had been real, enjoyable, and paid for. The next contemplated supper had been canceled. Its uncommitted allowance was now assigned to sickroom needs, lawful wage arrears, and measured work on the receiving-yard roof and drain.

A festival could not be made free by calling it a procession.

Sera came to the smaller audience room with the master of her players, Tessa's signed estimate, and two letters from houses willing to underwrite an evening's hospitality. Neither letter offered unlimited credit. One promised cloth and lamp oil up to a named quantity; the other promised a portion of the musicians' pay, subject to the company appearing for both performances.

“Those are offers,” Damaris said. “Not wages.”

“I know,” Sera answered. “The company has demanded its first payment before it unpacks.”

“They learned something at our last supper.”

“They have learned it at many suppers.”

Luceran had been reading the proposed invitations. The names were familiar enough to make the list pleasant: families that had stood beside him in hard seasons, families that had not, houses whose men had spoken for him and whose cousins had tried to humiliate him. He wanted to see them all under one roof without a council table between them.

“Why not the great hall?” he asked.

“Because you canceled its supper,” Damaris said. “Because the third hearth is cold, the upper hangings are deferred, and three dry guest chambers do not become thirty when somebody wears a mask. Because the hall watch and washing would be ours, whether Sorelle calls the evening private or public.”

Sera looked at the list again. “The village square, then. We can use the covered market awning and the old cart yard, if the villagers agree. The musicians can stand on the stone steps.”

“The village council will decide whether the square is available,” Damaris said. “The road keeper will decide which hours can be spared from traffic. The watch captain will decide how many living guards are needed. None of those decisions belongs to your invitation writer.”

“I had hoped for a royal invitation.”

“You may have the king's presence,” Luceran said, “if the necessary people consent.”

Sera inclined her head. “That is a smaller honor than I came for.”

“It is an honor I can actually give.”

Rhyse had already examined the proposed route. The lower market road could remain open to carts until dusk, then be held for a measured interval, with a second passage left clear for sickroom errands and ordinary foot traffic. No guard would be withdrawn from the barred lower stair or the watched upper-gallery recess. Extra evening duty would have to be named and paid. Private household challenges would not become gate law because the guests had hidden their faces.

Damaris added a final condition. Before the first lantern was lit, the sponsoring houses must place sufficient coin with the village paymaster to cover the musicians' agreed first and final wages, hired cleaners, the awning's use, lantern tending, and the extra watch hours. The village paymaster would witness payments to village workers; the Crown watch book would record any lawful extra duty separately. The houses could entertain their own guests beyond that at their own risk and cost. The east-gate net pledged to Jorvan would not pay a penny of it.

“And the invitations?” Sera asked.

“Say what is true,” Damaris replied. “Two evenings of music and masks in the village, subject to the weather. No royal banquet. No lodging promised in the castle. No road permission beyond the stated hours.”

Luceran signed permission for his attendance and for Rhyse to arrange the necessary protection once the village council and paymaster had done their parts. He did not sign a new household hospitality allowance.

Sera took the paper with a thoughtful expression. It would make an awkward invitation.

It would also make an evening possible.

---

The village council gave its consent after the baker demanded to know who would wash the square.

She had no objection to dancing. She objected to strangers putting wine cups in her rain barrel and leaving the broken ones where her customers walked at dawn. The council required the sponsors to hire cleaners and to leave the grain path clear until the evening closure began. A carter demanded a posted hour for reopening the road. The village reeve wrote both conditions beneath the council's seal.

Tessa and Ilen worked beneath the awning while the first of the masks arrived from the dye house. The blue ones had dried too close to the fire and curled at the edges. The gold ones looked splendid until rain touched them. Tessa returned the worst of them and charged the replacement work to the patron who had ordered a change after cutting began.

A noblewoman sent back a mask because another guest had chosen the same feathers. Ilen stared at the complaint.

“Do we make her a new one?”

“We can,” Tessa said. “When she pays for a new one.”

“I thought nobles were not meant to look alike.”

“They are not meant to admit it.”

At the castle kitchens, Tavin received an instruction to carry festival cups down to the square. It had no name beneath it and had been placed among the kitchen orders by a visiting steward.

He took it to the cook.

“I have the water for the upper watch,” he said. “And the pots after supper.”

The cook read the paper, crossed out the words *royal kitchen*, and sent it back. The Sorelle household could hire porters and borrow cups by agreement; it could not turn a kitchen boy's assigned hours into a private festival service. Tavin carried the watch water as ordered. The Doorwarden at the upper gallery thanked him without asking him to peer into the recess.

Behind the guarded opening, the incised bent line, three short strokes, and crooked hook remained on the stone. Ysabet's sealed impression stayed in the castle archive. The Copyists' Hall retained its separate impression. The mistaken reversed transfer remained labeled in the works record, and Elen Quorin's measured elevation still allowed another reader to check the mark against four physical points. No festival invitation included a picture of it.

Canon Merel refused a request to hang a copy above the musicians.

“It has not been read,” he said.

“Then let the guests guess,” the petitioner suggested.

“They already do. They do not require the temple's permission.”

Merel kept the nave open under its ordinary watch. He did not close it for the celebration, and he did not offer the dark first candle as a curiosity to be viewed by masked strangers.

The second evening's lanterns were being prepared when Pell Falbrecht came down from the receiving yard with a split glove and an account of two men who had lost a morning to moving festival benches out of a cart's way. The benches had been placed before the road closure began.

“Who ordered the benches?” Tomas asked.

“A Sorelle servant. He thought the road belonged to the festival already.”

“Who ordered our men to move them?”

“I did. The cart needed through.”

“Then enter their time as works labor and send the obstruction claim to the sponsors. Do not stop their wages while the houses argue.”

The men were not paid twice, and the works book did not pretend the interruption had been roof construction. The first length of gutter remained unfinished. Rain would find it whether the festival was beautiful or not.

---

By the first evening the square smelled of wet wool, roasted onions, lamp oil, and the sharp resin the players rubbed on their bows.

The musicians had received their agreed first payment in the presence of the village paymaster. Their leader refused to begin until the second night's balance was secured under the same witness. Sera had to send a runner for one sponsoring house's steward, who had come to admire the lanterns without bringing the promised coin. He returned with the money after being informed that the first dance might be performed without instruments.

The music began a little late. Nobody who was dancing complained.

The masks were splendid at a distance and more revealing close at hand. A man dressed as a stag had the voice of a merchant whose customers knew his laugh. A woman in silver leaves had borrowed shoes too tight to conceal her limp. A knight who wished to appear anonymous wore his own sword belt, with the same repaired buckle that his men had seen for years.

Luceran came in a plain dark mask, accompanied by Rhyse and two living guards who did not pretend to be invisible. The king was recognized almost immediately. He had never learned to hide the small motion of his shoulders when he heard a tune he liked.

He laughed when a child pointed him out.

“I had hoped to be a stranger for an hour.”

“Your Majesty,” the child's mother said, mortified.

“He is right,” Luceran told her. “I should have borrowed a different pair of shoulders.”

The child did not know what to do with this. He offered the king a sugared nut instead. Luceran accepted it, and the mother looked as if she might faint from relief.

For a while the evening succeeded on its own terms. The baker sold more hot bread than she had expected. A carter who had opposed the closure danced with the village reeve after making him promise to reopen the road on time. A young musician, paid for two nights, played a second tune for his sister without charging her. A noblewoman lent a cloak to a laundress whose dress had become soaked through at the hem. The laundress returned it before the owner could discover that a borrowed kindness might become a patronage claim.

Sera watched from the steps with the satisfaction of a person who had persuaded many reluctant people to do something they were now glad to have done.

A cousin of House Pell approached her.

“An excellent royal triumph,” he said.

“An excellent village evening,” Sera corrected.

“Surely the Crown will remember who made it possible.”

“I intend to see that the musicians remember who paid them.”

The cousin smiled. He had heard a promise of future precedence in her words. Sera had meant a receipt.

At the edge of the square, Bessa kept the ordinary gate tally. Jorvan's house-paid observer occupied his authorized place and witnessed the figures that belonged in his book. He did not check masks, control the festival, or collect extra fees for the sponsors. A separate temporary passage tally recorded the people admitted after the cart road closed. Rhyse's guards could use it to account for a missing guest; it was not proof of anyone's identity.

The first evening ended with the road reopened at the posted hour. Two revelers protested. The carter who had danced with the reeve told them to move their feet.

His cart was waiting.

---

On the second evening Alden Pell found himself standing beside a game he might once have invented.

A young cousin had offered a silver coin to anyone who could name the person beneath a white fox mask. The wearer had been dancing with a masked nobleman, and the cousin insisted she must be Lady Miren because he recognized the turn of her head. Several guests agreed. One disagreed, loudly. A servant was sent to ask whether the woman would lift her mask.

Alden remembered the kitchen boy stopped at the service door and the delays caused by private identity questions at the east gate. He had defended such games then as harmless caution. He had since been made to answer one of them himself, before his own cousin's steward, and had disliked the taste of it.

“Leave her be,” he said.

“It is only a wager.”

“It is a wager on a woman who did not stake herself.”

The cousin laughed at his sudden scruples. The fox-masked dancer turned toward them and lifted the mask of her own accord.

It was Tessa, who had been asked by a paying customer to demonstrate whether the new ties would hold through a full dance. The nobleman beside her was the customer's brother. The mask had been made to fit a face that was not Miren's, and Tessa had worn it to find the defect before the customer did.

“I can tell you the name of the maker,” she said. “I cannot tell you why you thought you owned the face.”

A few guests laughed. The cousin reddened.

“Then I have lost a coin.”

“No,” Tessa said. “You have tried to buy an answer I was not selling.”

She turned to her companion. “The left tie is too short. I shall alter it tomorrow.”

Alden was relieved that she had not been frightened. He was also ashamed of being relieved, as though the game would have been acceptable if the woman had endured it quietly. He said nothing more. He would have to decide later whether he meant to change how his cousins behaved, or merely to avoid being seen beside them.

The incident traveled through the square faster than the music. Some called it a clever rebuke. Others called it insolence. By morning two noble customers had withdrawn small orders from Tessa's workroom; another had placed a larger one, impressed that her masks survived dancing. Tessa entered all three changes. She did not count the larger order as payment until the advance arrived.

Rhyse heard the story from a guard who had not witnessed it. He did not make a formal accusation out of gossip. He reminded the temporary watch that a mask was not an identity warrant and that no private wager could authorize a compulsory search or challenge at the royal gate.

The watchman returned to his post. There were still carts to move when the road reopened.

---

Edda Kelm had not intended to dance.

She had come to the village to purchase fresh cord for her measuring case and to ask whether the ordinary Kaelmont dispatch had arrived. The post-house keeper said there was no answer yet to her disclosure. He showed her the outgoing entry and the next scheduled road pouch. She could see that her letter had been sent. She could not make its recipient answer by looking at the book again.

Orlen found her beside the lantern seller, where she was considering whether the cord was worth the asking price.

“I have brought my charge sheets,” he said.

“Are you selling them with the lanterns?”

“I have given the haulers their copies. Hesta's brother has one, and Tomas can inspect the figures. The price is still higher than anyone wishes.”

“Then the figures have probably become more honest.”

He offered his hand. “One dance?”

She looked at the open square, the masked faces, and the Kaelmont colors on her coat.

“You know what people will say.”

“That I have finally learned to ask instead of offering you a quotation.”

“They will say that too.”

He did not insist. Edda watched two couples pass, then set her measuring case with the post-house keeper and took his hand. She had not come to hide, and she would not pretend the dance had been authorized by an office. She danced because she wanted to.

Orlen was better at counting carriage costs than steps. Edda corrected him twice, and the third time she let him make the mistake because she liked the way he laughed when he discovered it.

Hesta Vale saw them from the bread stall. She did not interrupt. She was discussing the cost of carrying her third drain block to the proper mason for inspection, and she would not trade an enforceable first-refusal right for the pleasure of embarrassing a friend. When Orlen came to buy bread afterward, she asked whether he had sent the corrected hauler figures to the other bidders.

“I have.”

“Good. The third block is still mine to present, and still theirs to judge.”

“I know.”

“Then dance as much as you like.”

Edda heard the exchange. She was grateful to Hesta for speaking plainly and troubled by the fact that a dance could make an honest measurement look purchased. The Kaelmont office would answer her recusal request in its own time. Until then she would not assess Orlen's quotation or claim authority over the Crown's yard.

Later, when the music quieted, she and Orlen stood beyond the square where the road turned uphill. They were outside the established listening ways of Edselsto, but neither imagined that the darkness made them invisible to people.

“I do not know whether they will move me,” she said.

“I know.”

“I will not ask you to wait for a promise I cannot make.”

“Then I will not make one for you.”

She touched his wrist. He did not take the gesture for a contract.

They walked back separately enough to avoid blocking the cart road, not separately enough to prevent anyone from noticing.

---

Miren had worn no mask at the first dance. At the second she borrowed one from a friend and left it hanging from her wrist.

Luceran found her beside the stone steps after the musicians changed places. He had spent the evening being congratulated by men who had not paid for it and petitioned by men who had. His cheeks hurt from smiling.

“You have escaped the dancers,” he said.

“I have escaped a man who wished to explain why his household's silver leaves were superior to another household's silver leaves.”

“I have been hearing the same speech in gold.”

She smiled. The old ease between them was still capable of arriving without permission. That made its limits more painful, not less.

“Would you dance?” he asked.

“Not tonight.”

He nodded. He did not ask why, and she did not offer an excuse. They stood together watching a pair of old villagers move through a slow measure with the practiced economy of people who had danced in crowded kitchens.

“I thought this might be good for the court,” Luceran said.

“It is good for some of them.”

“For you?”

“For an hour, yes. I liked hearing the village children sing louder than the players.”

He waited. She looked at him.

“You are going to ask whether an hour is enough.”

“I was.”

“It is enough for an hour.”

He accepted that answer. She had seen him choose not to command Edselsto to listen where he had no lawful reach. She had not forgiven the earlier theft of private speech, the killing of Corven, or the false denial that followed. No music could turn those things into a courtship misunderstanding.

A messenger from the upper gallery approached Rhyse with a routine watch change. The guarded recess was still closed to casual entry. The lower stair was still barred. Luceran saw the captain read the note and return it without summoning the king. For once he was glad to be unnecessary to a proper watch.

Sera came up the steps carrying a folded account.

“The houses have met the musicians' wages,” she said. “The cleaners have their hire. There is a balance for lantern oil and the extra watch, and the paymaster will settle it at dawn.”

“Then you have given us a good evening,” Luceran said.

“I have given the village a paid evening. The court has made what it likes of it.”

She hesitated, then continued. “My house has borne the trouble of arranging this. We have a little practice now. When the next gathering is held, might Sorelle have first claim upon the musicians' stand and the two rooms nearest the hall?”

Miren looked away toward the dancers. She had heard enough petitions to recognize one arriving inside a compliment.

Luceran could have agreed with a smile. Sera had done useful work. He liked her; the evening was beautiful; a future preference sounded smaller than the cost already paid.

“The rooms are not mine to promise without hearing who uses them,” he said. “And the stand belongs to the village.”

Sera's expression remained courteous. “I asked for consideration, Your Majesty.”

“You shall have a hearing. Not a grant.”

“That is less than I hoped.”

“It is what I said.”

She bowed and withdrew. Luceran watched her go with the uneasy sense that he had disappointed a friend who had earned better treatment than most petitioners.

Miren said, “She will remember that you listened.”

“And that I refused.”

“You did not refuse. You deferred an answer until the people who bear its cost can speak. She may prefer to remember a refusal.”

He glanced at her. “You think I was right?”

“I think you asked a better question than you would once have asked.”

It was praise, and it was not forgiveness. He did not try to turn it into either.

The musicians began their last measure. Miren went down to speak with Yara. Luceran remained on the steps until the lanterns made the wet square look almost like a hall of gold.

---

At dawn the village smelled of spilled ale and cold ashes.

The cleaners had worked until the road was fit for carts. They brought the village paymaster a list of broken cups, a damaged awning rope, and the hour at which the last noble household had finally removed its benches. Their wages were paid from the sponsors' deposit; the damaged rope would be charged after the awning owner inspected it. The paymaster would not release the remaining deposit until that account was settled.

The musicians received their final agreed wages under witness. One player had lost a bow hair in the damp and paid for the repair himself. The company had not promised to remain for a third evening, and no amount of praise persuaded them to play without a new bargain.

Tessa paid Ilen the wages due for the bells and masks. She set aside the two canceled orders and began cutting the cloth for the customer whose advance had actually arrived. She had gained a reputation for refusing a foolish wager. She had also lost business. Both were real.

Pell found the roof men waiting beneath a gutter that had dripped through the night. The benches had been removed from the road, but their interruption remained in the works book. Tomas had the gutter measured again before sending the men up. No new stone had been bought, Hesta's third block was still uninspected, and Orlen's iron quotation remained only a quotation. The receiving-yard roof was not finished.

Damaris compared the village paymaster's account with Rhyse's extra watch record and the Crown household book. The sponsors' deposit covered the named festival expenses; no charge had been taken from Jorvan's pledged east-gate net, and no canceled hall supper had been restored by a change of name. The Crown had still borne ordinary security, attention and wear that could not all be turned into a sponsor's invoice. A court had been entertained, and the people who had made it possible had lost time they might have used elsewhere.

“The town is pleased,” the chamberlain said.

“Much of it,” Damaris answered.

“The houses are pleased.”

“Some of them.”

“And the king?”

She closed the book. “Ask him after the next petition.”

In the nave, Canon Merel entered the morning count: the first ancestral candle remained dark and untouched, the other six burning. The festival had not extinguished another, nor had it explained the first. Above the hall, the upper-gallery copies remained under separate seals and the lower stair remained barred. No new reading had come from the music.

A letter arrived from Sera Sorelle requesting a formal audience on future entertainment precedence and use of rooms. Attached were the musicians' witnessed wage receipts and the village council's consent for the two evenings just completed. Sera was not claiming that the festival had purchased the Crown. She was assembling evidence that her house had been useful, at cost, and ought not to be treated as interchangeable with a house that had merely attended.

Luceran read the letter after breakfast. He remembered the dancers, the children, Miren's smile, the road reopening on time, and Sera's face when he declined to promise her rooms.

The court had looked united beneath its masks.

By morning everyone had taken theirs off, and the claims remained.
