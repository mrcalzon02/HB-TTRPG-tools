# Season Three, Episode 11 — A Dangerous Attachment

Orlen Morcant had brought three versions of the same quotation to the baker's back room, and none of them had yet earned him a sale.

The first priced iron brackets at his workshop. The second added a cart to the foot of the mountain. The third included the wet upper turn, a second team, feed, a night's shelter and the time a driver might spend waiting for the Crown yard to open. He had written the last in a smaller hand, as if an expensive truth could be made less offensive by taking up less paper.

The baker looked at his stack of accounts and set down a plate of bread.

“You have paid for the room,” she said. “You have not paid for the ink you are leaving on my table.”

“It is my ink.”

“It is my table.”

He moved the papers onto his lap. She smiled and went back to her oven.

There would be no second great supper at Edselsto. The news had reached the village before the kitchen had finished dividing the stores for the smaller meals that would continue. The baker had expected to supply loaves for another gathering; now she would sell them to the people who actually came through her door. It was less splendid business and more reliable.

Edda Kelm arrived with rain on her shoulders and a Kaelmont road-office packet beneath her coat. She was late because a bridge keeper had asked her to witness a cart that had passed the upper turn with one wheel bound in rope. She had refused to certify it fit for another journey.

“Your figures are waiting,” Orlen said.

“They were waiting yesterday.”

“Yesterday they were wrong.”

“Then they have improved by waiting.”

She took the chair opposite him, but not the packet out of its wrapping. Orlen had learned that the distinction mattered. She could discuss public road measurements with traders. She could not hand a Crown-side supplier a private instruction from the office that paid her.

They had met often enough now that the baker knew how Edda took her bread, and Orlen knew she would inspect a sum before she accepted a compliment. They had met at the paid price meeting, again when the haulers brought axle measurements, and again when the road keeper returned the first note with a question about the washed culvert. None of those meetings had been an excuse. That was part of what made Orlen look forward to them.

He showed her the third quotation.

“The shelter is mine to arrange,” he said. “The cartage is priced by the haulers. The Crown will see the parts instead of one handsome total.”

“Will you let it see the part you hope to earn?”

He turned the page over. His margin was there.

Edda nodded. “Then I can compare it with the road note. I still cannot recommend your price to my office.”

“Why would Kaelmont care what I charge Edselsto?”

“Because you have asked whether its road can bear your iron. The road belongs to people who will be asked to repair it when your cart breaks a rut.”

“An answer to a road question, then. Not a favor to a merchant.”

“That would be a good beginning.”

Orlen watched her untie the string around a ruler. Her hands were red from the weather, and she had a small cut across one knuckle. He had noticed it on her previous visit and had decided not to ask about it. Today he asked.

“The culvert?”

“The clasp on my case.”

“An unromantic enemy.”

“I have known worse.”

She looked up. He had said nothing that required her to smile, but she did.

---

At the receiving yard, the new roof was still a framework with too much sky showing through it.

Tomas had men measuring the gutter line. The household order had released money for the next measured roof-and-drain work, not for a finished building to appear by command. Some of the workers stopped from descending the lower stair had received their waiting wages; others were still on the works account. The injured man's return to suitable labor remained the physician's decision.

Edda stood beneath the incomplete eaves with Orlen, Hesta Vale's brother, and two haulers. She asked them to walk the route a loaded cart would take, from the eastern approach to the unloading stones. The first hauler went ahead with a cord. The second stayed beside the wheel of an empty demonstration cart.

Hesta's brother carried the dimensions of the third drain block. It had not yet been inspected, and he corrected anyone who called it the third accepted block. One had passed; one had failed. His sister's right to the first offer on twelve accepted Crown-side loads within thirty days was not a promise to purchase stone before inspection.

“The cart turns here,” he said, marking the packed earth with his heel.

“Not if another stands at the bay,” Edda answered.

“Then the other waits.”

“Where?”

He looked toward the road. The road was too narrow for two carts to stand without one wheel taking the wet verge.

Orlen made a note. “A waiting place. More ground. More stone.”

“More money,” said the first hauler.

“Someone's money,” Edda corrected.

They put the empty cart through the turn twice. On the second pass, a wheel slipped toward the edge and the driver brought the horse to a stop before the axle struck the stacked timber. Nobody was hurt. Nobody pretended the stop had proved the turn safe for a loaded cart.

Orlen went to the horse's head and spoke to it while the driver checked the harness. Edda crouched beside the wheel and measured the distance between the rut and the nearest support post.

“You saw it before I did,” she said when Orlen came back.

“I heard the driver curse.”

“That is not the same thing.”

“It was early enough.”

“It was early enough today.”

He did not try to improve the claim. He held the cord while she measured.

The yard keeper asked whether the test was Crown work. Tomas said no: it was a merchant's proposed route demonstration, conducted with the keeper's permission. The Crown would not pay for the cart, the horse or the hour merely because the route might later be useful. Nor could a Kaelmont clerk authorize alterations to a Crown yard.

Orlen paid the haulers their agreed trial hire from his own purse. He also paid the keeper for the covered place in which they could dry the harness before the return journey. The first hauler counted the coins and demanded a receipt.

“Do you think I will deny paying you?” Orlen asked.

“I think you will remember paying me. I would like something that proves I remember receiving it.”

Edda borrowed the keeper's board and wrote the amount, the purpose, the hour and the names of the witnesses. She left a space for Orlen to sign.

“You have made an expense of my honesty,” he told her.

“Your honesty was already expensive. Now it has a date.”

As they left the yard, Orlen offered her his dry cloak. She looked at the wet shoulders of her own.

“If I wear it, you will freeze.”

“I have a workshop.”

“You have a walk to it.”

“Then we will both be wet.”

She accepted the cloak for the distance to the village, on the understanding that he would take it back before she reached the post-house. He found the condition unreasonable and obeyed it.

---

Hesta Vale was waiting at the baker's when they returned.

She had brought a small piece of the failed drain stone, not to sell it but to show the flaw that had opened under the mason's tool. She put it on the table between Orlen's quotation and Edda's ruler.

“My brother says the yard trial was your doing.”

“It was,” Orlen said.

“And you paid for it.”

“I did.”

“Then why does the rumor say Kaelmont is going to choose the Crown's iron?”

Edda took off the borrowed cloak and placed it over the chair.

“Because someone has confused a measured road with a purchased bracket.”

“Can you promise that no one in your office will recommend him?”

“No. I can promise that I have not done so, and that I will not assess a quotation in which I have a private interest.”

Hesta looked from one to the other. She was not a fool. She had seen Orlen bring the same road clerk into every discussion that required careful figures, and she had seen how he listened when Edda contradicted him.

“What private interest?” she asked.

Orlen opened his mouth. Edda answered first.

“I find him worth speaking to when there is no account between us.”

For a moment the baker's stove was the loudest thing in the room.

Hesta examined the broken stone again.

“Then I hope he is worth the trouble. He is a poor choice if you need a man who always agrees.”

“I have discovered that,” Edda said.

“And my first refusal?”

“Remains your first refusal,” Orlen answered. “I have not bought it from you, and I have not asked her to cancel it.”

“Nor could I,” Edda said. “Your Crown-side stone agreement is not Kaelmont's to revise.”

Hesta pressed the two broken faces together. They did not meet cleanly.

“I would have you both remember that when the next block is offered. My family can afford a fair rejection. It cannot afford a fair rejection invented after someone else has been promised the order.”

“Then the inspection should be witnessed,” Orlen said.

“It already must be.”

“By the proper mason,” Edda added. “Not by a woman who happens to like the supplier.”

Hesta let the silence sit long enough to make both of them uncomfortable. Then she laughed.

“I was going to say something kinder. I have changed my mind.”

She gathered the stone and her brother's measurements. Before she left, she told Orlen that she still intended to compare his delivered price with the quarry's actual cost. She told Edda that Kaelmont's road would remain Kaelmont's problem until its own office said otherwise. Then she paid the baker for the bread she had eaten.

Orlen waited until the door shut.

“You did not have to say that.”

“Which part?”

“That you liked speaking to me.”

“I could have lied by omission,” Edda said. “It would have made the next meeting more difficult.”

He looked at the door, where the rain was beginning to run down the threshold.

“The next meeting,” he repeated.

“I have not canceled it.”

---

At the village post-house, Yara Fen was separating a League packet from two ordinary merchants' letters. The packet bore a Dols seal. She entered its arrival in her own book before asking whether the road had flooded again.

Edda came to borrow a clean sheet and a quiet corner. She needed to write to her superior at Kaelmont.

“An official report?” Yara asked.

“An official disclosure.”

“Then do not let me read it.”

“I was going to ask how you handle a matter when you know the person it concerns.”

“Usually badly at first.”

Edda gave her a look.

“Then with a second book,” Yara said. “One for what I am charged to transmit. Another for what I am not entitled to tell the first book.”

She laid the sealed Dols packet beside the receipt register. Her duties to the Three Estuaries League had not vanished because the Crown needed grain or because she had come to care about people in Edselsto. Jalen's restricted medical observations remained his to authorize. His personal letter to Leora had no acknowledged receipt; Yara would not turn either into a public proof merely to ease the diplomats' work.

“A private affection does not make a sealed packet public,” she said. “Nor does an official packet make a private affection a state secret. You must decide which of your acts the office needs to know.”

Edda unfolded the Kaelmont paper.

“I have helped measure a route used in a Crown supplier's proposed price. I have not approved the road or recommended the supplier. I have begun seeing him outside the work.”

“Seeing?”

“Talking. Walking. Finding reasons to remain when the figures are finished.”

Yara's expression softened, but she did not ask for a name.

“Then your office may need a different clerk to review his price. It need not own the conversation you had after the meeting.”

“If I tell them enough, they may take the road work from me.”

“Perhaps.”

“If I tell them too little, they may be right to take it.”

“Also perhaps.”

Edda disliked advice that refused to become an answer. She liked Yara better for giving it.

She wrote that she had a developing personal attachment to a supplier whose quoted carriage costs she had measured; that she had issued no authorization and had not handled a Crown award; and that she requested another Kaelmont officer review any formal submission touching his price. She offered to preserve the public measurements and answer factual questions under her name. She did not write what Orlen had said about her hands, or that she had worn his cloak.

Yara witnessed the sealing, not the contents. The post-house keeper took the packet under an ordinary Kaelmont dispatch entry. It would travel on Kaelmont's route, not under a League diplomatic seal or a Crown command.

Edda stayed long enough to help Yara count the outgoing bags.

“Do you think this is a foolish beginning?” she asked.

“I think it is a beginning that can cost you something.”

“That was not the question.”

“It is the answer I can prove.”

---

Damaris did not receive Orlen in the great hall. The canceled supper had left the chamberlain with unused place cards and several irritated noble houses; it had not made the Crown's accounts less crowded.

She received him in the small office beside the works books, with Tomas present and the windows open to let the damp out of the paper.

Orlen placed the revised quotation and the yard trial receipt on the desk.

“The price is higher,” Damaris said.

“The earlier price did not deliver what its heading promised.”

“And the trial?”

“Paid by me. I do not ask reimbursement.”

“Good. The Crown did not commission it.”

Tomas checked the route measurements. The receiving roof remained incomplete, the gutter unlaid and the lower drain unfinished. An unaccepted iron quotation could not become an order by being more honest than the previous one.

Orlen explained that Edda Kelm had measured public road dimensions but would not review any price recommendation touching his own quotation. He had not seen her letter to Kaelmont. He asked that the Crown obtain an independent account of the wet turn if it wished to compare bids.

Damaris looked at him over the paper.

“Are you asking me to pay for another survey?”

“No. I am asking you not to use a private conversation as though it were one.”

“And what will you do?”

“Pay for the haulers' written charges to be available to any bidder who wants to inspect them. If a separate survey is ordered by the proper road authority, I will submit my figures to it like everyone else.”

“You may lose the order.”

“I may have lost it already.”

“You may lose it because the better figures show someone else's delivery is cheaper.”

“That would be a legitimate loss.”

Damaris took a moment before she answered. She had heard merchants say similar things when they expected to win anyway. Orlen looked as if he understood that a competitor might actually win.

“I will retain this as a quotation,” she said. “Not a contract. Not a preference. Not a Crown undertaking to pay for your trial. Tomas will compare it with the actual costs once the authorized measurements are complete.”

“And Hesta?”

“Her first refusal stands. One block accepted, one failed, the third awaiting inspection. Neither your price nor your affection changes a signed right.”

He had expected the word to sting more than it did.

At the door, Orlen passed a servant carrying the old supper place cards toward the storeroom. A guest had written a complaint that the canceled dinner denied him an audience with a Kaelmont representative. Damaris had directed him to the public petition hours instead. There were still two usable hearths and three dry guest rooms; the third hearth remained cold, and the upper-gallery hangings would wait.

The Crown had not become rich by refusing a feast. Orlen had not become a favored supplier by falling in love with a road clerk.

Outside, the first hauler asked whether the revised price meant work.

“Not yet,” Orlen said.

“Then what did we measure for?”

“So the man who does hire you knows what he is asking.”

“That man had better pay.”

“I agree.”

---

Edda found Orlen at the village well after the afternoon rain stopped.

He had come to return the ruler she had left at the baker's. She had come because she had guessed he would do that rather than send it with a servant. They had been careful about official boundaries all day. Neither was innocent of arranging an ordinary encounter.

“Kaelmont has my letter,” she said.

“Already?”

“The post-house has it. Kaelmont will have it when its rider arrives.”

“What did you tell them?”

“That I should not be the officer deciding whether your price is fair.”

“It may cost you the road survey.”

“Yes.”

“I could withdraw the quotation.”

“You could. You would still be the man I have been meeting, and I would still have made the measurements. Withdrawal would not undo the reason for disclosure.”

He held out the ruler. She took it but did not put it away.

“I have kept your cloak dry,” she said.

“I was hoping you would keep it.”

“I would rather return it.”

“That is disappointing.”

“Because you wanted me to have it?”

“Because I wanted an excuse to see you again.”

“You have the ruler.”

“You are holding it.”

“I am.”

The well stood beyond the castle's walls. The baker's door was visible from it; so was the public road. There was no marked listening silver, no court officer demanding their private words, and no reason to ask Edselsto what either of them meant. They had to ask one another.

Orlen leaned against the low wall.

“I like you,” he said. “Not because you can make Kaelmont open a road. You have spent three meetings telling me you cannot. I like the way you tell a man where his numbers end. I like that you remember the horse before the price. I find myself saving the best part of the day to tell you the worst part of mine.”

Edda looked down at the ruler. A bead of water had collected in its carved mark.

“I have been in road offices long enough to know that being useful can be mistaken for being loved,” she said. “A clerk makes a difficult journey possible and someone calls her indispensable. Then the journey ends and she is a woman who has not been asked whether she wants supper.”

“Do you want supper?”

“Not a court supper. I hear there isn't one.”

“I could buy bread.”

“You could. The baker would charge you for the room again.”

“I would pay.”

“I know you would.”

She set the ruler on the well wall. He reached for her hand, slowly enough that she could refuse it. She did not. His fingers were cold from the wet walk. Hers were rough where the case clasp had cut them.

“Kaelmont may tell me to leave this road work,” she said.

“Then I will not ask you to stay on it for me.”

“I may have to leave the valley.”

“Then I will write, if you want letters.”

“Private letters. Not through your Crown office.”

“I have no Crown office.”

“Good. Keep it that way.”

He laughed. She kissed him once, deliberately, and stepped back before he could mistake it for a promise that all the harder things had been settled.

“I will write when I know where I am posted,” she said. “If the office reassigns me, you will hear it from me, not from a rumor at the gate.”

“And I will send the corrected charges to the other bidders as I said I would.”

“Even if one of them underprices you?”

“Especially then. Otherwise you will have paid for my honesty with your position, and I will have bought nothing but a better-looking lie.”

She picked up the ruler. They stood together until the light began to fail, speaking of smaller things: a baker's impossible table, the horse that had disliked the empty cart, a bridge keeper who believed rope could repair anything. When they parted, neither asked the other to make a grand declaration. They had made two smaller promises that could be broken and would matter if they were.

---

The next morning, the Kaelmont dispatch had gone. Orlen's revised quotation remained in Damaris's file, unaccepted. The haulers had their trial hire and a witnessed receipt. Hesta's brother brought the third block for the proper inspection; nobody called it accepted before the mason tested it. The Crown's roof men returned to the unfinished gutter.

At the east gate, Bessa opened the original toll book for the Jorvan observer. He witnessed the net without collecting it. The twenty-four-mark advance and twenty-seven-mark repayment cap remained governed by twelve market days of east-gate net surplus after ordinary expenses, with at most four further market days at half-net. No private road meeting, no household-watch payment and no merchant's affection had altered that pledge.

Yara entered the arrival of another foreign packet without opening Jalen's medical record or inventing an acknowledgment of his letter. Dols had still not accepted the wall or granted a military countersign. Merehaven's second advance remained withheld; Teth's ferry was not the Crown's to seize. Six sacks of grain remained north and six at Edselsto. Those facts were not made easier by two people finding each other pleasant.

At the barred lower stair, Vess and the independent mason still owed a dry-night reading before anyone could certify a descent. The first ancestral candle remained dark. Canon Merel watched the other six burn and entered the hour without explaining why the seventh had failed.

Luceran heard that a Kaelmont clerk had declared an interest in a Crown supplier. He asked Damaris whether the declaration threatened the works.

“It may delay a price comparison,” she said. “It may also prevent a bad one.”

“Can we afford the delay?”

“We cannot afford to pretend the road is sound when it has not been measured.”

He looked toward the hall where the supper would not take place. He had wanted a court whose friendships made difficult things possible. He was beginning to see that a friendship could also make a simple thing expensive.

Miren passed the office door on her way to the south gallery. She heard only the end of their exchange, and Luceran did not ask Edselsto to supply the rest. The physically disconnected withdrawing room remained private. Their own affection survived without renewed intimacy, forgiveness, political trust or an answer about marriage.

At the baker's, a folded note waited for Orlen. It had come by an ordinary messenger from Edda before her departure for the Kaelmont office. It contained no road dimensions, no private dispatch, no promise of a favorable report.

She had written: *I have kept my word to the office. Keep yours to the haulers. I should like to see you when there is nothing left to measure.*

Orlen read it twice. He paid the messenger, put the note away from his business accounts and went to deliver the promised price sheets.

He had no order for iron. She had no certainty of her post. They had given each other no jurisdiction, no purse and no right to command the other's life.

They had, however, made themselves answerable to one another.

That was the dangerous part.
