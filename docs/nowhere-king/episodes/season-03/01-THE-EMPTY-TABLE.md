# Season Three, Episode 01 — The Empty Table

The first morning after the Dols witnesses departed, the great table was laid for twelve and occupied by five. The kitchen had prepared the usual bread, porridge and small beer before the chamberlain sent word that the audience would be private. By the time the servants had carried away the unused places, the porridge in Luceran's bowl had formed a skin.

He knew every absence. Senna and Harven were on the road with records of his admission. Miren had declined the morning table. Yara was attending to her separate foreign dispatches. Jalen ate where a physician could reach him. Two household officers had gone to inspect the receiving-yard roof. The empty chairs were not a single conspiracy, and that made them harder to resent.

Damaris spread a sheet beside his plate. She had ruled it into three narrow columns: grain actually delivered, grain actually held, and grain that someone had promised to move. The third column was the longest.

"Six sacks at the northern posts," she said. "Six here. No second advance from Merehaven. No second team waiting to be hired. Dols has not accepted its wall. Eighteen patrol mounts remain withdrawn. Teth's ferry belongs to its owners."

"I know."

"Then I shall not read the figures twice."

Rhyse, who had come in wet from the gate, asked whether the king meant to recall the Dols escort before the witnesses reached home. Luceran looked up sharply.

"No."

"Good. I have given the captain the ordinary road allowance and no authority to inspect the packets. If a man returns claiming he was told otherwise, I will ask him who told him and when."

Ysabet set down the royal copy of the non-retaliation order. Her original lethal instruction remained locked under her own seals, available for witnessed comparison. She would not send it away to improve the appearance of cooperation.

Luceran had once imagined that a confession might at least simplify the next day. Instead it had created more separate things that must be done correctly. He asked what the northern posts could eat if the next six sacks did not move.

"Less," Damaris answered. "Or grain bought by another road, at a price that will make you angry."

The king could not order Dols to accept its wall, Merehaven to lend, Teth to surrender a boat or a hired carter to wait without pay. He could order his own people to find the price of those refusals. He told Damaris to do so, and for the first time that morning she looked relieved rather than grateful.

---

Hesta Vale had come to the receiving yard to collect a pair of iron shoes for her quarry mule. The smith had finished only one. His apprentice held up the other, still black from the forge, and explained that the Crown's gutter brackets had taken the morning's first heat.

"My mule cannot walk on a royal gutter," Hesta said.

The apprentice, who had been awake since before dawn, laughed before he could stop himself. Hesta paid for the finished shoe and told him she would return after the noon firing. She did not ask the smith to abandon the brackets. A roof that stopped dripping on grain would be worth something to everyone who hauled through the yard.

Her brother had brought three smaller quarry blocks on a measuring sled. The original stone for the Dols wall had been too heavy for the softened approach; no one was eager to repeat the cart accident. These blocks could be moved by ordinary hands and weighed before loading. But a measured stone was not yet a permitted crossing, and a load traveling downhill did not magically pay for a grain wagon traveling up.

Tomas met her with two offers from carters. One team could be had after three days if its owner finished a mill delivery. Another wanted a payment for each idle night whether or not the bridge opened. Neither offered the old waiting rate. The released carter had already taken his horses home.

Hesta pointed to the road account. "Your figures forget my passing place."

"The Crown built the passing place."

"The Crown promised my family four seasons without the Crown-track toll. Your grain teams still use the width that my men keep clear. If you put two loaded wagons there at once, one will wait in the ditch."

She wanted a paid porter at the narrow turn, the damage terms written before the first quarry block was lifted, and her toll privilege left untouched. Her brother wanted his stones accepted by a mason before any claim of carriage was entered. These were not insults to the king. They were the difference between a road on paper and a road under a wheel.

Tomas offered to bring the measured terms to Damaris, not to sign for her. Hesta accepted that much. She asked the apprentice to put the second mule shoe aside when it was done; she had another customer at the quarry and could not spend her day standing in a royal yard.

When she left, Tomas altered his price sheet. The Crown's cheapest carriage was no longer the cheapest once the porter, damaged axle risk and the lost day's work were included.

---

Jalen's new chair had one side lower than the other. The carpenter had taken his measurements twice and still looked frightened of presenting the work. Jalen reached from the seat to the writing board, lifted a sheet of paper and set it down again.

"That," he said, "is what I asked for."

The carpenter smiled, then stopped smiling when he noticed Isera watching the strain in Jalen's shoulders. She explained that the chair might need another brace after a few days. It was a practical adjustment, not a cure. Jalen asked her to have the brace fitted before the carpenter was paid his final portion. He had learned to distinguish a kind promise from a completed piece of work.

Yara brought the letter he had dictated the day before. Its outside named a possible address for his sister and asked for his old service papers. The lines he had marked private remained folded within, unread by the Crown clerk. Rhyse had provided a household courier without demanding that Jalen undertake any further service.

"Will they read it at the road house?" Jalen asked.

"They will read the direction," Yara said. "The fold is yours."

She could not promise the address was still good. He had been dead to ordinary accounts for too long. The casualty roll named Orris, but the original discharge and signed payment receipt had not appeared. He asked that the search continue whether or not his sister answered.

Miren arrived with a length of plain cloth to cover the chair's unfinished back. She had asked the carpenter what size was needed and cut it herself. Jalen thanked her. Neither made a ceremony of his independence. He wanted to write, eat, sleep and be spoken to as himself; the chair was a means to those ordinary things.

Yara watched him fold the private sheet with a care that had nothing to do with foreign warrants. She had spent weeks preventing a dead envoy's authority from being carried by the wrong person. Now she was helping a different man reach his family. She would not confuse the two services again.

The courier left with a dated entry in the ordinary household dispatch book. No reply had arrived from the sister, from Leora, or from any river bench. Jalen knew that sending a letter was not the same as being heard. It was nevertheless something he had chosen to do.

---

Ysabet found Damaris in the little chamber where the old Jorvan offer had been kept. The seal was unbroken on the folded counteroffer, but the writing had been copied for examination. House Jorvan had once offered money against broad customs; after objection it had named two market-gate tolls instead. Narrower security did not mean harmless security. Those tolls paid for gate repairs, night watches and the clerks who counted the traffic.

"If we pledge both," Damaris said, "the money can move grain. If the tolls fail, the repairs wait. If we promise them twice, someone will call it fraud, and rightly."

Ysabet asked whether the offer had expired. No term on the surviving paper said so. That did not mean the lender was still willing. The king's admission had changed what a prudent creditor might demand before handing over coin.

Damaris proposed a hearing, not an acceptance. A Jorvan factor could inspect the last three toll rolls under named Crown witnesses, compare the sums with the gate keepers and state what security the house would actually require. The Crown would not grant possession of the books or a lien by allowing inspection. If the factor wanted a pledge, he would have to name its term, its priority over existing expenses and what happened upon default.

"And who pays for this inspection?" Ysabet asked.

"The Crown, if it invites him. A clerk's time and a horse's feed are not free because the answer may be no."

Ysabet saw why the king would dislike the arrangement. A living creditor could study his weakness, refuse him and tell other lenders what he had learned. Edselsto, by contrast, could remember a silver chest and offer it without asking what the king had done to deserve the need.

She agreed to draft a limited invitation with Damaris, preserving the Crown's right to refuse final terms and the house's right to refuse credit. They left a space for the messenger's name and departure hour. Nothing was yet borrowed. No market toll had changed hands.

---

Leora's niece had decided the ribbon horse was too proud to eat oats from a plain wooden dish. She demanded a silver one. Leora offered a polished spoon instead, and the child declared it a feast.

Her mother laughed from the settle, then coughed hard enough to make the girl look away. Leora held the bowl while the older woman caught her breath. There were mornings when the household's ordinary work was the only thing that prevented Leora from living entirely inside the unanswered letters.

The representative she had sent to the village watch was returning with a dated inventory of original records, but no one at her house knew how far he had traveled. She did not know of Luceran's public admission. She knew of the later accident, of a man calling himself Jalen who had returned to Edselsto, and of her own private question that the apparent Corven had refused to answer.

Her steward brought paper. She wrote to Jalen by that name, not to her husband. She asked whether he would accept a letter from her and whether he could tell her, without anyone prompting him, what he himself remembered of his own life. She did not enclose the private question she had once sent to the man claiming to be Corven. It belonged to another uncertainty.

The steward asked if the household seal should be used to make the road officers hurry.

"No. Put my name on it. Ask for a dated receipt."

He looked doubtful. The family seal had opened doors before. But she did not want a clerk frightened into claiming delivery, or a guard inventing an answer to please a grieving wife. She paid the messenger's fee from her own household account and watched him leave with a horse that had been fed for the road.

Her mother asked whether she expected Corven home.

"I expect a letter to travel," Leora said. "After that, I don't know."

The child had finished feeding the ribbon horse. Leora helped her tie the harness again. She had learned not to make a certainty out of love merely because certainty would have been a kindness.

---

Above the eastern cistern, the mason had propped the first stones of the blocked outlet and found the old mark exactly where Edselsto remembered it. Beyond that mark the passage was packed with soil. The timber he had requested had arrived damp and would need drying before he trusted it beneath the weight.

Luceran stood with him in the chill while the man pressed a thumb into the wet grain of the wood. The king wanted to ask why no one had fetched dry timber. He could see the yard from the window: men were already moving boards to save grain from rain. There were only so many carts, shoulders and hours.

"Can you tell me where it comes out?" Luceran asked.

"Not until we clear it or measure from the other side. The mark tells us who dressed this stone. It doesn't tell us what the hillside has done since."

When the mason left, Edselsto spoke of an older gutter running eastward above the foundation. It could give the old turn and the name of the wall where a junction had once stood. Luceran thanked it. He did not ask it whether Jorvan would lend or Miren would return. It had no grounds to know either answer.

Miren found him there after visiting Jalen. She saw the timber and the chalk, and understood that the work would proceed slowly.

"Damaris means to bring a creditor here," Luceran said.

"Then I hope he knows how to count."

"He will ask to see our weakness."

"He will already know some of it. Better that he see what we can actually pay."

Luceran looked at her hands, still roughened by cutting the cloth for Jalen's chair. He wanted to ask her to dine with him. He knew the answer was hers, not the castle's and not his reward for confessing.

"Would you sit at the small table tonight?" he asked.

"I have promised Jalen I will return to see whether the cloth rubs his shoulder."

"Afterward?"

"Afterward I will go to my room."

He nodded. She stayed a moment longer to ask whether the mason's wages had been entered, then left him to his inspection. He felt the familiar ache of her departure, sharpened by the knowledge that she was not abandoning him. She was carrying out obligations that mattered without his presence.

Edselsto answered another question about the old eastern stone promptly and correctly within the limits of its memory. It did not have to choose between helping Jalen and sharing Luceran's supper. He found that simplicity comforting, and disliked himself a little for the comfort.

---

At dusk, Damaris returned to the small table with two sheets. The first was Tomas's corrected carriage account, now including Hesta's porter, the passing place and a measured return load that no bridge authority had yet accepted. The second was Ysabet's draft invitation to Jorvan.

Luceran read the second twice. It offered a witnessed inspection of two named market-gate toll rolls and an audience to discuss limited credit for grain carriage and road work. It expressly withheld any pledge, assignment or priority until fresh terms were written and accepted. The inspection would cost the Crown wages, lodging and a clerk's time even if Jorvan declined.

"Why must we show him the tolls before he tells us what he wants?" Luceran asked.

"Because he has already told us what he might take," Damaris said. "He wants to know whether it exists. After Dols, no one sensible will lend against your word alone."

He thought of the silver chests Edselsto had remembered. He had taken them for a gift until Damaris had shown him how many belonged to other people. The castle had been right about the room and silent about the owners. Now the same distinction stood between a royal invitation and a creditor's consent.

Rhyse asked for a separate guard on the visiting factor's papers and Ysabet insisted that the original toll rolls remain with their keepers. Etta entered the two named gate officers whose accounts could be compared. No one proposed to make the Jorvan clerk a prisoner or to let him walk away with the books. They could invite a difficult person without giving him everything.

Luceran signed the invitation. He also signed a limited order paying Hesta's proposed porter for a single measured trial on the Crown side of the road, conditional on the mason's acceptance of the quarry blocks and without altering her four-season toll right. No stone was thereby permitted over Dols's unaccepted wall. Tomas would take the road terms to Hesta in the morning; she could refuse them.

A named courier took the Jorvan letter after Etta recorded the hour, seal and destination. The lender had not yet received it. The king had committed himself to a meeting he could not command to end in agreement.

When the last of the council departed, Luceran remained at the table. It was still mostly empty. The signatures had not filled the chairs, but they had given absent people a lawful reason to come and demand something of him.

Beyond the door, the castle was ready to answer. In the family nave all seven candles continued to burn.
