# Season Three, Episode 06 — Doors With Opinions

The boy who had been asked to make a service door admire his breeding was called Tavin. On the morning after the reopening, he discovered that everyone in the kitchen knew about the silver penny except the cook, who knew only that the water had arrived late.

He had carried six jugs to the hall, fetched two more when the musicians began playing, and returned with the last pair while a nobleman explained to his friends that the castle preferred a gentleman's voice. The nobleman had gone home. Tavin was still scrubbing the bottoms of kettles.

"Did you take the penny?" the cook asked.

"No."

"Good. It wasn't his to pay you for my work."

She turned the borrowed kettle over. Its handle had split where the rivet passed through the iron. Someone would have to walk it to the smith, and the washing hands hired for the supper were gone. The kitchen had enjoyed the music through the open hatch, but the pleasure had left pans, grease and a cracked kettle behind.

Tavin asked whether Rhyse's intervention meant he would no longer be sent through that passage.

"It means you go through it with water," she said. "Not with an audience."

A porter came down the stair to say that three young guests were examining the marks along the south gallery. They had found the silver line in the plaster and were betting which door would answer if spoken to politely. The cook looked at the waiting bread, the kettle and the two boys still carrying ashes.

"If they need entertainment," she said, "give them a bucket."

Tavin laughed because he thought she was joking. She was not.

---

The young Pell cousin had a name, Alden, and enough relatives at court that he was accustomed to being remembered even when nobody could remember what he had done. At the reopening he had made the suggestion about the silver-marked passages. By breakfast he had improved it into a pastime.

He led three acquaintances down the south gallery and showed them a line of silver set into the old stone. It was an actual restored listening way, marked after the works so that household officers could tell connected spaces from those deliberately isolated. Alden had been told as much by a clerk. He preferred the explanation that the castle chose its favorites.

"The west passage has answered the king," he said. "This one may answer someone worth listening to."

His companions tried titles, old family names and a line from a drinking song. Nothing replied. A passing Doorwarden told them that no one had been assigned to take petitions from a wall.

"Then why mark it?" Alden asked.

"So people know where not to hold private conversations," the guard answered.

That made the game more interesting. Alden announced that whoever could make a marked passage disclose a secret would win a pair of gloves. One of the others objected that the house had never promised to answer them. Alden said that made it a proper wager.

Alden was not seeking state secrets. He wanted a story to carry into the next supper: that he, who owned no great estate and held no office, had coaxed an ancestral palace into noticing him. He had watched Luceran receive useful answers from old stone and mistaken access for personal favor.

They moved to a service turn where a door had once refused to yield until a living keeper brought its latch into line. One companion pulled the handle. It did not move. Alden bowed extravagantly.

"Your most noble threshold, will you admit us?"

The Doorwarden on the other side said, "Not while you are leaning on the bolt."

Alden's friends laughed. He smiled as though the reply had come from the stone itself.

The guard opened the door and showed them the crooked iron catch. Its lower pin was loose. The mason had been told. Until it was repaired, the keeper had to lift the bolt with both hands.

"Does that count as an answer?" one guest asked.

"It counts as a repair," the guard said.

Alden decided that he had nearly won.

---

At the prepared eastern threshold, Iven Marrick was keeping the duty he had chosen. The two living guards had changed at dawn. Pel Ordan checked the written watch while the other man inspected the latch and the approach. Iven could recognize some voices in the place to which his service was bound. He could not see a face beyond the turn or tell whether a stranger wore a crown.

Sergeant Dain brought Rhyse the account of the gallery wager. Rhyse listened without surprise. After the ball, a castle that had offered a little pleasure was suddenly expected to provide a great deal more.

"Is anyone asking Marrick to play?" Rhyse said.

"Not yet. One of them asked Pel whether the dead keeper could name a woman from the sound of her step."

"What did Pel say?"

"That he could not, and that the question was none of his duty."

Rhyse went to the threshold himself. Iven's voice came from its appointed place, quiet enough that the men in the outer passage could not make a spectacle of it.

"They are asking whether I know things I do not know," Iven said.

"Do you want them turned away?"

"I want the watch kept. I chose to keep a door. I did not choose to judge who loves whom."

Rhyse let the distinction stand. Iven was neither an oracle nor a prisoner hired out for amusement. He remained a finite keeper under living authority, with the same incomplete memories and limitations he had acknowledged when he volunteered. Pel still had to check passes. Dain still had to supply living guards. If Iven warned that someone approached, it did not replace a man looking to see who had come.

Rhyse ordered Dain to make a plain notice for the marked approaches: household traffic, guard work and authorized inquiry only. The notice would be read aloud to guests as well as servants. It would not claim the castle was unable to hear elsewhere; the limits of its hearing had never been proved merely by a mark on plaster.

"Shall I put a guard on every line?" Dain asked.

"With whose wages? Put the guard where a door must be kept. Make the rule plain where it can be read."

They had enough trouble paying the watches already. The king's guests would have to learn that a rule did not become optional because its keeper was busy.

---

Miren took Yara into the withdrawing room off the south gallery, not because she believed the room could protect every secret in the kingdom, but because she had seen Vess remove the silver bridge and had watched the foreign mason inspect the resulting gap.

The door shut. There was no answer from the wall. Outside, someone was still laughing at Alden's experiment.

"They will call this a lovers' room by tomorrow," Yara said.

"They already do."

"Then why bring me here?"

"Because I am tired of asking permission to speak without an audience."

Yara had carried Jalen's refusal to send his whole medical examination to Dols. She had also seen the way a clerk could turn a careful refusal into a rumor that a man was hiding the truth. She wanted Miren's help persuading the household to stop passing the same request to him in prettier words.

"He said no," Miren answered. "A different seal does not make the question different."

"I can send that back to the clerk."

"Send it as your judgment. Not as mine. He trusts you to keep the record, and he trusts me to change the cloth. Those are different trusts."

Yara smiled. "You do enjoy telling me when I am doing my own work badly."

"Only when you do it elegantly."

They stood for a moment listening to the footsteps beyond the door. Miren had danced with Yara the night before and enjoyed it. That was the whole fact. Luceran's jealousy did not turn it into a declaration, and court gossip would not turn it into a promise.

"Are you going to speak to him?" Yara asked.

"I speak to him every day."

"Not about the roof."

Miren folded her arms. "He made a lovely evening. He paid people. He let Jalen be absent. I can be glad of those things and still remember what he ordered done to Corven, and what he stole from me when he made the house repeat a private conversation."

Yara did not try to finish the sentence for her. A man could learn a better habit without being owed a return to her bed.

When they left, two attendants looked away too quickly. Miren recognized the look. She said nothing. She could not command their thoughts any more than Luceran could command hers.

---

At the village baker's bench, Orlen Morcant laid his iron quotation beside a charcoal merchant's tally. Hesta's brother had brought the dimensions of the rejected drain block, and two haulers had come because they wanted to know whether there would be enough Crown work to justify keeping their teams nearby.

They were not all friends. One hauler resented Hesta's first offer; the other had once borrowed a wheel from her and returned it cracked. Orlen's price for brackets was honest enough to withstand inspection, but he had not included the cost of carrying them up the last wet turn. Hesta's brother noticed.

"Then your cheap iron arrives by wishing," he said.

"Then put the hauling beside it," Orlen replied. "I want the Crown to compare the whole cost."

The baker moved their papers when she needed the table for dough. No one objected. They paid for bread and a small room behind the shop for the next market afternoon, when a Kaelmont road clerk could attend if his duties allowed. They would compare actual measurements, cartage and delivery conditions before offering Tomas a joint account of prices. Hesta's witnessed thirty-day preference remained lawful; nothing at the baker's bench could annul it.

Orlen was not trying to topple the king. He wanted the next purchase to be one in which his own figures were visible before the Crown thanked someone else for solving the problem. The haulers wanted paid journeys. Hesta's brother wanted his family's existing contract honored without being blamed for every wet road in the mountains.

Above them Edselsto's towers stood against the clouds. The men had not chosen the baker's bench to escape a supernatural listener. It had been a place to argue over carts for years. The distinction would matter later to anyone who decided that business conducted beyond the castle must be treachery.

---

Alden's second attempt began with a joke and acquired an audience because nobody wished to be the first to stop laughing.

He had heard that Iven Marrick served at a prepared threshold. He brought the same companions to the approach and proposed that a person of low birth ask a question, followed by a noble. If the dead keeper answered one and not the other, Alden would have his story.

Tavin was carrying fresh water from the yard. Someone recognized him from the hall and called him over. He stopped because the caller wore good cloth and because he remembered how easily laughter could follow a servant back to work.

Alden offered the silver penny again. "Only ask whether the door knows your name."

"I've got water to carry."

"One question."

Pel Ordan stepped into the passage. "He has said he is working."

Alden lifted both hands. "I mean no harm."

"Then cause none."

The guests were already watching. Tavin could feel the shape of the tale they would tell if he walked away: a boy too frightened to speak to a door. He set the jugs down and said, more loudly than he intended, "My name is Tavin. I'm here for the kitchens. May I pass?"

Pel checked the ordinary passage and opened it.

From the prepared threshold Iven said, "A keeper has admitted the water. Mind the loose flag beyond the turn."

Alden clapped as though the house had declared a winner. Tavin knew better. Pel had opened the passage, and Iven had warned of a stone already marked for repair. But the laughter was louder than his explanation could have been.

Rhyse arrived in time to hear Alden ask whether the keeper would now name the noblest man present.

"No," Iven said.

It was a refusal, not a judgment.

Rhyse dismissed the spectators from the working passage and required Alden to give his name to Dain for the watch account. He did not accuse him of plotting against the Crown or invent a law against curiosity. He told him that another interference with service traffic would cost him access to the household approaches, and that a guest's amusement did not authorize a servant's time.

Alden flushed. His companions had enjoyed the game while he was winning it. They were less eager to stand beside him when a captain began taking names.

Tavin carried the water late. The cook had sent another boy to fetch what was needed, losing half an hour's work between them. She did not punish Tavin for being stopped. She sent the account of the interruption to the household steward and asked for the loose flag to be repaired before someone carrying a full jug fell.

That afternoon Alden came to the kitchen hatch and tried to give Tavin the penny.

"I don't want it," Tavin said.

"I was apologizing."

"Then say it."

Alden did, awkwardly and without an audience. Tavin accepted the words as words. He did not promise to like him.

---

Luceran read Rhyse's account after the west hearth had been inspected again. The flue could be used with care, the mason said, but the roof above it still needed work. The household reserve had paid for one splendid night and had not purchased another winter.

"Was the door actually speaking?" Luceran asked.

"Iven spoke at his appointed threshold," Rhyse said. "A living guard opened the passage. A loose flag was already known to the watch. Those are three different things."

Luceran had heard guests describe the episode as a test of Edselsto's favor. He had almost believed the description because it pleased him that his ancestral home was interesting rather than feared.

He asked the castle what it had heard along the marked south gallery. Edselsto gave him the words of a song sung there and the time at which someone had called for a keeper. It did not identify the singers, their motives or the people who had later met beyond its connected ways. He did not ask it to repeat Miren's conversation.

"Do they think you love them?" he asked the pale silver presence.

"I have kept what I was given to keep," Edselsto replied.

The answer was useful and insufficient. Luceran could not tell whether the guests had learned anything about the house. He could tell that they had learned how easily a servant could be made part of a noble's amusement.

He approved Rhyse's notice and ordered the household steward to charge Alden's visit to the watch account as an interference, not a supernatural incident. The repair of the loose flag and the cook's lost labor would be entered for ordinary payment. He refused a suggestion that every curious guest be shown the withdrawing room; a place made for private speech was not another attraction.

Damaris arrived with the next household figures. The borrowed kettle was at the smith's. Linen and roof repairs still lacked their former reserve. Jorvan's observer had received his proper east-gate duplicate, and no amount of court laughter had reduced the twenty-seven-mark repayment ceiling. The third drain stone remained untested. Dols had not accepted its damaged wall.

"At least the court is enjoying the castle," Luceran said.

"Yes," Damaris answered. "And they have discovered that somebody else carries the water."

By evening the notice stood beside the marked approaches. Some guests called it prudish. Others repeated the story of a door that had refused to name a nobleman. Tavin returned to his ordinary work, knowing that an apology had not stopped the story from travelling.

At the baker's bench below the mountain, Orlen's paid room was reserved for the next market afternoon. A second place for making bargains was taking shape, not by enchantment but by people who preferred to compare prices where the bread was good and no courtier interrupted them.

All seven candles still burned in the family nave.
