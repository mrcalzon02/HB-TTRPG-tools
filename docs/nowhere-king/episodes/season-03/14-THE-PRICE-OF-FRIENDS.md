# Season Three, Episode 14 — The Price of Friends

The awning rope had not broken during the festival. It had waited until the music was over.

That was what the market reeve said, and he had brought three witnesses to prove it. One was the woman who owned the awning, one was a cleaner who had heard the first fibers part at dawn, and the third was a boy who had been sent to fetch a ladder. They agreed on the hour and disagreed on the cause. The reeve wanted the disagreement written down before anybody began charging the village for it.

Lady Sera Sorelle stood in the market square beneath a roof of damp canvas. The rope lay on a table between the witnesses, pale where it had pulled apart and dark where the rain had entered the old twist.

“It was sound when we hired it,” she said.

“It held,” the owner replied. “Sound is another matter.”

“Your own steward examined it.”

“My steward examined the poles. Your men tied the lantern line to this rope.”

Sera looked toward the boy. He was watching the adults with the hopeful alarm of someone who might be asked to swear to something he had only half seen.

“Did you see the lanterns tied there?” she asked him.

“I saw them taken down,” he said. “The knots were still there.”

“Then that is what you saw. Nothing more.”

The owner gave Sera a brief, surprised look. The reeve put the boy's answer beside the cleaner's account, not beneath it. The rope had been worn before the festival; the added lantern weight might have finished it. The village paymaster still held a remainder of the sponsors' deposit, but the owner had not yet produced the cost of replacing the damaged length, and the paymaster would not guess it.

Sera could have sent the matter to her steward. Instead she asked the reeve for the written inspection, the owner's estimate when it arrived, and a chance to examine the knots before the rope was discarded.

“Will you pay?” the owner asked.

“I will pay what the witnesses establish that our use caused. I will not pay for every winter that rope has endured.”

“And I will not mend your festival for nothing.”

“Then we have something in common.”

The owner did not smile. Sera had already discovered that good humor was a poor substitute for a measured account.

Her carriage waited at the edge of the square. Inside lay the copy of her petition to the king, its folded edges rubbed soft from being taken out and read again. She had not asked for a purse of royal money. She had asked for first consideration when the musicians' stand was next wanted and the use of two rooms beside the great hall for the company and its guests.

It had seemed a small return for two paid evenings.

Now she could see the village stand through the rain and the broken rope above it. A thing could look small from a distance because somebody else had to stand beneath its weight.

---

Damaris received the petition in the castle's smaller accounts chamber, not the great hall.

The great hall had two usable hearths and a third that remained cold. Three dry guest chambers were available elsewhere in the household, none of them vacant merely because Sera's company had not slept in them. The upper hangings were still deferred. On the accounts table, a drawing of the receiving-yard gutter lay under a stone so the draft would not lift it.

“The rooms nearest the hall,” Damaris said, tracing Sera's wording with a finger. “Which rooms?”

“The north anteroom and the west service room,” the chamberlain answered.

“The north anteroom is where the linen is counted.”

“Until the next stock delivery.”

“And the west room?”

“Watch meals, dry cloaks, and the roll of men assigned to the upper passage. We moved them there when the gallery works began.”

“Then they are not empty.”

“They are not elegant,” the chamberlain said.

“That is a different fact.”

Tomas came in with the roof men's time book. The two men who had shifted premature festival benches out of the cart road had received their ordinary works pay for that morning. The obstruction had delayed the gutter. Their wages were not waiting on the Sorelle sponsors, but the works account had a separate claim for the interruption. Tomas had marked the work not completed rather than charging it twice.

“The gutter will not care whether the court is offended,” he said. “The first length still leaks.”

Damaris set the book beside the petition. “If we move linen sorting for a performance, the sorters must have another dry place and their ordinary hours must still be paid. If we move the watch, Rhyse must approve a real alternative. If we hire extra hands, somebody must fund them. And if we grant first claim for every future evening, the price cannot be entered until we know how many evenings there will be.”

“The lady did pay for the festival,” the chamberlain said.

“She did. We should say so without turning her receipt into title.”

Luceran entered as Damaris was asking Ysabet to draw a hearing notice. He had imagined Sera standing with her company beneath the lanterns, an old friend asking for a modest favor after a costly success. He had not imagined the west room full of wet cloaks or the north room holding linen that the sickroom still needed counted.

“Could we give her the rooms for one evening?” he asked.

“Perhaps,” Damaris said. “After the people using them speak.”

“I have already promised her a hearing.”

“That promise is in the book. It does not answer the petition.”

Luceran read the names Ysabet had written for summons: the village reeve, the awning owner, the musicians' representative, the linen keeper, the watch captain, a worker from the roof, Sera, and any other household seeking the same use. It was a longer list than the guest list at the festival's first supper.

“Will Sera think this a trial?” he asked.

“She may,” Ysabet said. “We can make it a hearing instead by allowing the witnesses to answer without being punished for what they say.”

---

The musicians' leader, Corin Vale, was late because he had been paid to teach two village children a tune.

He came to the hearing carrying a small ledger and a fiddle case with a cracked latch. The company had been paid for both festival nights, first and final wages, in front of the village paymaster. That was not in dispute. What he disputed was the idea that his company might be summoned by the person who controlled the stand.

“The stand belongs to the village,” he said. “The company belongs to its members. Those are two different things.”

Sera inclined her head. “I asked for first consideration, not compulsory service.”

“If the village gives you first claim to the place, another patron must ask your leave before offering us a better bargain. We might refuse you, and still be unable to play there for someone else.”

“Then what would you consider fair?”

“A dated booking, paid in advance. No permanent preference. And no promise made in our name without our consent.”

The reeve nodded. “The council can consider a booking when the road and market are free. We cannot promise the stand for all future seasons. The grain carts do not stop coming because the king enjoys music.”

Sera's steward opened his mouth. She raised a hand before he could speak.

“I understand the carts,” she said. “My request was made because another house has begun offering entertainment in the same weeks, and we cannot keep hiring players and cloth workers on uncertain notice. If we are always last to know when the hall or square is available, we will lose our company to patrons who can promise dates.”

Corin looked at her differently. She had at last described a real problem.

“Then ask for a calendar,” he said. “Not a throne over the stand.”

Alden Pell had come on behalf of relatives who wished to sponsor a winter reading and relief collection. He brought a proposed date and a signed estimate for bread, candles and two readers' fees. His relatives had not deposited the money. He said so before Ysabet could ask.

“I would like the same notice of available dates,” he said. “I do not ask to be ahead of Sorelle. I do ask not to be behind her forever because she arranged the first festival.”

Sera's gaze sharpened. “Your cousins were happy to call it a royal triumph when the musicians were playing.”

“They were,” Alden said. “They did not pay the musicians. I have brought no claim that they did.”

One of the Pell retainers frowned at him from the benches. Alden noticed and continued anyway.

“The reading would be open to villagers. That does not give my family the right to close the road. We can ask the council as Sorelle did.”

Luceran remembered Alden's old delight in testing servants at doors, and the later story of his rebuke to a cousin who had wagered on Tessa's identity. Neither memory entitled Alden to a reward. Both made it difficult to dismiss the man before him as a single unchanging mistake.

The reeve requested that the two houses submit actual dates rather than arguing about imaginary future evenings. Ysabet entered the request in the hearing record. No calendar had yet been granted, and no future event had been approved.

---

The linen keeper, Mistress Fera, placed three bundles on the table.

The first held clean sickroom covers, already supplied from existing stock. The second held worn sheets awaiting mending. The third held a book in which every remaining usable length of household linen was counted. It was not an impressive collection for a castle that had entertained a masked court.

“The north anteroom is dry,” she said. “That is why we use it. I can move the book for a day. I cannot move the wet mending into a passage and pretend it will dry.”

Sera looked at the bundles. “Could my household lend cloth?”

“You could,” Fera said. “You could also pay the hands to carry it and make sure it is suitable. Cloth offered for a feast is not automatically a sickroom cover.”

“I did not mean to insult your work.”

“I did not say you did.”

A young laundress named Berenna, summoned because she had worked the last festival cleanup, asked whether the proposed room use included washing after the guests. The chamberlain said the room could be restored before the next morning's linen count. She asked who would do the restoring and whether the extra hours were included in Sera's petition.

They were not.

Sera wrote a note to her steward. Berenna watched the pencil move.

“Will that be a wage?” she asked.

“It will be a costed offer,” Sera said. “You need not accept it.”

Berenna nodded once. She had heard the difference.

Rhyse spoke next. The west service room was where his men changed wet cloaks and took their meals between watches. It also held the current upper-gallery duty roll. The guarded recess behind the linen cupboard had not become a public curiosity because the festival had ended; the living watch still controlled the approach. The separate lower stair remained barred.

“I can move a meal,” Rhyse said. “I cannot remove the watch. If your guests require the west room, you must show me a safe alternative with water, heat and passage. There is no spare hearth waiting to be lit for convenience.”

“We could bring braziers,” Sera's steward suggested.

“Not into a room beside old timber and hanging cloth without Pell's inspection,” Rhyse replied. “And not on a stair used by guards carrying a man who is hurt.”

Pell Falbrecht, standing near the door, confirmed that no inspection had been requested. He had enough men measuring the gutter and roof to know the cost of a casual assurance.

Sera looked at the room plan. She had imagined a place for musicians to set down instruments and a place for guests to wait before entering the hall. Now one was a ledger and linen room; the other was a living watch station. The plan had not lied. She had simply not asked what the squares contained.

“You could tell me no,” she said to Rhyse.

“I could,” he replied. “I am telling you why.”

---

Miren had asked to attend as a witness, not as a petitioner. She sat at the side of the chamber with Yara and made no attempt to sit beside Luceran.

When the hearing recessed, Sera found her beside the narrow window.

“You must think I have made a foolish request,” Sera said.

“I think you made a request before you saw the cost.”

“Every court request is made before somebody shows the cost. That is why people keep accountants.”

“You paid the musicians when others would have called the promise payment. That matters.”

“And now I am told that it matters so little I cannot have two rooms.”

“You are told that the people in the rooms matter too.”

Sera leaned against the stone, suddenly tired. She had spent weeks persuading musicians to come, merchants to advance supplies, nobles to bring their own cups, the council to release the square for two measured evenings. She had kept her house's reputation through a rainstorm and a broken rope. The festival had succeeded, and success had made the next petition harder rather than easier.

“My mother kept this company alive through three winters,” she said. “If I cannot give them dates, I will lose them to a house that can. The court will still ask why Sorelle no longer brings music.”

“Then ask the court to publish its dates,” Miren answered. “Ask the village to publish its own. Ask for a hearing before a season is filled. Those things are worth having, and other people can use them.”

“Other houses will use them first.”

“Some will. You cannot demand that they remain grateful enough to wait behind you.”

Sera looked toward the hearing room. “Luceran used to say I could make a feast out of a bare table.”

“He may still believe it.”

“Then why does he not help me?”

Miren took time to answer. She remembered the king who had ordered Edselsto to repeat words spoken in confidence, and the king who had confessed to Corven's killing only after his denial had injured people who could not compel him to speak. She also remembered the man who had stopped himself from making a promise to Sera under the lanterns. Better conduct could be seen without being exchanged for intimacy.

“He is trying to learn the difference between helping a friend and making other people pay for the friendship,” she said.

“That is a noble lesson when you are not the friend.”

“Yes,” Miren said. “It is.”

Sera waited for a defense of the king. None came. Miren was not going to turn a private conversation into testimony for Luceran, nor would she turn Sera's disappointment into a reason to abandon the workers.

“Will you speak for me?” Sera asked.

“I will say what I saw: your house paid for an evening that people enjoyed, and the village kept its authority. I will not promise rooms I do not use.”

“Then at least you are not pretending to agree.”

Sera returned to the hearing before the bell. Miren remained by the window until Yara came to tell her the witnesses were assembling. She went back to her own seat.

---

The afternoon hearing began with the accounts rather than the king.

Damaris laid the festival receipts on the table. The musicians' first and final wages were witnessed. The cleaners' hire was paid. The additional watch hours had named entries. The village paymaster retained the awning deposit remainder pending the rope owner's measured claim. The roof men's interrupted morning was recorded as paid works labor, with a separate claim to the sponsors for the obstruction. None of those costs had been taken from the east-gate net pledged under Othra Jorvan's twenty-four-mark advance and twenty-seven-mark repayment cap.

“There is a difference,” Damaris said, “between honoring the money a house spent and awarding that house the power to decide what everyone else may do next.”

Sera did not deny the distinction. She asked whether the Crown could offer a regular booking notice, issued before wealthy houses filled the calendar by private conversation.

The chamberlain said he could publish the hall's available dates, provided the notices did not imply that a hall event was funded, approved or entitled to lodging. The village reeve said the council would consider a similar notice for the stand, but the council had not voted on it and the Crown could not order it. Corin Vale said the musicians would decide which offers to accept; their company could not be listed as available without its consent.

Alden Pell asked whether the first notice could be given to all houses at once.

“Yes,” the chamberlain said, “if I am permitted to say that the first notice promises nothing.”

Luceran listened to the room take shape around an answer he had not brought into it.

Sera rose.

“I ask the king to decide my petition as written,” she said. “I do not want it quietly changed into a favor so small that no one can tell whether I received it.”

The room became still. A lesser patron might have accepted a vague promise and told her friends it meant precedence. Sera wanted a public answer, even if it was no.

Luceran asked Ysabet to read the request aloud. She read it exactly, including the words *first consideration* and *the two rooms nearest the great hall*. The clerk then read the village reeve's objection, the musicians' independent terms, the linen keeper's statement, Rhyse's watch requirement, and the competing date request from Alden Pell's house. Each witness was named beside the part they could actually attest.

Luceran stood.

“I grant no standing precedence at the village musicians' stand,” he said. “The village council controls its use, and the musicians control their own labor. I grant no continuing claim to either hall-adjacent room. The north room is in use for linen and sickroom supply, and the west room for the living watch. They will not be displaced by a favor I have not paid for.”

Sera's expression barely changed. Luceran had known her long enough to recognize the effort.

“I will direct the chamberlain to issue an equal written notice of any hall date offered for private hire, to Sorelle and every other house that asks to receive it,” he continued. “The notice grants no banquet allowance, no free staff, no guest chamber and no right to command the village. If Lady Sera wishes to request a particular date, the officers and present users will cost that date before I decide it. Any hiring must be paid and agreed in advance. The village may adopt its own notice or decline; that is the council's decision.”

He paused. He could have added a private assurance that Sera would be first in his thoughts. She would have understood it as an advantage, and the others would have learned of it later.

“I thank House Sorelle for what it actually paid,” he said. “I do not repay a debt of gratitude with another person's room.”

Ysabet wrote the decision. The reeve witnessed the boundary on the village's authority. Fera and Rhyse witnessed the room uses. Corin witnessed that no musician had been promised. Sera asked for a copy, and Ysabet made one without charging it to the festival deposit.

“Your Majesty,” Sera said, “I accept that this is your decision. I do not call it a reward.”

“No,” Luceran said. “I do not suppose you would.”

---

The loss appeared before the room had emptied.

Sera's steward approached with a second paper, a draft offer to underwrite a winter court music evening if the house could secure reliable rehearsal space. Sera read it, folded it once and handed it back.

“Do not lodge that offer,” she said. “We have no rooms and no date. I will not pledge the company to a performance we cannot house.”

The steward looked toward Luceran, as if the king might rescue the promise.

“He has just told us not to do that,” Sera said.

Luceran heard. He had hoped the public decision would preserve enough of the friendship that she would still bring music to Edselsto. It might, in time. It had not done so today.

Outside the accounts chamber, Alden Pell caught up with Sera. His family wanted the same notices, but he had watched her pay the price of asking first.

“I could speak to my cousins about hiring your players for our reading,” he said.

“You could,” she replied. “But do not offer them my company as a peace gift before you have asked the company.”

He flushed. “I have done that before.”

“I know.”

“I was trying to be useful.”

“So was I.”

They walked together as far as the stairs. At the landing, Sera turned toward the village rather than the guest passage. Her carriage would take her past the market awning. She had a rope account to settle and musicians who needed to know she could not promise a winter court date.

Alden went back to his relatives with no musicians secured and no right to claim that the king had preferred him. It was less than his cousins wanted. It was more truthful than the story they would have told.

---

At the village paymaster's table, the awning owner had brought the rope maker's estimate.

The maker had inspected the worn length and the lantern knots. His account separated the cost of replacing the whole aged span from the additional damage reasonably attributable to the festival's load. The cleaner and reeve signed as witnesses to the condition they had seen, not as experts in rope making. Sera accepted the portion charged to the sponsors and asked that it be paid from the retained deposit first. The remainder was not enough to cover the assessed festival portion. Her steward entered the balance as a new Sorelle obligation, not as a debt owed by the village.

The paymaster recorded the liability. He did not mark it paid.

“Will the awning be ready for market day?” Sera asked.

“If the owner hires the maker,” the reeve said. “The council will inspect the repair before permitting a crowd beneath it.”

“And if the house pays the balance tomorrow?”

“Then the paymaster will record tomorrow's payment tomorrow.”

Sera almost laughed. The day's answer seemed to be that tomorrow would remain tomorrow, however much she wished to arrange it today.

Corin Vale was waiting near the steps with his fiddle case. He had heard the king's decision from the reeve, not from a noble's messenger.

“Will there be another court evening?” he asked.

“I cannot promise one.”

“Good,” he said. “I have been offered a wedding in the west village on the same week your steward proposed. It pays less, but the date is real.”

“Take it if you wish.”

“I might. I wanted you to hear it from me.”

Sera thought of her mother keeping the company together through the lean winters. A patron could help musicians survive and still have no claim upon their next song. She had known that in her accounts. It was harder to know it in the wet square.

“I hope the bride dances well,” she said.

“She will have to. Her brother plays faster than I do.”

They parted without a bargain. Sera had lost a possible winter performance, not the right to make another honest offer.

---

Luceran found Miren at the end of the south passage, where the lamps made a soft line across the floor and stopped before the physically disconnected withdrawing room.

He did not enter it. He waited in the passage where she could see him.

“You heard the decision,” he said.

“I did.”

“I think I have lost a friend.”

“You may have disappointed one. Those are not the same.”

“She withdrew her offer for the winter music.”

“She could not responsibly make it without a date or rooms.”

“I know. That does not make the hall warmer.”

Miren looked toward the window. The receiving-yard roof could be seen beyond the inner wall, men still working beneath a gutter that would take more than one dry afternoon to finish. A messenger passed with a folded wage sheet. Somewhere below, a cart wheel struck a stone and the driver swore at the delay.

“You could have given Sera the rooms,” she said. “Fera would have found somewhere for the linen. Rhyse would have shifted the watch. They would have obeyed you.”

“And I would have owed them an explanation after the fact.”

“You would have owed them their time and their work whether you explained it or not.”

He did not ask whether she was proud of him. He had asked her to dance at the festival and accepted her refusal. Today he accepted that she had watched the hearing as a person with judgment of her own, not as a witness brought to certify his improvement.

“I wish there were a way to keep the music and keep the rooms,” he said.

“There may be. It will have a date, a cost, and people who can say no.”

“And Sera?”

“She may choose another hall.”

Luceran nodded. He wanted to ask whether Miren would stay with him that evening. The wish was familiar, and the limits were familiar too. He did not turn a difficult day in government into a claim on her affection.

“Thank you for coming,” he said.

“I came to hear the petition.”

“I know.”

She gave him a small smile, neither cold nor reconciled, and went toward her own business. The south-gallery withdrawing room remained disconnected; no silver way had been restored, and no private speech had been retrieved from it. Luceran stayed where he was until the passage grew quiet.

---

At evening accounts Damaris entered the hearing as a decision, not a festival expense.

The canceled great-hall supper remained canceled. The third hearth remained cold. Three dry guest chambers were still three. The north anteroom remained assigned to linen counting and sickroom supply; the west service room remained assigned to the living watch. The chamberlain had an instruction to prepare an equal-notice procedure, not permission to sell dates or displace their users. The village council had received a copy of the king's refusal to claim its stand, not an order to change its booking rules.

Tomas entered another measured length of gutter work, with the earlier lost morning still identified in the works book. The roof and drain were not finished. Remaining stopped-worker wages and ordinary household obligations did not vanish behind the day's applause. Jorvan's east-gate pledge remained a bounded twenty-four-mark advance against a twenty-seven-mark cap, with its twelve-market-day net and limited four-day half-net tail untouched. Hesta's first refusal and toll rights, her uninspected third block, and Orlen's unaccepted higher iron quotation remained what they had been. Edda's Kaelmont disclosure still had no answer by ordinary post. Dols had granted no military crossing, Merehaven no second advance, and Teth no involuntary ferry. The grain sacks remained divided six north and six at Edselsto; eighteen patrol mounts remained withdrawn from Crown service.

Ysabet filed the hearing under the names of its witnesses. The upper-gallery inscription's two direct impressions remained separately sealed records of one surface, the reversed transfer remained marked as error, and the anonymous lower-return sketch remained uncertified. The guarded recess had not been opened to the petitioners, and the separate lower stair remained barred. No scholar had read a new phrase from the stone.

In the family nave, Canon Merel counted the lights without ceremony. The first ancestral candle was dark and untouched. The other six burned.

Sera rode home with a copy of the king's refusal and a rope bill still partly unpaid. She would have to tell her mother that the company had no winter court date. She would also have to tell her steward to stop describing the two festival nights as an investment that had bought them rooms.

Luceran ate a plain supper near one of the two working hearths. There was no music. He remembered Sera's face beneath the festival lanterns and the harder look she had worn when she asked him to decide the petition as written.

He had given her a public answer she could use.

It was not the answer she wanted, and it had cost them both something.

For once he did not ask Edselsto to tell him what she would say about it when she reached home.
