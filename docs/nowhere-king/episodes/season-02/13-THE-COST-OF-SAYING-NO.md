# Season Two, Episode 13 — The Cost of Saying No

The bridge watch at Dols had set a table beneath a tarred awning so that nobody could later claim the survey had been held in secret.

Captain Savelin arrived with two men, a measuring line and a letter authorizing him to inspect a civilian road. He had left his troop dispositions at Edselsto. The bridge reeve had brought a carpenter, a clerk and the watch captain who had refused him passage on his previous visit.

The ferryman was there too.

"You have my hook," the man said by way of greeting.

"I have the name of the smith who will mend it," Savelin answered. "And a receipt for his price. I ought to have brought the coin sooner."

The ferryman turned the receipt over in his fingers. He had caught Savelin when the captain slipped from a wet landing; the iron hook had bent under their combined weight. A royal captain's gratitude had not straightened it. Savelin had arranged payment from his own purse rather than enter a foreign boatman's damaged tool as a Crown military charge.

"Your king pays debts in interesting ways," the reeve observed.

"His captain pays this one."

They walked the western approach while the river rose against the bridge piers. The watch captain showed Savelin the old wheel ruts where wagons could wait without blocking the ferry track. A civilian grain cart could pass there in fair weather. A file of armed men could not, unless the bridge council permitted it, and the council had permitted nothing of the kind.

At the lower bend, the reeve pushed his staff into a strip of dark ground. Water welled around the tip.

"Three days ago that held a cart," he said.

Savelin knelt. The bank had slumped beneath a turf covering. The upper road was sound, but its approach narrowed where a loaded wagon would need to turn. A man with a flag could keep carts apart. He could not prevent a wheel from dropping through rotten ground.

"Shore it with timber," Savelin said.

"Whose timber?" asked the reeve.

"That is what we must settle."

The Dols carpenter could bring stakes and a working party if the Crown paid for the material and the bridge council kept the right to inspect the work. Savelin offered to have the king's carters deliver timber, but he could not sign for the council. Nor could the reeve sign away the council's military countersign.

By noon they had a measured sketch, a proposed daylight cart interval and a list of repairs. They had no permission to move a convoy.

"The council will hear it after the next sitting," the reeve said. "If the river does not rise another hand."

Savelin could not turn the river down by giving an order. He sealed his report, thanked the ferryman for saving him a second time from a poor judgment of the bank, and sent a rider toward Edselsto.

The day of survey had been spent. The road was still closed to the grain it was meant to carry.

At Tervain's lower barns, the grain was dry, weighed and going nowhere.

The steward had separated the Crown's contracted sacks from the tenants' winter stores. He had done so in the presence of two village reeves, because a lord's written promise to preserve seed was of little use if a hurried wagon-master emptied the wrong bins.

A Crown messenger arrived asking when the purchase would leave.

"When the Crown sends carts," the steward replied.

"His Majesty was told the grain could be delivered."

"He was told it could be sold. Our draft teams are hauling the tenants' last hay from the lower fields. The bargain does not include taking their horses away before the frost."

The messenger read the agreement again. He found the price, the number of sacks, the right of the tenants to retain seed and winter bread, and the place at which the grain could be collected. He did not find a promise of Tervain wagons.

The steward did not enjoy making the distinction. He knew what hungry garrisons did to the villages they were supposed to defend. But a winter shipment purchased with next spring's sowing would merely move the hunger forward.

He offered the messenger a dry store for another five days without charge. After that he would need the space for the estate's own stores, and the Crown would pay rent or collect its sacks.

"Tell your king I have kept his grain," he said. "That is not the same as having carried it."

The messenger rode away with an honest answer and no grain.

Hesta Vale had learned to distrust any contract in which a road was described as if it were empty.

Her quarry wagons needed the Crown track downhill. The receiving yard needed grain uphill. Yara Fen's League carts might eventually serve both, but they were not yet authorized to cross Dols, and Hesta would not gamble four seasons of toll-free Crown-track access on a foreign council's future consent.

They met in the yard while carpenters fitted the last rafters of the roof. Rain had returned before the tiles. Two teams waited in the open, their drivers arguing about who had arrived first.

"Your porter has been working three days without a settled master," Hesta said.

"He has been paid for three days," Yara answered. "You paid the first. I paid the next two."

"He will need paying tomorrow."

Yara put the draft between them. It concerned only the use of the road and the carts once the lawful permissions existed. It could not bind Dols, Teth or Merehaven, and it did not disturb the Crown's right to close the track for military need. The proposed hours gave loaded grain wagons the narrow climb in the morning and stone wagons the descent after the midday meal. The porter would be paid from both carriage charges. A road keeper's neglect and a carter's negligence would not be called the same thing merely to spare an argument.

Hesta read the damage clause twice.

"Your brother needs the return stone," she said.

Yara looked up.

"Who told you?"

"Nobody. You asked after a warehouse on the river and then after a buyer for blocks small enough to fit a barge. My brother measures stone for a living. I am not required to be foolish because you are discreet."

"The floods took part of his quay. If he can keep the barges working, he may keep the warehouse."

"Then say so. I will not sign a private favor disguised as the League's instruction."

Yara told her what she could: her brother owned the warehouse, the freight might help him, and she had no authority to promise League trade to him. Hesta could choose another buyer for the stone if she preferred.

"I prefer a buyer who can pay," Hesta said. "And a woman who tells me why she wants a bargain."

They signed a limited private carriage agreement, conditional upon the required public consents. Hesta retained her existing Crown-track right; Yara bound only her own commercial arrangements and the carts she could lawfully hire. The porter received his named wage and a written instruction that he was not to wave a loaded wagon into a blind bend merely because a noble was late.

The agreement did not move a single sack across Dols. It did mean two women had put money and reputation behind a road they expected other people to use.

When Yara carried the copy to Corven, she told him about her brother before he had to ask.

He listened, then said, "You have not sold a council warrant?"

"No."

"Good. We shall declare the interest when we ask the benches to approve the carriage. A private profit can be honest. A hidden one becomes a weapon in another man's hands."

Yara was relieved and irritated that he had understood so quickly.

The first letter from the northern posts was not a plea for more soldiers.

It asked permission to send horses south.

Rhyse brought it to Damaris with the figures marked in a rough hand. The northern garrison had enough grain for its men if the ration was tightened, but not enough fodder for every patrol mount through the expected snow. The officer proposed returning twelve horses and six spare mounts to lower pasture before the road closed. He could hold the post. He could not ride the same winter circuit afterward.

"Which circuit?" Damaris asked.

"The eastern timber track and the two hamlets beyond it. They will have foot patrols when weather permits, not the regular mounted round."

She studied the map. One hamlet had lost cattle to raiders in the previous winter. The other maintained a bridge the Crown had promised to inspect. A reduced patrol was not a line in a ledger. It was a road on which help would arrive later.

Tomas set down Jorvan's offer. The lender still wanted two named market-gate tolls for the shorter loan. Those tolls were also meant to pay for spring repairs. Damaris had asked for the cost of the hinges, stone and wages before agreeing to pledge them. The answer was now beside the offer, and it was larger than Tomas had hoped.

"We could accept," he said. "The lender has offered actual coin."

"We could. Then the gates must wait, or we must find another purse to mend them. Tell me which."

"The road tolls might recover."

"Might is not a repair order."

He did not press her. She did not pretend refusal was painless. She instructed him to offer one named gate for a smaller advance and a fixed repayment date, with the other gate's repair money protected. Jorvan could reject it. No money was borrowed merely because the letter was sent.

For the immediate shortage she authorized Crown carters to collect a first portion of Tervain's purchased grain from the lower barns. They would be drawn from nonessential quarry haulage for two days, with the owners paid and Hesta notified before the teams were taken. The grain could reach the lower store; there was still no secure League route or enough transport to supply every northern post at its former rate.

"His Majesty will have to sign the horse order," she said.

Tomas hesitated. "Shall I call it a precaution?"

"Call it what it is. Eighteen mounts removed from winter patrol because the fodder has not arrived. If he is to bear the cost, he must be permitted to see it."

Corven had begun writing his wife a shorter letter every evening, not because there was less to say, but because he no longer trusted himself to explain Edselsto without making it sound either kinder or stranger than it was.

Leora's mother had recovered enough to complain about the physician. Their niece had returned the coal scuttle. The ordinary news hurt more than Corven expected. He wanted to be home for a quarrel about coal.

Yara brought him the signed private carriage agreement and the admission about her brother. He approved her candor, not the League's absent consent. He would send the draft to the benches with the personal interest plainly named.

Rhyse came to escort him to a meeting with Damaris. Corven put down his pen.

"Has the danger you did not name changed?"

"No new attempt has been reported," Rhyse said.

"That is not the question I asked."

"I know."

Corven waited. The captain's refusal to lie had become more troubling than a convenient falsehood would have been.

"I have three councils to answer to," Corven said. "If I depart because I fear an insult, I may leave them without a voice. If I remain because I have not been told a real danger, you have chosen for me. I cannot judge which duty I owe without the truth."

Rhyse had not seen a human plot against Corven. He had seen a written royal refusal to an appalling suggestion. He also knew that a royal refusal could be changed by the same hand that signed it. He could not honestly call the envoy safe because the king had been honorable once.

"I have carried your request," he said. "I will carry it again. If I judge that a new order places you in danger, I will not wait until the danger has passed to seek your warning."

"That is a captain's answer."

"It is the only answer I can give you today."

Corven picked up the letter to Leora. He did not demand Rhyse break his oath in the corridor. He did not mistake that courtesy for consent to remain ignorant forever.

"Then please allow me time to finish this before we go," he said.

Rhyse waited by the door while Corven wrote that he missed the sound of her arguing with her mother.

Nessa Rell refused to call the second descent delayed by mystery.

It was delayed by timber.

Pell Falbrecht had drawn a brace that could support the opened lintel from the safe side, but the required lengths were not lying idle in the yard. The roof carpenters needed their beams first. Talla Rusk had obtained a fitted shutter for the cellar opening; the drain still needed clearing before another party tracked mud across the floor. The injured worker could walk without a crutch, but Pell would not return him to carrying stone until the physician's assistant agreed.

Vess offered Nessa a look at the old measurements while the timber was found.

"I have looked," she said. "The numbers have not improved."

"Could you draw a conjectural lower plan?"

"I could draw a hundred. None would hold that lintel."

She had other work waiting beyond Edselsto: a mill foundation whose owner wanted an answer before the river froze. If the Crown meant to retain her for the second inspection, it would pay for the days it asked her to remain. If not, she would leave her measured section and return when summoned with proper notice.

Vess did not threaten to replace her. He asked Damaris for two paid days to settle the brace and the cellar protection, and accepted that the deeper survey might wait until Nessa could return. Talla insisted that her casks be counted again after the shutter was fitted.

The old openings still faced the lower turn. Nothing about their direction had changed. Nobody had learned whom they were meant to stop.

Luceran received the northern officer's request after supper.

He read the number of horses twice. Damaris had marked the lost patrol circuit and the villages it served. Savelin's report from Dols lay beneath it: sound bridge, unsafe western approach, a civilian survey completed, timber needed, a council hearing still to come, no military countersign. Beside it was the signed private carriage agreement that could not take effect without the river benches.

"Corven has not refused to hear us," Luceran said.

"No," Damaris replied. "Nor has he given away authority he does not possess."

"Then I have done everything properly and the eastern track loses its patrol."

"You have done some things properly. Other people have done their work properly too. The grain is still in the wrong place."

He did not resent her for saying it. He resented that she was right.

Ysabet read Savelin's measured sketch and asked that the bridge council's conditions be entered exactly. The Crown could pay for shoring and submit a civilian passage plan; it could not convert the survey into a warrant for troops. Rhyse required the king to see Corven's renewed request for warning. The sealed proposal remained in Ysabet's keeping. She did not bring it into the open council, and Damaris still did not know its words.

"I will sign the withdrawal," Luceran said at last. "Eighteen horses to lower pasture. The post commander will keep enough mounted men for urgent messages and tell the two hamlets which patrols are lost. Send a copy to their reeves. I will not have them learn it when nobody comes."

Rhyse asked for two foot patrols to be assigned where weather allowed. Luceran approved the expense, knowing that men on foot could not cover the same ground as horses.

He approved the first Tervain collection teams, authorized the bridge approach timber subject to Dols inspection, and sent Jorvan the narrower counteroffer. He would not promise a second gate before its repairs were priced and protected. He asked Damaris to show him the next ration report rather than spare him another unpleasant number.

None of those orders restored the lost mounted circuit that night.

Later Miren found him beside the south-gallery window. He had brought no council papers into the physically deaf room.

"Did you sign it?" she asked.

"Yes. I have left two villages with less protection than they had yesterday."

"Then they deserve to know who made the decision."

"They will."

She stood close to him, and after a while he rested his forehead against hers. She did not tell him the choice was harmless because it had been necessary. She did not withdraw her affection because he was angry.

"Corven has asked Rhyse to warn him," Luceran said.

"I know only what you have told me. What will you do?"

"I have refused the castle. I have not changed that."

"That is an answer about the castle."

He understood the distinction. It was not yet an answer about the guest.

Outside, the receiving-yard roof stood unfinished over empty space. The bridge council still held its countersign. The grain was contracted, the carts were being hired, and the horses had begun their journey down from the northern post. A refusal had spared one man from a monstrous proposal. It had not fed a garrison, mended a road or restored a patrol.

In the family nave, all seven candles burned.
