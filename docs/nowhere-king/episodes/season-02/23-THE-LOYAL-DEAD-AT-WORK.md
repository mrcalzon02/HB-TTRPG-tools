# Season Two, Episode 23 — The Loyal Dead at Work

Pel Ordan found the mistake in the watch book because his own name had been crossed out.

The inner gate was to have two living men until third bell. The new sheet had one living man and a note in the second place: *Marrick, as before.* Pel had been sent to the receiving roof with the spare guard, although he had not been asked whether he could lift a beam or judge a failing brace.

He carried the book to the oak door.

"Iven. Did you agree to take the second watch?"

"Which second watch?"

"The one they have given you."

There was a pause. The grain around the hinge did not change.

"I agreed to keep this gate."

"That's what it says."

"It says you aren't here."

Pel glanced toward the outer arch, where a cart was being turned by two men who had no reason to know what a dead soldier could hear.

"Can you tell whether that fellow has a knife?"

"No."

"Can you tell whether he's a friend?"

"Not by his boots."

Pel had once fought on the wrong side of these walls. Iven had refused him passage even after the living captain cleared him. The old injury had not become friendship merely because both now served the same watch.

"Do you trust me yet?" Pel asked.

"No."

"Then you may be pleased to hear I'm not leaving you alone."

Rhyse arrived to find the roof officer demanding his guard and Pel refusing to move. He read the amended watch sheet, then the original order that required a living post beside Iven. Neither had been superseded.

"Who changed this?"

The roof officer named a junior clerk who had copied the work demands into the watch book. The clerk had seen a name occupying the gate and assumed it was a man available for a duty. He had not asked the man, living or dead.

Rhyse struck through the substitution in the presence of both officers. He sent the roof officer back to Pell to request a properly assigned work guard and signed the correction himself. The roof work would wait for a living guard if it needed one.

Pel looked at the oak.

"You might have said you wanted me here."

"I didn't," Iven said.

Rhyse almost smiled. Pel did not.

The gate remained covered by two living men and one dead keeper whose permission to remain had never been a permission to count him twice.

---

Mara Marrick came after breakfast with her younger son, Tomas Marrick, and a wool scarf folded in a basket.

The scarf had belonged to Iven. She had found it at the bottom of a traveling chest, beneath a pair of boots he had been saving for a winter he had not lived to see.

"Don't hang it on the door," Iven said.

"I haven't said I would."

"You brought it all the way from the capital."

"I brought your brother. The scarf was easier to carry."

Tomas put his palm against the wood. Iven recognized him after he spoke, not before. Tomas pretended not to notice the delay. His mother noticed and asked nothing until the living guards had moved far enough away to grant them a little courtesy.

"Do you remember the boots?" she asked.

"Brown."

"Black."

"I had brown ones."

"Those split at the heel. You were saving the black pair."

Iven was quiet.

His mother could not tell whether the silence was embarrassment, fatigue, or the thinning of something no one knew how to measure. She would not let the court turn her uncertainty into a useful proof.

"I don't need the scarf," Iven said at last.

"I know. It is mine to remember you in."

Tomas looked at the two guards standing where Iven had died. "They've put your name on a watch sheet again."

"Pel corrected it."

"Then why does everyone keep asking whether you can do more?"

"Because I can do something."

His mother placed the basket on the bench, not against the door.

"Your father could mend a shoe," she said. "That didn't mean the whole street owned his evenings."

"I can leave."

"I know."

"Then don't speak as though I cannot."

Mara touched the iron strap. The oak felt cold through her fingers.

"I am trying to learn the difference between visiting my son and visiting his place of work."

For once Iven had no answer ready. Tomas sat with the basket until his mother was ready to go. They left the scarf in her keeping.

---

In the pay room Damaris had three petitions, one ordinary shortage and an excellent reason to be angry.

The shortage was in living watch wages. The receiving roof, the repaired east recess and the escort arrangements had all consumed men who would otherwise have rotated through the gates. No silver saved by a dead keeper had bought the Crown an extra pair of boots or fed a soldier's children.

The first petition asked whether a deceased gate soldier might be approached for service at a second door. It supplied no evidence that the soldier had wished for such a thing while alive. The second came from a household officer who wanted an archive threshold watched at night without paying a night clerk. The third, written by a relative of another dead man, asked whether service could secure a continued allowance for his widow.

Aldren had put all three under a copied heading: *Requests concerning extraordinary household service.*

"Who gave you that heading?" Damaris asked.

"I made it so they wouldn't be lost."

"Then you have made a class of work before anyone has agreed there is a worker."

Aldren flushed. "I haven't granted anything."

"No. But the widow will read that heading and think we can employ her husband's death. The officer will read it and think he can dismiss a clerk."

She pushed the ordinary wage book toward him. Pel's name remained in its proper place. The roof officer's request was awaiting Rhyse's signature. The two books had to agree before anyone was paid or reassigned.

"Mrs. Marrick's household compensation was awarded because Iven died in service," Damaris said. "It is not rent paid on his continued obedience. That rule was signed when he first spoke."

"Suppose the family asks for wages instead?"

"Then we explain the difference. We do not make a mother dependent upon her son being unable to rest."

Aldren took back his petitions. The archive officer's request was plainly about money, but the widow's relative might be frightened and sincere. He had put both beneath the same heading because it saved him a second page.

"Should I destroy them?"

"No. Find out what each person actually wants. Put the widow's claim with family compensation, not with a proposal to bind a dead man. Let the archive officer account for the clerk's wages he hopes to save."

He looked at the blank form from yesterday's cart-hire exception, lying behind the signed original with Ysabet's warning across it. He had thought a good form made a difficult decision safer.

Now he began three separate sheets.

---

Canon Merel held the afternoon hearing in the family nave because he refused to have a dead man's consent discussed in the room where roof timber was priced.

Calienne came from the sickroom with a stained sleeve and a physician's list of men still needing paid dressings. Mara and Tomas Marrick attended as family, not as advocates for the Crown. Pel stood by the door because he had been asked to say what a living watch actually did.

The archive officer argued that a threshold which could answer questions at night might preserve sealed papers from theft. He had brought a drawing of the archive door and a calculation of the candles a clerk consumed.

"Who would answer?" Merel asked.

"Someone willing."

"Name the person."

"I cannot."

"Then you have proposed a door, not a servant."

The officer said he meant no disrespect. Merel believed him. That did not improve the proposal.

A relative of the deceased soldier asked whether it was wrong to hope for one more conversation with a man whose burial had already taken place. Calienne did not tell him to stop hoping.

"It is not wrong to want him back," she said. "It would be wrong for us to promise that he is waiting to be employed."

Mara spoke without standing.

"When Iven answered us, I asked whether he knew who he was. His brother asked things I had forgotten. Then Iven chose again, after death, with us there. Even that does not mean I can tell you what tomorrow will feel like to him."

Merel opened the original gate terms. Willingness, revocation, no punishment for departure, no transfer without consent, no substitution for living watches, family access independent of royal favor. Each clause had been made in front of a family that could say no.

"The Crown can pay a mason to prepare a socket," he said. "It cannot purchase a dead man's assent by preparing one."

Pel described the changed watch sheet. Iven had heard him at the gate; he could not have searched a visitor, read a pass or followed a fleeing thief into the yard. The officer listened and amended his candle calculation. He still wanted a night clerk relieved, but now he knew he was asking someone else to bear a risk he had not priced.

No one was summoned from the dead. Merel recorded the petitions as questions, not approvals. The relative went home with a copy of the compensation rules and no promise of a voice.

---

Jalen Orris was learning to climb stairs by deciding which foot ought to move first.

Corven Sareth's left knee punished haste. Jalen had learned the sequence well enough to hide the worst of it before an attendant entered. He could not make the joint belong to him by understanding it.

Ysabet brought the casualty-pay extract he had asked to see. It contained his name, a blank line for payment and a notation that a family inquiry remained unresolved. The paymaster's original was still being sought.

"Does that mean my sister has had nothing?" he asked.

"It means this copy does not prove she was paid. It does not prove she was not."

"I could tell you where she lived."

"You can tell me what you remember. Someone living must check the address and the original account."

He rubbed the unfamiliar knee.

"I thought when I agreed to serve I would still be myself."

Ysabet did not correct him. She had witnessed his answer after death, in a chamber where the king had already killed the man whose body he was to occupy. She had also witnessed Corven's absence from the choice.

"You remember agreeing," she said.

"Yes."

"Corven did not agree."

"I know that now."

He looked at the hands that had once written letters to Leora. He had stopped touching her correspondence after learning who she was. He had not stopped being the man who wanted to know whether his own sister had enough to eat.

"Iven can leave his gate."

"Yes."

"If I asked to leave this—"

He did not finish. Ysabet would not ask Edselsto to invent an answer to a question no living witness had tested. She would not promise that departing a stolen body was the same as opening an oak door.

"I cannot tell you what would happen," she said.

Jalen nodded, disappointed and not surprised.

"Then please don't put me in the book with him."

She had not intended to. Until that moment she had not realized how easily the same heading could make two different violations appear to be one kind of service.

She left the casualty extract with its uncertainties intact. Rhyse arranged for the next inquiry to go through the living paymaster, not through the man wearing the envoy's face. Yara still held Corven's three separate warrants. No one in the room pretended Jalen had inherited them.

---

Luceran received the petitions at evening council, along with the corrected gate watch and the price of another day of roof work.

Damaris explained the wages. Rhyse explained the watch. Merel explained that he would not certify willingness on behalf of an unnamed dead person. Ysabet placed Iven's signed terms beside the new requests and kept Aldren's copied heading separate from them.

"One keeper has served us well," Luceran said.

"One keeper has served by choice," Merel answered.

"We need guards. We need clerks. The northern posts need grain."

"The dead cannot carry those sacks across Dols," Damaris said. "The river has not granted us another cart."

He knew that. Six sacks lay under Edselsto's roof, six beyond the river, none at the northern posts. He was tired of being told what he already knew, and more tired of how little knowing improved the situation.

The archive officer offered his drawing. A prepared door, he said, might spare the cost of a night clerk. Vess, called from the roof, studied it and asked who had measured the hinge, the stone around it and any silver way leading to it. Nobody had.

"I can inspect a door," Vess said. "That is not the same as preparing it for a dead man."

Luceran considered the hours a living watch consumed. He considered how much a court could do with a hundred willing keepers who never needed bread. The idea had the shape of thrift and devotion. It also required a hundred deaths and a hundred decisions no king could honestly make for other people.

"Let the petitions be heard individually," he said. "No binding, transfer or new prepared threshold is authorized. No living post or wage is to be removed on the expectation of dead service. Vess may report what physical inspection would cost, without altering the archive door."

Aldren began writing. Luceran saw Ysabet watching him.

"Does that satisfy you?"

"It tells us what you have not ordered," she said. "It does not tell us how many people will now ask."

He nearly replied that people were free to ask. They were. He was also the reason the court had begun to imagine the dead as a class of available labor.

Miren had attended without sitting beside him. She waited until the others had gone.

"You did not command another one," she said.

"No."

"I am glad."

It was the smallest mercy she had offered him in days. He wanted to reach for her hand. He did not.

"I thought you would say it was not enough."

"It isn't."

She left him with the signed refusal and a room full of empty chairs. His love for her had not made her forgiveness a duty. His authority over a household did not make every useful silence consent.

---

At the last watch change Pel brought the corrected roster back to the inner gate.

"Two living," he read. "One dead, voluntary. No substitute duty."

"That seems a great deal of ink to say I'm still here," Iven said.

"It's a great deal of ink to say I'm still here too."

Pel took his place where he could see the yard and the passage beyond it. He checked the next carrier's pass himself. Iven heard the latch and recognized the voice when Pel challenged the man, but offered no judgment about a face he could not see.

Mara had left the scarf at her lodging. She would return tomorrow if the road stayed passable. Tomas Marrick had promised to bring a songbook, though Iven could no longer remember whether the second verse of his favorite song had ever been worth hearing.

"I could go," Iven said after a while.

Pel kept his eyes on the gate.

"Yes."

"I might, one day."

"Then I'll have to do my own work."

"You already do."

The door gave the small creak that passed for Iven's laugh.

Below the castle, a clerk carried two unanswered petitions toward the chapel. In the works yard, Vess set aside a measuring rod for the next day's staffed inspection of older masonry; the undercroft would require ropes, wages, light and living witnesses before anyone could draw conclusions about it. Neither the petition nor the rod had yet been given the authority of an accomplished thing.

The night watch continued with living men beside the dead. In the family nave, all seven candles burned.
