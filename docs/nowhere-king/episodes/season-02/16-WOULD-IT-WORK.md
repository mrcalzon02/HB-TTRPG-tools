# Season Two, Episode 16 — Would It Work?

The miller at Dols was willing to clear his ditch. He was not willing to let the river carry away his kitchen garden to save a bridge.

The reeve brought Savelin to the mill without soldiers. The carpenter walked the length of the drainage cut and returned with mud to his knees. A straight trench would weaken the miller's wall; a bend would leave standing water beneath it. He proposed a shallower cut with a timber lip and a second inspection after the next heavy rain.

"And who pays if your timber fails?" the miller asked.

"The Crown pays for its own work," Savelin said.

"That is not what I asked."

The reeve wrote terms the miller could enforce. His mark would permit workers through his yard, not surrender his water rights or certify the bridge. The council's watch captain would still have to inspect the western approach before a loaded cart crossed. Savelin witnessed the consent and sent the new price to Edselsto. It was a real advance. The bridge remained closed, and no military countersign existed.

The ferryman offered the carpenter passage home before dark. He lost an hour of fares. Nobody entered that sacrifice in the Crown's account.

---

Corven moved his papers to a chamber nearer the outer stair. His own attendants kept their keys and could leave freely; Rhyse's living guards protected the corridor without holding him prisoner.

Yara laid three letters before him. Dols still controlled its bridge, Teth its ferries, and Merehaven the proposed first-cargo bond. The warning Corven had received about his life did not dissolve any of those separate warrants.

"Are you safe?" Yara asked.

"Safer for knowing what was proposed. That is not the same thing."

She did not know the concealed means Edselsto had suggested, and he would not ask her to treat imagination as evidence. He had written to Leora about the danger in terms he could defend. Her mother's recovery and the quarrel over the coal scuttle remained in the same letter. A wife was not merely someone to be warned after a man had finished being useful to his king.

"If the king learns more, I want Rhyse to tell me before I hear it as gossip," Corven said.

"Shall I threaten the captain?"

"No. He has already paid for being honest."

Yara sealed the bench letters in her own service. She had a brother with a flood-damaged warehouse and a possible return-stone trade to protect. Neither interest gave her leave to turn a private carriage agreement into public authority.

---

Damaris had a price for the miller's drain, six roof beams, Bram's rest wage and the smith's unpaid nails. She had no price for a miracle.

Tomas wanted to hire the next grain cart before another week was lost. Damaris showed him the coin required for the bridge carpenter and the nail apprentices.

"If we pay those men, we cannot hire the cart today," he said.

"Then the cart is not hired. The northern post cannot eat a wagon we have not paid for."

She released earned wages, acknowledged the remaining smith's bill, and refused to pledge Hesta's four-season track right as a second surety for Jorvan's smaller loan. The first twelve Tervain sacks remained dry under the completed roof bay. The rest still required carts and shelter. Eighteen patrol mounts remained in lower pasture.

Luceran listened to the accounts without asking Damaris to advise him on a proposal she had never heard.

"Every answer comes with another condition," he said.

"Yes," she replied. "You have heard several yeses. None is large enough to be the yes you want."

He signed the wages, left the loan unsigned, and took the road report away.

---

Ysabet found Luceran waiting in the east map gallery beside the marked listening arch. She had brought the sealed record of Edselsto's earlier proposal and a fresh leaf for anything said today.

"You asked me to witness a question," she said.

"I did."

"Do you know what you will do with the answer?"

"No."

"Then that is the first thing I shall write."

Luceran called Edselsto. The pale silver mask appeared, courteous as ever.

"You offered to remove Corven Sareth," Luceran said. "I refused. I have not withdrawn that refusal."

"I remember, Your Majesty."

"If a man died and a loyal shade entered his body, would it retain enough of him to carry his office? Would it work?"

Ysabet's pen stopped for the length of a breath. The question was not an order. It was not harmless either.

"I know the service of the dead," Edselsto answered. "A shade may serve within a prepared vessel. Habits of the flesh may remain. A loyal servant may do what is commanded."

"Have you performed this service where living witnesses could verify it?" Ysabet asked.

"I have knowledge of service."

"That is not a witness."

"No witness here has seen it."

"Can you demonstrate it without killing anyone or taking a body whose owner cannot consent?"

"I have no such demonstration to offer."

Luceran stared at the mask. He had feared an answer that would make the choice simple. Instead he had a claim no one could examine.

"Would he remember his wife?"

"Some habits remain."

"That is not memory."

"It may suffice for service."

"And would his councils accept his signatures?"

"His hand could make them."

Ysabet lowered her pen. "Dols, Teth and Merehaven are not his hand. Their warrants are distinct. Even a perfect signature would not make a dead envoy the living holder of their authority."

"I asked whether it would work," Luceran said.

"Then we must decide what work means. A hand that signs is not a council that consents."

He turned back to the mask. "How would a shade know what Corven knows?"

"From what is given and what remains."

"Then you cannot promise his judgment."

"I promise service, Your Majesty."

The difference was terrible in its smallness. Edselsto offered obedience, not understanding, and Luceran had come hoping the two might be interchangeable.

"You are not to prepare a vessel, seek a shade, approach Corven, alter his protection or treat this inquiry as permission. The refusal stands."

"As Your Majesty commands."

When the mask vanished, Ysabet read back the exchange. Luceran corrected her summary of his question. He wanted the exact words recorded: *Would it work?* He signed beneath them.

---

Rhyse read the new record that evening.

"The king has forbidden action again," Ysabet said.

"He forbade it before."

They brought the question to Luceran. Rhyse would not keep protecting Corven under a silence that had become another danger. He asked leave to warn him that the king had reopened the matter of the castle's ability, while withholding the unverified means.

"He may leave," Luceran said.

"He may. That is what choice costs."

Luceran gave permission. Ysabet witnessed it.

Corven received Rhyse with Yara and his own attendants in call. The captain reported the inquiry, the absence of proof and the renewed prohibition without dressing it as reassurance.

"His Majesty wants my work," Corven said. "Now he wishes to know whether I can be made to do it after I am dead."

"I cannot tell you what the castle can do," Rhyse replied.

"Neither can he, it seems."

Corven ordered his travelling chest packed for a lower-village lodging from which he could see the road. He kept his commission. Yara retained the three warrants and his instructions that no new undertaking in his name leave without his living voice and the benches' countersigns. He accepted an escort only on condition that his attendants could dismiss it at the village gate.

He did not decide whether to return home. He would wait for the next river letters, with fewer locked doors between himself and departure.

---

Miren heard the question from Luceran himself, in the physically deaf withdrawing room. She asked him to repeat what Ysabet had written before asking why he had done it.

"I wanted to know whether there was another way to save the posts."

"Dols has a miller who consented. Teth has a valuation. Merehaven has not rejected the smaller bond."

"They may be too slow."

"Then say you wanted a faster way."

He could not.

"I ordered no death," he said.

"No."

"I renewed the refusal."

"Yes."

"Then what have I done?"

"You have asked the castle to make a murder useful enough for you to consider."

The bowl beneath the leaking window had filled. Miren moved it to catch the next drops. She was not offering him an argument he could defeat by repeating the limits of his order.

"The castle proved nothing," he said.

"That should have relieved you."

"It did."

"And disappointed you?"

He thought of the eighteen horses, the northern hamlets, Corven's bad knee, the letters to Leora, the benches that could refuse him and the soldiers who could not eat patience.

"Yes."

She sat beside him, close enough that he could reach for her hand, but did not immediately give it.

"I will not let my fear for those soldiers become my agreement with this," she said. "Nor will affection make the earlier theft of my conversation right."

"I am not asking you to forgive me."

"You are asking me to remain while you learn what you might be willing to do."

They sat with that knowledge. She took his hand for a moment, then let it go. Neither the intimacy nor the refusal vanished.

At the receiving yard, Bram directed the carts from his bench while another porter worked the blind turn. The smith's apprentices had their wages; the roof remained unfinished. At Dols, the miller had consented to a drain, not a crossing. Corven could still leave by the road. The first twelve sacks remained at Edselsto and no northern convoy had departed.

In the family nave all seven candles burned. Luceran had obtained no proof and given no permission. He had nevertheless lost the belief that he could examine an atrocity without changing the man who examined it.
