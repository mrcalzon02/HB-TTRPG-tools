# Season Two, Episode 7 — The Knife That Missed

There were three ways to carry a knife through Edselsto.

A cook carried one openly and complained when the edge was poor. A guard carried one where everyone could see it, which was supposed to make people feel safe. A man who meant to kill the king carried one wrapped in a piece of old linen and looked, to the people around him, as though he was bringing a tool to work.

The third man was called Marek Venn on the day's wage roll.

Whether that was his name would become a question later.

For the moment he stood in the lower village before dawn, waiting with the stone carriers while the frost loosened from the cart wheels. He had worked the previous two days moving rubble out of a collapsed store beneath the east receiving rooms. The foreman knew he could lift his share. The pay clerk knew he had signed with a mark. Neither knew where he slept.

Pell Falbrecht came down the line with a broken hinge in his hand.

"Anyone who thinks a door is held up by the lock," he said, "can spend the morning looking for the other half of this."

Nobody volunteered.

He stopped before Marek.

"You're with the lime carts."

"I was told the lower passage."

"Who told you?"

"The man with the tally."

"Which man?"

Marek pointed toward a clerk arguing with a carter.

Pell watched the clerk long enough to decide the answer might be true.

"Lower passage after the lime is counted. No one moves a barrow through the archive approach while the king's people are there. The guards will have you carrying it back again."

Marek nodded.

A sensible instruction. A real one.

By the time the carts climbed the road, it had been repeated twice.

Marek remembered it.

He also remembered which doorway the foreman had indicated.

---

Rhyse disliked the lower archive approach for entirely human reasons.

The passage was too narrow at the western turn. Its old roof had been propped with new timber. Two repair crews needed to cross it because Vess had opened a wall farther east and discovered that the stones behind it were not the stones shown on any plan.

The king wanted to see the new threshold.

Vess wanted the king to see it only after the master mason had decided whether it was going to fall on him.

Damaris wanted to know how much the discovery would cost before anyone gave it a name.

"Then we have agreement," Luceran said at breakfast. "We shall all be disappointed together."

Damaris did not look up from the paper beside her plate.

"Some of us have practiced."

Miren smiled into her cup.

Luceran noticed the smile and nearly made a joke of it. A week ago he would have. Now he let it pass.

They had shared supper the previous evening. They had not shared a bed. She had asked him about the Red Ford survey, listened to the answer, and declined to answer his careful question about whether she wished to walk after dinner.

She was still kind to him.

That was not the same as things being repaired.

Rhyse unfolded Vess's passage sketch.

"Two guards ahead, two behind. No carts while we cross. The eastern work door stays barred until the king is past. If Vess says the roof is unsafe, we turn back."

"Yes," Luceran said.

Rhyse looked suspicious.

"All of it?"

"All of it."

"You have become agreeable."

"Do not spread the rumor."

Miren put down her cup.

"Who keeps the work door barred?"

"The Doorwardens."

"Who tells the carriers when to resume?"

"The foreman."

"Does the foreman know the king is coming?"

"He knows the passage is closed for inspection."

"Then he knows enough."

Luceran looked at her. She was not trying to win an argument with him. She was trying to keep the movement of a royal household from becoming an invitation to every man with a grievance.

Rhyse marked the route.

"Good."

There were two black ear tiles along the approach. One at the archive stair. One beside the east receiving door. The middle turn had no verified silver continuity. Vess had refused to bridge it until the roof was repaired.

Rhyse had not forgotten.

He placed a living guard there.

---

Hesta Vale had come to Edselsto to argue about a road.

She had already won four seasons of toll-free Crown-track passage for her family's quarry hauling. That did not mean the carters believed the bargain would survive the first officer who wanted coin.

She brought the signed copy, the name of the reeve who had witnessed it, and two carters who had been charged at a gate that morning.

Tomas Reed was trying to find Damaris.

Damaris was trying to find a moment in which no one wanted money.

Neither succeeded.

"The bargain says military closure," Tomas said, reading the seal. "It does not say an officer may charge a toll because the road is inconvenient."

"The man at the barrier called it a wheel fee," Hesta said.

"A wheel fee?"

"Four wheels. Four pennies."

Tomas looked at her.

"Did he at least count correctly?"

"That is what makes it so insulting."

They went to find the gate officer's written order. Hesta would have preferred the officer himself, but Tomas insisted that an unsigned recollection was not worth a quarrel.

On their way through the east receiving yard, she saw a cartload of lime standing unattended beneath the covered walk.

"That should be under canvas," she said.

Tomas glanced at it.

"It is."

"The canvas is on the other cart. This one is taking the damp."

A man with a folded cloth under his arm moved past them toward the lower passage.

Hesta stepped aside. She noticed his boots because they were not the boots of a man who had spent the morning in lime. The soles were clean at the instep. That might mean nothing. A worker could have changed them. A worker could have arrived late.

She forgot him when the carter behind her began arguing about the four pennies again.

Later she would be ashamed of forgetting.

Later she would learn that forgetting a stranger was not a crime.

---

The passage inspection began late.

Pell had been delayed by a cracked support foot. Vess refused to let anyone near the eastern wall until it had been wedged and braced.

Luceran waited in the lower archive with Ysabet, Rhyse, two Doorwardens and a clerk holding a board of survey notes.

Edselsto's mask stood at the far end of the connected stair.

It had appeared without being called.

Luceran had grown used to that.

"Is the passage safe?" he asked it.

"The stone has stood since the southern roof was raised."

Vess, arriving in time to hear, snorted.

"So have several buildings that fell yesterday."

"I remember no collapse there," Edselsto said.

"That is not the same thing as there being no collapse tomorrow."

"No."

Luceran smiled despite himself.

Vess set down his hammer.

"That is the most useful answer it has given all week."

Rhyse ordered the eastern work door shut. The guard at the middle turn acknowledged the order. Another guard checked the west stair. Pell went ahead to inspect the brace.

The workmen withdrew, or appeared to.

Marek Venn remained behind the lime cart near the east receiving door. A young carrier told him the passage was closed.

"I left the measuring cord inside."

"Then leave it."

"The foreman will take it from my pay."

"Ask him."

Marek looked toward Pell, who was already out of sight.

The carrier shrugged and went to find a dry place for the lime.

Marek waited until the guard at the work door turned to answer a question from the east yard. He passed behind the stacked planks and reached the short service recess beside the door.

There was no black ear there.

He had watched the masons mark the connected rooms with black tiles. Everyone had watched. The marks were meant to tell the household where it could be heard.

Marek did not know whether the unmarked recess was truly deaf.

He was willing to gamble that it was.

The guard turned back.

Marek was gone from the yard.

---

The first warning came from a living man.

"Hold."

The Doorwarden at the middle turn had seen movement where no movement belonged. A sleeve beyond the braced timber. A shadow slipping across the work door.

Rhyse heard the call.

He put his hand against Luceran's chest and stopped him.

"What is it?" Luceran asked.

"Wait."

Pell, ten paces ahead, looked back from the threshold.

A man stepped out from behind the timber.

He carried a folded cloth.

The guard said, "You were told to stay outside."

"Measuring cord," the man said.

"Put it down."

Marek took one step forward.

The guard moved to intercept him.

There was a small, dry sound as metal slid against metal beneath the cloth.

Edselsto spoke from the archive stair.

"Your Majesty. Left."

Luceran turned his head.

That movement saved his throat.

The knife came in low and rose toward the place where his neck had been. It caught the edge of his coat, tore through the collar, and struck the oak upright behind him hard enough to lodge for a heartbeat.

Rhyse hit Marek before he could draw it free.

The first Doorwarden seized the man's wrist. The second dragged Luceran backward into the archive. Pell dropped the hammer he had been carrying and caught the falling brace before it struck anyone.

Marek twisted, kicked, and nearly broke loose.

The guard at the middle turn took the second blade across the back of his hand.

A second blade.

That would matter later.

Rhyse drove his shoulder into the man's ribs and pinned him against the timber. The Doorwarden struck Marek's wrist with the flat of a sword. The knife fell. Pell kicked it under a cart plank.

"Alive," Rhyse said.

He said it twice.

A frightened guard had drawn steel.

"Alive. We need his mouth."

Marek stopped fighting only when three men had him on the ground.

Luceran stood against the archive wall with a torn collar and a shallow cut above his shoulder.

The world had become strangely narrow.

The blade in the oak.

The blood on the guard's hand.

Rhyse breathing hard.

The pale mask standing still beside the stair.

"Did you see him?" Luceran asked.

Rhyse turned.

"Who?"

"Edselsto."

The mask answered.

"I heard the blade leave its wrapping. I heard the guard command him to stop."

"Before he struck?"

"Yes."

"Why did you say left?"

"That was where the sound came from."

Luceran stared at it.

No vision through every wall.

No reading of murderous thought.

A sound, a direction, a warning.

It had been enough.

Rhyse took Luceran by the shoulders and looked at his neck.

"Physician," he ordered.

"It is nothing."

"Physician."

Luceran did not argue.

That frightened him more than the blood.

---

Calienne's assistant stitched the guard's hand while Calienne examined the king.

The cut was shallow. The collar had taken most of it. The guard would likely keep the use of his fingers, provided the wound stayed clean and he did not insist on returning to duty before the swelling passed.

Rhyse remained in the room until Calienne made him leave.

"Captain," she said, "I have a patient."

"You have two."

"And you are making both worse."

Luceran sat on the edge of the examination table.

"I have had worse scratches from rose bushes."

"Then perhaps stop trying to inspect them with your throat."

Calienne did not sound impressed.

Miren arrived while Calienne was cleaning the wound.

She stopped in the doorway.

For one moment her face held nothing he could interpret.

Then she crossed the room and looked at the blood on his shirt.

"How deep?"

"Not deep."

"That was not a measurement."

Calienne answered for him.

"Skin. No vessel. He will live long enough to argue about it."

Miren nodded.

Luceran reached for her hand.

She gave it to him.

He felt relief so sudden that it was almost shameful.

"Edselsto warned me," he said.

"I heard."

"It heard the knife."

"I heard that too."

He wanted to say that the castle had saved him. That a week of quarrels about listening had been answered by one terrible second. That perhaps some rooms had to be heard, and perhaps there was no clean boundary between safety and privacy.

Miren saw the thought arrive.

"Do not," she said.

He stopped.

She did not release his hand.

"I am grateful you are alive. I am grateful the guard moved. I am grateful the wall heard what it heard. None of those things makes what you did to me lawful."

"I know."

"Do you?"

He looked at her.

"Yes."

She considered him.

Then she leaned forward and pressed her forehead to his.

It was a private act in a room Edselsto could not hear.

Calienne looked away.

No one mistook the gesture for an answer about marriage.

---

Rhyse interviewed the prisoner before supper.

Ysabet attended to record the questions. A Doorwarden with a bandaged hand was not permitted to attend because he had seen the attack and would be needed as a witness, not because Rhyse doubted him.

Marek sat on a bench in a locked workroom with his wrists bound before him.

"Name," Rhyse said.

"Marek Venn."

"Where from?"

"East road."

"Which village?"

"Not yours."

Rhyse waited.

Ysabet dipped her pen.

"Who hired you?"

"The foreman."

"Pell hired you for stone carrying. I asked who hired you to carry a knife toward the king."

Marek looked at the floor.

"Nobody."

"Then why?"

No answer.

Rhyse put the recovered blades on the table, separated by a folded cloth.

One was a work knife sharpened far beyond the needs of rope and leather. The other was narrow, made for slipping between ribs. Neither carried a maker's mark that identified a patron.

"Did you bring both?"

"Yes."

"Why?"

Marek said nothing.

Rhyse had no interest in frightening him into a story.

He had seen too many stories extracted from frightened men.

He asked where Marek slept. The prisoner gave the name of a lodging house below the village. He asked who had witnessed the hiring. Marek named a pay clerk. He asked why he had chosen the lower passage.

"Work."

"Why did you hide when the passage closed?"

Silence.

Ysabet kept writing.

After a long time Marek said, "They told me he would be alone."

Rhyse did not move.

"Who?"

Marek stared at the knives.

"They said he would be alone."

"Did you see them?"

Marek swallowed.

"One."

"Where?"

No answer.

"Was the promise money?"

Marek shook his head.

"Then what?"

Marek closed his eyes.

"My brother."

Rhyse let the silence stand.

He did not offer a pardon. He did not promise to find a brother whose existence had not been verified. He did not tell the prisoner that cooperation would save him from a punishment the captain had no authority to remit.

"Your brother's name?"

Marek whispered something.

Ysabet asked him to repeat it.

He refused.

They ended the questioning.

Outside the room Ysabet said, "He may have been coached."

"Yes."

"He may be lying."

"Yes."

"He may have a brother."

"Yes."

Rhyse rubbed his face.

"Three answers. None of them an arrest warrant for anyone else."

She closed the book.

"The king will want the fourth."

"Then the king can wait for evidence."

---

The evidence was untidy.

Pell Falbrecht brought the hiring sheet and spent ten minutes explaining that a man could be competent at moving stone and dangerous in other occupations.

"I checked that he could work," Pell said. "Not whether he wished the king dead."

Rhyse did not blame him for that.

He blamed the access crossing.

The passage had been closed, but the lime cart had been allowed to stand beside a service recess. A work door had been watched, but the man had entered through a narrow space the guards had not recognized as a route.

It was an ordinary security failure.

That made it repairable.

The pay clerk remembered Marek arriving with two other carriers. One of those men had left the works at noon the previous day. The other was still on the timber crews and gave an account that matched the clerk's in most particulars.

No one knew Marek well.

The lodging-house keeper knew his boots, his appetite, and that he paid in small coin. She remembered no visitor. She did remember that he had asked whether the king walked the lower archive approach every morning.

That was evidence of interest.

Not of who supplied it.

Hesta Vale returned with the road order she had come to dispute and found the east receiving yard full of guards.

When Tomas told her what had happened, she remembered the clean insteps and the folded cloth.

She went to Rhyse herself.

"I saw him."

"With a knife?"

"No."

"With a man?"

"No."

"Did he threaten anyone?"

"No."

"Then tell me exactly what you saw."

She did.

Rhyse wrote it down.

She looked unhappy.

"I ought to have said something."

"About clean boots?"

"He looked wrong."

"People look wrong to us after we know what they did."

Hesta studied him.

"You are trying to make me feel better."

"No. I am trying to keep you from becoming a witness to a thing you did not witness."

She sat back.

"Then what good am I?"

"You can tell us where he was, what he carried, and which way he walked. That is good enough."

She thought about that.

Then she remembered the uncovered lime.

"The cart was left in the damp. The other one had canvas."

Rhyse asked for the cart to be held.

Under its axle the guards found a narrow scrape in the wet earth and a strip of linen snagged on a splinter.

It matched the wrapping Marek had carried in appearance.

Whether it came from the same cloth required a comparison by living hands.

Rhyse ordered one.

He did not ask Edselsto to remember the yard.

The yard was not connected.

---

The council gathered in the sealed chamber after dark.

Luceran's collar had been repaired badly enough that everyone could see where it had torn.

He left it that way.

Damaris placed a sheet of costs before him.

"One guard injured. Two days of work stopped. A support foot broken in the struggle. Three replacement watches, extra food for the men held at the gates, and the physician's supplies."

Luceran looked up.

"Is this the moment you ask me to economize on being assassinated?"

"No. It is the moment I tell you the price of not being assassinated is still entered in the accounts."

Rhyse stood beside the passage sketch.

"The attacker entered through a service recess. The guards had closed the main work door. The king was not alone. We stopped the man. We failed to keep him from striking."

"You stopped him," Luceran said.

"After he struck."

"Edselsto warned me."

"Yes."

Rhyse said the word with effort.

"Without the warning, you might have taken the blade in the throat."

Silence settled.

Luceran looked at the captain.

"You sound angry."

"I am grateful."

"Those are not opposites."

"No."

Rhyse pointed at the black ear marked on the plan.

"The warning came after the man entered the connected approach. It could not tell us he was waiting in the recess. It did not know his intention. It heard a knife drawn. We had already challenged him. A guard was already moving."

"That does not make the warning worthless."

"I did not say it did."

"Then what are you saying?"

"That I cannot post a mask at every door and send my living men home."

Ysabet spoke from the far side of the table.

"And that the ears advertise their own limits."

Vess had been waiting to say precisely that.

"The black tiles show where we have restored the branches. A stranger with eyes can count them. I could conceal them, but then our own people would not know where they might be overheard."

"Could the channels be hidden?" Luceran asked.

"They are already inside walls."

"The marks."

"Remove the marks and we remove public notice."

Miren looked at him.

"Which is a different kind of danger."

Damaris said, "There is also the question of what a warning means. A knife being drawn in a workshop is not an assassination."

Rhyse nodded.

"If we tell Edselsto to report every blade, hammer, raised voice or running foot, the castle will be shouting all day."

"And if we tell it to decide which ones are threats?" Ysabet asked.

Nobody liked that.

Luceran looked at the plan.

A room that heard.

A corridor that did not.

A guard who saw.

A wall that spoke.

He remembered the knife, and the one moment in which he had moved because the mask said left.

He also remembered Miren's stolen words.

"Tomorrow," he said, "we decide what an alarm may be, who receives it, and who may act upon it. We decide what happens when it is wrong. We decide who can challenge it."

Rhyse looked at him.

"And until then?"

"Living watches. Closed work passages. No new listening branches without the existing survey."

Vess nodded.

"Good."

"Do not sound surprised."

"I am a mason, Your Majesty. I trust things after they bear weight."

Luceran looked at the empty chair where a wounded guard ought to have been sitting at supper.

"Make certain his family receives his pay while he heals."

Damaris made a note.

"Already ordered."

That was government too.

---

After the council, the king found Rhyse in the east receiving passage.

The black ear tile was still beside the door.

A guard stood beneath it, alert and visibly exhausted.

Rhyse had changed the watch twice since the attack. He was about to change it again.

Luceran stood with him.

"You saved my life."

Rhyse shook his head.

"We all did what we could."

"You heard it say left."

"Yes."

"Would you have moved me without it?"

"I was already trying."

"Would you have been fast enough?"

"I don't know."

Luceran disliked the answer.

He believed it.

Rhyse studied the damaged collar.

"I do know that a man got inside my cordon."

"And you will make certain it does not happen again."

"I will try. I will change the route, close the recess, check the work lists, move the king's inspections away from active carts, and give my guards a way to stop you without being dismissed for insolence."

Luceran looked at him.

"That last one sounds aimed at me."

"It is."

They stood together for a while.

Then Rhyse said, "I am glad the castle spoke."

Luceran waited.

"I am frightened by how glad."

That was the thing Luceran had not wanted to hear.

Not that Edselsto was dangerous.

That its usefulness could make frightened, competent men want it.

"Do you trust it?" Luceran asked.

"To hear a knife?"

"To protect me."

Rhyse looked at the tile.

"I trust the sound it heard. I trust what I saw. I trust you to tell me when it says something I cannot verify."

"And if I do not?"

"Then we have a worse problem than a knife."

Luceran said nothing.

Rhyse bowed.

Not deeply.

Enough.

Then he went to change the watch.

---

Miren was waiting outside the south-gallery withdrawing room.

Ardel stood with her, holding a folded road map.

They had been speaking behind the deaf door. Luceran knew that only because he had seen them emerge.

Ardel noticed the torn collar.

Her face softened for a moment before she controlled it.

"I hear you are difficult to kill."

"I am trying to make a habit of it."

"Do not."

It was a small word.

Not a reconciliation.

She handed Miren the map.

"Kaelmont will answer the road question by the end of the week. The winter question is mine."

Luceran nodded.

"I understand."

"Good."

Ardel walked away.

Miren watched her go.

"Did you ask Edselsto who sent the man?"

"No."

"Why?"

"It did not hear him arrange the attempt. It heard the knife."

She looked at him.

"That sounds like Rhyse."

"He has become difficult to avoid."

"He was always difficult to avoid."

Luceran smiled.

Miren touched the repaired seam of his collar.

"You frightened me."

"I frightened myself."

"I know."

He put his hand over hers.

"I wanted to tell you the warning proved we needed the ears."

"It proved one ear was useful."

"Yes."

"And?"

"And I wanted it to prove more."

She nodded.

"That is the difference."

They remained in the gallery until the evening watch passed.

He did not ask about marriage.

She did not offer an answer.

Their hands stayed together.

Beyond the next turn, Edselsto's mask appeared in the connected corridor. It bowed when Luceran looked toward it.

"Your Majesty."

"Thank you," he said.

The mask inclined its head.

"Your safety is my service."

Luceran felt a fierce, foolish comfort.

Then he heard Rhyse's footsteps coming from the stair, and the comfort changed shape.

There was a servant who could not refuse him.

There were living people who could.

One had warned him.

One had thrown himself at a knife.

One had told him no.

All three had kept him alive in different ways.

At the family nave, Canon Merel's evening keeper tended seven steady flames.

No candle had guttered.

Nothing in the mountain announced a victory.

By morning the guards would have new orders, the masons would close the recess, the pay clerk would be questioned again, and a man with two knives would still be waiting to say who had sent him.

The king would have to decide how much more he wanted the walls to know.

That was the trouble with surviving.

It left decisions.
