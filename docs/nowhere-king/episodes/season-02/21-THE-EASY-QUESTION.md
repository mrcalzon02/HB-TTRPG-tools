# Season Two, Episode 21 — The Easy Question

The first question was about a hook.

Tomas had spent half the morning looking for the iron hook that lifted the receiving-yard pulley clear of its beam. The spare belonged to the Crown, which meant that three different men were certain someone else had signed for it. Without the hook, Pell could not raise a length of roof timber, and without the timber the second bay could not take the remaining grain sacks out of the weather.

In the connected receiving passage, a mason's apprentice said, "I wonder where Henn put the spare."

The pale silver on the arch caught the daylight. Edselsto answered from the stone, as though a person had asked it directly.

"Behind the lime chest. He said he would fetch it after the midday bell."

The apprentice stared. Tomas sent him to look.

The hook was there, wrapped in old sacking. It was cold and perfectly serviceable. Henn, summoned from the scaffold, remembered leaving it there before the rain. Pell lost only the time needed to check the iron for cracks. The roof timber rose before the bell.

"Who gave the answer?" Tomas asked.

The apprentice pointed at the arch.

Tomas wrote **hook found** on the receiving slip. He hesitated, then wrote **castle said behind lime chest** in the margin. That was not a witness's name and would not tell the next clerk who had moved the hook. Still, the beam was going up, and the grain might remain dry.

Henn came down to claim his hook. He had not been asked before the walls were.

"Did you ask me?" he said.

"I sent for you."

"After."

Tomas had a houseful of accounts, two roof bays and an unpaid smith's bill. He could hardly apologize for saving half a morning. Yet Henn had found another thing wrong with the transaction: there was no entry showing that an answer from the arch had become an instruction to enter his work chest.

He told Tomas so.

Tomas made another mark on the slip, this one beside the first. Neither knew what such marks were supposed to mean.

---

At the lower kitchens the question was about a pot.

The kitchen mistress had ordered broth carried to the yard men who were bracing the roof, but the pot had not arrived at the west steps. A boy said he had seen it go toward the buttery. A maid said the boy had been asleep beside the bakehouse door. Both were indignant.

The house's voice came through the connected hearth passage. "I heard three people pass the west stair with the pot. One complained that the handle burned."

The mistress sent a runner. The pot was at the west stair. One handle had indeed torn its cloth wrapping, and the carriers had stopped to bind it with cord.

The boy was vindicated. The maid said a sensible cook would have found the pot anyway. The broth was still warm, which mattered to the men outside more than either argument.

By the second meal, servants had begun addressing the stone without asking the kitchen mistress whether they might. Where was the flour tally? Who had called for dry straw? Had the water carrier passed the second landing? Edselsto answered some questions, declined others, and sometimes replied with a phrase heard earlier in another voice.

A scullery maid named Etta kept a tally of those replies on the back of a torn provision wrapper. Five questions, three useful answers, one answer that had described yesterday's errand, and one that had not been an answer at all.

When the mistress found her writing, she asked whether Etta was keeping an account for Ysabet.

"No, mistress."

"Then why?"

"Because if we keep asking the wall, someone will say it told us to do things. I want to remember which things it actually said."

The mistress considered the wrapper, then had Etta copy it onto clean paper. She did not forbid the questions. She had broth to send, fuel to count and a day's wages at risk if the kitchen did not feed the roof men. The wall was quick.

Etta kept the original anyway.

---

At the guardroom Rhyse found a younger watch officer already writing a castle answer onto the change-of-watch sheet.

The officer straightened. "Captain."

"What is that?"

"Question concerning the west service door, sir. It was opened after second bell. I asked who passed."

Rhyse read the line. **Edselsto reports a man wearing mail.**

"Did you see him?"

"No, sir. The castle heard mail against the latch."

"Then strike out the man. It heard a sound."

The officer protested that he had asked twice, and the castle had given the same answer.

"A second telling is not a second witness." Rhyse laid down the sheet. "Did anyone inspect the latch? Did anyone question the men on the western watch?"

"Not yet."

"Do so. The arch is connected. The stable passage is not. You have a sound from the one and no account from the other."

It proved to be two haulers moving a repaired mail shirt in an open basket. They had used the service door with the quartermaster's permission because the yard was crowded. Neither carried a blade. Their written work order was found beneath a damp bench.

Rhyse made the officer keep the original mistake visible and initial the correction, instead of tearing out the sheet. He sent a copy to Ysabet under the sixth-clause review, marking the answer useful for locating a sound but mistaken as to the person responsible.

The younger officer looked humiliated.

"You stopped the watch from arresting a hauler," Rhyse said. "That is worth more than a tidy sheet."

"Yes, sir."

"Next time, ask the hauler first if you can find him."

Rhyse could not entirely resent the question. He had used Edselsto's warning to survive a knife. He remembered the warmth of relief before he remembered the rules. What unsettled him was that the young officer had not been afraid of asking at all. He had been afraid only of getting the answer written incorrectly.

---

Damaris heard of the hook, the pot and the guardroom correction before she heard the first complete account of the roof bill.

The apprentice smith had delivered six more pins, according to Tomas's receipt. Hesta Vale's carter claimed only four had been unloaded from his cart. The disagreement was small enough that a careless officer might have deducted the difference from the carter's pay. It was large enough to delay a beam if the pins were missing.

Tomas stood at the desk with both receipts.

"Edselsto says six passed the receiving arch," he said.

"Did it see them?" Damaris asked.

"It heard the smith count six. The boy says he put them on the cart."

"That is two statements about a count, not six pins in a rack."

They walked to the receiving yard. Pell had already fitted four pins into temporary braces. Two lay in a covered crate beneath the carpenters' bench, still bearing the smith's chalk mark. All six existed. The carter had unloaded four because Pell had taken the other two directly from the cart.

Hesta arrived in time to hear the resolution.

"You could have counted them without the castle," she said.

"We did," Damaris replied.

"After it told you the number."

Damaris disliked how accurately Hesta had described the order of events. There had been no fraud, no missing goods, and no magical revelation. A useful answer had simply become the first document everyone wanted to reconcile.

Hesta demanded that the carter's name be cleared on the bill and that the smith's apprentice be paid for the two pins as well as the four. Tomas corrected both receipts in the presence of Pell and the carter. No one could charge the loss to an absent worker.

"Are we making new rules?" Tomas asked.

"We are remembering the old ones," Damaris said. "Put the castle's answer in a separate margin. Do not enter it as a delivery or a debt until a living person has counted the goods."

"That will slow the accounts."

"It will slow the mistakes."

Tomas returned to his desk with two more lines to copy. Damaris stayed in the yard, looking at the second roof bay. The last sacks were still uncovered. The wall had saved time, and the time was real. The temptation was real too.

---

Ysabet asked Etta to read her kitchen tally aloud.

The girl stood in the copy room holding the clean version with both hands. She had expected a reprimand for writing on a provision wrapper. Instead the keeper of the record wanted the wrapper as well as the fair copy.

Ysabet compared the questions with the connected-space survey. Two had been asked in marked ear-tile rooms. One had been asked through a kitchen arch whose silver bridge remained intact. Another had come from a passage where the workmen had removed the bridge the previous week.

"How did you hear the last answer?" she asked.

"I didn't, mistress. The runner said someone had heard it."

"Who?"

Etta gave the runner's name. That name led to another servant, who admitted he had repeated an answer given the day before.

The ledger would have contained a false second answer if Etta had written what she had merely been told.

"Why did you keep the first wrapper?"

"I knew which lines I had written while I was there."

Ysabet did not smile. She laid the wrapper beside the six-clause listening order.

The order still stood. Questions needed proper authorization and a named living reviewer when their answers would affect people. Urgent warnings belonged to the watch, not to a wall's private judgment. Certain rooms were physically deaf. A question about work or goods did not authorize retrieval of private conversations. Every action required its own living evidence and correction route.

No one had repealed those provisions. The staff were simply learning to ask before deciding whether the provisions applied.

Ysabet brought Tomas's marginal notation, Rhyse's corrected watch sheet, and Etta's wrapper to the king's council. She did not combine them into a single declaration that Edselsto had lied. It had found a hook and a pot, reported the sound of mail, and repeated a count. Each answer was narrower than the decision someone wished to make from it.

Luceran read the three papers.

"Has anyone been hurt?"

"Not by the questions, so far."

He leaned back. "Then you have brought me a success."

"Several small successes," Ysabet answered. "And the beginnings of a habit."

"Would you rather they waste half a day searching for an iron hook?"

"I would rather the man whose chest is searched be named before the search is ordered."

Damaris laid the roof receipt beside the listening order.

"I can show you the time saved," she said. "I can also show you how easily a report became a charge against a carter who had done what Pell asked."

Luceran had wanted to hear that his servants were fed and his roof secure. The papers offered him those facts. They also required him to hear that a quick answer could settle an argument long before anyone understood what had actually been asked.

He ordered the three original records preserved, with the officers who made each decision named. He did not suspend ordinary use of the marked passages. Neither did he give the wall authority to sign for property or accuse a person.

Ysabet wrote the order before they rose.

Miren passed the kitchen on her way to the south gallery and heard a young page ask whether the queen's supper had gone up.

The wall answered that a tray had passed the landing.

There was no queen at Edselsto. There was a king's companion who had asked not to have her movements reported through his servants.

Miren stopped the page, who looked immediately miserable.

"Who told you to ask?"

"No one, my lady. The cook wanted to know whether to send another tray."

"Then ask the chambermaid. She carried it."

"I didn't mean—"

"I know."

She did not ask Edselsto what it had heard. She went to the kitchen mistress and requested that ordinary questions about her chamber be put to the people who served it, not to the connected stair. The mistress agreed, though she admitted the stair would answer more quickly when the maid was busy.

Miren went upstairs. The physically deaf room remained physically deaf; its silver bridge lay locked away under the keeper's seal. She had not won privacy by persuading the castle to behave. Someone had removed metal and other people still honored the gap.

At the landing she encountered Luceran.

"Ysabet says there were questions," he said.

"There always will be."

"She says they have been useful."

"They have. That does not tell you which ones to ask."

He watched the servants pass below.

"I have been grateful for every answer it gives. I have not asked anyone to hide these records."

"No," Miren said. "Today you let them be made. That matters."

He looked as though he might take the acknowledgement for forgiveness. She did not offer it.

"The maid carried my supper," she said. "If you want to know whether I ate it, you may ask me. You will have to accept that I might not answer."

She left him on the landing.

---

Before the last bell Tomas entered a new column into the receiving-yard daybook: **Question put to the house; living confirmation; action taken; person affected.**

It was not one of Ysabet's six clauses. It was a place to keep the clauses from being forgotten amid the day's bills. He showed it to Henn, whose chest had been searched, and to Pell, whose roof needed the recovered hook. Both corrected something in the entries. Henn wanted the chest named. Pell insisted the hook had been inspected before use, because a story about finding it was not a report on the metal.

The entry became slower and more accurate.

The next morning's roof work would still begin on time.

At the eastern receiving desk a junior clerk came to Luceran with a seemingly modest request. A cartman's hire fee had been entered under two hands, and the clerk wanted to know which of them had spoken the agreed amount aloud in the connected passage. Neither man was present. One had ridden toward Dols; the other was fetching witnesses.

"If I ask Edselsto just for the words," the clerk said, "we could pay the man before the road closes."

Luceran saw the account, the waiting wage and the harmlessness of a simple question. He also saw the empty witness line.

"Bring Ysabet," he said. "And the signed hire slip."

The clerk withdrew, relieved rather than rebuked. He had been given a route to the answer, and it began with the castle.

Luceran remained beside the silver arch. Once he would have asked a steward where the carter could be found. Now the first thought had been whether Edselsto remembered the voice.

The pale mask appeared faintly in the stone.

"My king," it said.

He did not put the question.

Not yet.

In the family nave, all seven candles remained alight.
