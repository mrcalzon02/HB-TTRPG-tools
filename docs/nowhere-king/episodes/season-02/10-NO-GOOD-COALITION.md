# Season Two, Episode 10 — No Good Coalition

Damaris had been offered eight hundred sacks of grain by a lord who did not own eight hundred sacks without consequence.

She laid the offer beside the Crown's accounts and waited for Tomas to finish counting the cost of the roof above the receiving yard.

"House Tervain has the grain," he said.

"Some of it is the lord's to sell. Some is his tenants' winter reserve. He can make the second kind appear to be the first by calling it rent."

"Would he?"

"His letter is careful not to say."

Tomas examined the other proposals. House Jorvan would advance coin against two seasons of customs. Cendreval offered a smaller loan on condition that it receive first claim on restoration-silver contracts. Ardel of Kaelmont could arrange guides and teams along the high road, but a bridge needed new decking and the pass would close in heavy snow. The League's barges, if the three river benches consented, remained the quickest route to the northern garrisons.

"Then we have four choices," Tomas said.

"We have more than four. We can use pieces of each. We can also make four different promises that will be collected by four different people."

She turned the paper over and wrote three headings: money, time, consent.

Under money she entered the roof, wages, storage, transport, grain and interest. Under time she entered the days until the first hard frost, the delay at Northbridge and the uncompleted Red Ford survey. Under consent she entered Tervain's tenants, Kaelmont's toll holders, Jorvan's creditors and the three League benches.

"The king will ask which is best," Tomas said.

"Then we shall tell him which is possible, and what it takes away from something else."

A messenger arrived from Captain Savelin, whom Rhyse had sent to inspect the Dols crossing. Rain had delayed the party at a ford. They expected to reach the bridge by evening.

Tomas sighed. "Even the inspection is late."

"The inspection is teaching us something," Damaris replied. "A road that exists on a map does not necessarily exist for a loaded wagon on the day it is needed."

---

Hesta Vale found Yara Fen beside the quarry carts, measuring a passing place with a knotted cord.

"My brother has brought the sizes of the bridge stones," Hesta said. "He has measured the large blocks, which are too heavy for your return barges. I have sent him to measure the smaller ones."

Yara smiled. "A large river contains surprisingly small boats."

"And a royal road contains surprisingly many people who believe they alone may use it."

They spread their drawings on the back of a wagon. Grain could travel uphill in the morning and dressed stone downhill after the second bell. The arrangement required a porter empowered to stop both streams when the passing place filled. It also required a wider turn near the east yard. Neither woman could command the Crown's traffic or the League's barges. They could at least put a workable proposal before the people who could.

"Will your envoy accept it?" Hesta asked.

"He can recommend it. He cannot surrender the rights of three river councils."

"I begin to like him."

"Many people do, until they want him to sign something."

Hesta pointed to the Crown-track bargain she had won over the old eastern-road compensation claim. Her quarry teams held four seasons of toll-free passage, subject to military closure. She would not let a new foreign grain agreement erase it.

Yara copied the clause. "Then the carriage agreement must name your existing right."

"And pay for the extra porter."

"And say who pays if a cart breaks the passing place."

They argued about that for a quarter hour. Yara wanted liability to follow the cart. Hesta wanted the road keeper responsible for its condition. They settled on a joint inspection before each new convoy and separate liability for damage caused by a negligent driver.

"No signatures yet," Yara said.

"No signatures," Hesta agreed. "But now we know what we are arguing over."

After Yara left, Hesta paid the porter for his measurements from her own purse. Even if the royal treaty failed, she intended to find a river market for the quarry's smaller dressed stone. Her workers could not live forever on repairs to a single castle.

---

Luceran's council received the alternatives without ceremony.

Damaris described Tervain's grain first. The Crown could purchase a limited portion without exhausting the estate's winter reserve. More would require a levy whose burden would fall on tenants who had no voice in the chamber.

"Buy the portion he can sell," Luceran said.

"It will feed two northern posts for a short time," Rhyse replied. "Not the line."

"Two posts are better than none."

"They are," Damaris agreed. "We must not confuse them with all."

Ardel's Kaelmont letter came next. It offered guides, draft teams and a route through the mountain road, with a price for the unfinished bridge deck and no promise that the pass would remain open after snow. Luceran read the careful little mark beside the steepest turn and recognized Ardel's hand. She had answered the road question he had asked. She had not answered any question about her winter plans.

Miren watched him put the letter down. She did not fill the silence with what Ardel had privately told her.

"Can we make the road passable?" Luceran asked.

"Yes," Rhyse said. "For some carts, in some weather, after work. It is a good secondary route. It would be a poor sole hope."

Jorvan's credit could purchase grain and hire teams quickly, but two seasons of customs would be paid to the lender before the Crown's discretionary works. Cendreval's smaller advance would preserve more customs at the price of a preferred position in the silver restoration trade.

Vess objected to exclusivity. "If one house controls the price of silver at the moment we most need it, we have exchanged a winter problem for a permanent one."

"And Jorvan's claim would leave the spring works waiting behind debt service," Damaris said.

"Could we accept both?" Luceran asked.

"Yes," she replied. "We could accept both, buy the Tervain grain, and send carts over Kaelmont. We would move supplies. We would also pledge future revenue, privilege a metal supplier, weaken an estate's reserves, and depend upon a road the first heavy snow could close. It is possible. It is expensive."

Rhyse leaned over the map. "A soldier does not eat an objection to interest."

"Nor does a farmer eat the knowledge that a soldier was fed," Damaris answered.

The king let them disagree. Rhyse was not cruel for wanting food at the posts. Damaris was not timid for counting the villages. Their arguments were both part of his duty.

Ysabet reminded the council that the first League convoy had not yet been approved by the benches. A roof could be built in anticipation; a bond could be priced; neither action would create foreign consent. The six-clause listening order remained in force. No one had asked Edselsto for a private report of the envoy's deliberations, and Ysabet would not turn the absence of a request into proof that temptation had ended.

Luceran ordered the lawful Tervain purchase negotiated, the Kaelmont deck surveyed, and Jorvan asked for a narrower claim. He rejected Cendreval's exclusive silver condition but invited an ordinary competitive offer. He authorized Damaris to price the temporary roof separately from the proposed convoy.

"Have I done enough?" he asked after the others rose.

Tomas, collecting the maps, stopped.

"You have given us work that can be done, Your Majesty."

"That was not the question."

"No," Tomas said. "But it is the answer I can prove."

---

Captain Savelin came back from Dols with mud to his knees and no military crossing.

The bridge was sound for modest civilian cargo. Its approach could not hold a marching column and a loaded grain train at once. The bridge reeve had shown him the toll house, the cargo tally, the watch bell and the chest in which the council kept its emergency warrants.

"The watch captain said he would close the gate if our troops came without the countersign," Savelin reported to Rhyse. "He did not boast. He looked as though he hoped never to have to do it."

"Could we force it?"

"With enough men. We would damage the bridge we need, lose the barges we need, and make enemies of the people willing to sell us food. We might cross. We would not be supplied."

Rhyse nodded. It was a military answer, not a diplomatic one, and for that reason he trusted it.

Savelin had brought the ordinary tolls, a sketch of the approaches and an offer from the reeve to survey the western road jointly. He had not brought a promise about soldiers. The council's authority was real, and its captain had not pretended otherwise.

"The reeve says they have fed foreign soldiers before," Savelin added. "He remembers which captains paid for boats and which did not."

"Then the hearing clause has a history."

"Yes. He spoke of a boat taken during a flood. The owner was promised payment and spent three years asking which officer had authority to approve it."

Rhyse ordered the cargo information copied for Damaris and the troop observations held separately. Corven's demand for a remedy after emergency seizure no longer sounded like an invented insult. It was an answer to an old injury. Rhyse still disliked foreign review of royal captains. He could no longer dismiss the reason for it.

Savelin's cuff was torn. A ferryman had caught him with a hook when he slipped boarding a skiff. Rhyse told him to enter the man's name in his report.

"Why?"

"Because another captain may need him."

---

Corven Sareth read the Dols account beside a letter from Leora.

His wife wrote that her mother had slept well for two nights but still insisted on carrying coal upstairs. Their youngest niece had hidden under the table during the argument and would not come out until bribed with honey bread.

"Domestic rebellion," Corven told Yara. "Better organized than most leagues."

"Then perhaps you should return before they replace you."

"I should like to return with my commission intact."

He read Yara's revised carriage proposal. It allowed grain uphill and quarry stone downhill without displacing Hesta Vale's four-season right. The added porter and passing place cost money. That made the plan less beautiful on paper and more likely to work.

"Will Merehaven accept the expense?" Yara asked.

"Perhaps. It earns money from cargo that actually arrives."

"And Dols?"

"Dols may approve civilian hours. It will not surrender the military countersign."

"You could recommend that it do so."

"I have recommended that it receive the king's captain and hear his case. My commission does not turn three councils into one."

Yara studied him. "The king thinks you are withholding a price."

"Then he is accustomed to men who say no in order to be paid for saying yes."

"Are you not one of them?"

"Sometimes," Corven admitted. "Not in this."

He dictated a letter to Dols praising Savelin's restraint and professional inspection. The king had sent an officer rather than an armed demand. That deserved consideration even if the council later refused.

When Yara left, Corven returned to Leora's letter. He wrote about the broken road, the castle's astonishing repair works and the excellent onion pie served at supper. He did not describe the terms of the negotiations in the family letter. He did tell her that the young king was learning how difficult it was to govern people who possessed rights of their own.

He missed her. He also knew that if he traded away those rights to hurry home, she would ask him why he had gone at all.

---

Miren found Luceran with a bowl of cold broth and Ardel's road sketch.

"You have forgotten supper," she said.

"I have remembered every expense in the kingdom."

"Then perhaps you can remember the broth."

He ate while she sat beside him. She did not ask what he thought of Ardel, and he did not ask what she knew of Ardel's winter plans. The silence was deliberate rather than empty.

"Corven could be using his councils as a shield," Luceran said.

"He could."

"He could be waiting for a larger payment."

"He could."

"And you still want me to negotiate."

"I want you to find out which is true without stealing his conversations."

The reminder hurt because it was fair. Luceran had not called Edselsto to listen through the connected arches. Miren was glad of that. She did not call it forgiveness.

"The castle asks nothing for its help," he said.

"Nothing you can see on an account."

"It has not demanded coin."

"Neither do all creditors at first."

He looked at her. She touched the cloth near the scar Marek's knife had left above his shoulder.

"I am frightened of choosing badly," he said.

"Then choose with people who can tell you when you are wrong."

"They can refuse."

"Yes."

He reached for her hand and she gave it to him. She stayed long enough to tell him about a woman in the village whose new roof leaked on her bed, and who had threatened to bring the faulty beam itself to the royal court as evidence. He laughed at the image. It was not a lesson disguised as a story. The roof truly leaked, and the woman wanted it fixed.

When Miren kissed him goodbye, he did not ask what the kiss promised. She did not answer the marriage question he had once overheard without permission.

Outside, a living Doorwarden announced her passage. The silver break in the south-gallery withdrawing room remained open. No new listening branch had been approved.

---

The first timbers for the receiving-yard roof arrived two mornings later.

Vess had set the posts away from the weakened east wall. Pell Falbrecht refused to hurry the rope team merely because a royal clerk was watching.

"A roof that falls on the grain will not keep it dry," he said.

The clerk objected that the League convoy was not yet agreed.

"Then it will keep our own grain dry."

Damaris had released the timber and wages against the measured design. The expense would leave less of the usable Eastern Household Reserve for the next discretionary silver restoration. She entered that consequence plainly.

Corven joined Luceran beside the work. His bad knee made the uneven stones a trial, though he would not accept an arm.

Damaris laid out the proposed civilian passage: named barges, named wagons, a bond against loss, staged arrivals, a roofed store and no troop movement under the civilian warrant. Corven would recommend it to Merehaven and Teth. Dols's bridge reeve still had to approve the hours. No three-bench consent had yet arrived.

"Then I am paying for a roof before I know whether the grain will come," Luceran said.

"Yes," Damaris replied. "The roof will remain useful if the grain does not."

Hesta and Yara stood near the carts with their unsigned proposal for quarry-stone return carriage. Both women had done work that would matter whether or not the king and envoy reached a treaty.

Rhyse asked again about military passage.

"I cannot give you a right I do not possess," Corven said.

"Your refusal may leave men hungry."

"I know. I have written to the council asking them to receive your captain. I cannot make them agree."

Luceran authorized the first civilian bond to be deposited only after the required consents arrived. He directed the limited Tervain purchase to proceed on negotiated terms, ordered an alternate supply march prepared without assuming Kaelmont's pass would stay open, and sent another request to Dols. Jorvan's narrower offer remained unanswered. Cendreval's exclusivity remained refused.

He had chosen roads, wages, timber, grain and negotiation. He had not chosen a single solution large enough to end the danger.

The workmen lifted the first beam. A porter shouted for the rope to be eased, and six men obeyed in their own time. The timber settled safely into its sockets.

Corven watched it with the king.

"You have made choices that can be carried out," he said.

"Not enough of them."

"No," Corven answered. "Not enough."

The envoy had not been defeated. Neither had the Crown. Both had discovered how much ordinary cooperation could cost when nobody possessed the right to command everybody else.

In the family nave, all seven candles burned steadily.
