# Season Two, Episode 32 — The Useful Monster

Etta laid the copy of Luceran's admission beneath a weight and waited for the ink to dry. Aldren had written the words as the king spoke them: he had ordered the attack on Corven Sareth, and his later denial to Dols had been false. The document established that those words were spoken. It did not turn Senna and Harven into witnesses to the earlier killing.

Senna, who had come from Dols with the reeve's authority to receive an answer, wanted to inspect Ysabet's original private instruction before departing. Ysabet placed it on a cloth, showed the seal and the old fold, and retained it in her own custody. A witnessed comparison would travel; the original would not.

"I shall tell the reeve I saw it," Senna said. "I shall not tell him I saw the order given."

Harven asked for his miller's-lane testimony to travel under a different seal. The falling stone, the injured apprentice and Jalen's impossible survival were matters he had witnessed. Corven's earlier attack was not. Etta prepared two packets and entered the expense of the extra wax and copying hour. Aldren attested his transcription only. No one was asked to certify more than their own part.

Rhyse offered six living guards as escort to the lower road house. Senna accepted protection but retained the right to dismiss the men after that point. Rhyse gave the captain a written order not to seize or examine the witnesses' packets. The captain had served a king whose command had been publicly contradicted, and understood why the words needed to be written.

The witnesses would depart at dawn. The reeve would learn of Luceran's admission when the papers and people reached him, not before. Merehaven and Teth would receive Yara's separately dispatched reports on their own roads and make their own decisions. Edselsto could not shorten the miles between three river courts.

---

The receiving-yard carpenter refused to work on the gutter while the staging stood in water. Moving the staging meant shifting the six sacks of grain again. The grain was sound; the roof remained incomplete; the second wagon still had no accepted Dols wall to cross.

Damaris paid the carter the waiting wages and fodder his contract required, then released his team. He would have preferred another day's hire, but he also wanted his horses home before the road worsened. She told him the truth: if Dols accepted the wall tomorrow, the Crown would have to hire him or someone else afresh. Releasing the wagon saved money and lost readiness. Both consequences went into her account.

The Merehaven factor examined the witnessed inventory. His principals had advanced against the first delivery, which had reached the northern posts. That receipt remained valid. He had no authority to release a second advance merely because a king had admitted a lie, or because a clerk assured him the remaining sacks existed.

"You may send your principals my count," Damaris said. "Not my assurance that a closed bridge will open."

"You would have given me that assurance a month ago."

"I would have given you a date I hoped to keep."

He looked at her without satisfaction. He had wanted a functioning grain route, not a lesson in royal dishonesty. He agreed to carry the count as evidence of goods, not as a pledge of passage.

Tomas was sent to price a replacement team. The carpenter received wages for the safe staging work, not an unlimited repair. Damaris dictated a notice to the northern posts: six sacks delivered, six still at Edselsto, no authorized date for the next movement. Eighteen patrol mounts remained withdrawn. Men at the posts would have to plan their rations around that unwelcome truth.

A yard worker asked whether the foreigners would take the grain back. Damaris said no one had requested that. When he asked whether they might, she said she did not know. He went back to moving boards, disappointed but able to plan around something more solid than confidence.

---

Jalen examined the carpenter's drawing for a supported chair. He asked for a lower side so he could reach a book without help. The carpenter marked the change.

Isera had told him that better furniture could ease his days, not restore his mobility. Jalen accepted the distinction. He wanted a small measure of independence, not another promise made to comfort a king.

Rhyse brought the casualty-roll reference, which pointed to a missing original discharge. The search had not found the document. Jalen asked him to keep looking.

Yara came with a writing board and asked whether he wanted a letter sent. He gave her the last address he remembered and dictated a request that his old service papers be found. A few lines belonged only to the person he hoped would read them; he asked that those lines not enter the Crown register.

Yara agreed. Her three river warrants gave her no claim over his private correspondence, and she had no reason to pretend otherwise. Rhyse offered a named courier from the ordinary household account, with no condition that Jalen perform another duty in return.

The carved horse from the Dols apprentice lay beside the bed. Jalen turned it in his hand and asked that the boy be told he owed nothing further for the rescue. He wanted the child to grow up without believing that gratitude was a lifelong debt.

The carpenter returned to ask whether the chair should be finished in royal colors. Jalen said plain wood would do. He could not recover what had been lost, but he could still decide what was made for him.


---

At the lower road house, Senna checked both seals while Harven fed the horses. The Crown escort kept its distance. The captain knew his men could protect the witnesses from a roadside thief, but not from every consequence of carrying a sovereign's admission home.

Harven feared the accident would be repeated in market gossip as proof of Corven's murder. A broken man had spoken after an unsurvivable injury. That fact was dreadful enough without making the miller's mason a witness to an event he had not seen.

"Then keep the cart in its own account," Senna said. "Our reeve can ask what it proves."

"Will he believe the king?"

"He can compare the records. Belief is not ours to carry for him."

They resumed the road. Neither knew when Yara's separate messages would reach Merehaven or Teth. Dols had not yet received its witnesses. No foreign court had issued a new verdict, and no crossing authority had changed.

---
Leora's representative reached the village watch office after a wet morning on the road. He had been instructed to inspect original records, not to announce a verdict. The clerk showed him the missing-person entry, the preserved broken walking stick and the dated accounts of two attendants. The original book stayed on its shelf while the visitor compared the copies already sent toward Dols.

The widow who had rented the unused room repeated what she remembered. She had seen a room remain empty; she had not seen anyone die. Her chimney leaked and she wanted the promised repair before winter. The visitor entered that concern beside her testimony rather than treating her ordinary needs as an obstacle to the investigation.

One attendant still carried his shoulder stiffly. He asked whether the visitor had brought authority to settle his outstanding lodging account. The visitor had not. He recorded the question and promised only to report it. He would not purchase a more dramatic statement with a payment he had no authority to make.

He paid the ordinary copying charge and left the watch book and stick with their original custodians. His dated inventory distinguished what he had seen from what the witnesses told him. It began the journey back to Leora. She had not received it and knew nothing of the king's later admission.

At her house, Leora helped her mother finish a bowl of broth and listened to her niece explaining why the ribbon horse needed a second harness. She had asked whether she might write to the man calling himself Jalen under his own name, but no reply had arrived. She kept her original marriage letters and the private-question refusal locked separately. Waiting did not mean she had stopped living.

---
Miren agreed to walk with Luceran in the south gallery. He had sent a servant to ask; he had not entered her physically deaf room to fetch her.

He told her the witnesses were on the road and that he had left Ysabet in possession of the original instruction. She acknowledged both facts. He asked whether they changed what she thought of him.

"They change what I can say you did this morning."

"I told the truth yesterday."

"Yes."

"And you will still sleep apart."

"Yes."

He almost argued that the six sacks already delivered north had fed soldiers who otherwise would have gone hungry. Miren would not deny it. That was what made the argument worthless as a request for forgiveness.

"I wanted you to see that I could choose differently," he said.

"I saw it. I will not make the choice into a return to what we were."

She spoke without contempt. She knew he could govern competently, and she knew he had ordered Corven's death and lied afterward. Neither knowledge erased the other. Love had not stopped, but intimacy and political trust had not returned.

Rhyse arrived with the final witness-protection instruction. It named the departing Dols party and prohibited retaliation against servants who had spoken truthfully about the lane, mortuary or escort. Rhyse had signed it under his own office. Luceran read it, signed beneath him and entered the hour. The order could protect someone. It also prevented the king from later pretending that he had never known the witnesses were allowed to speak.

Miren touched his sleeve once before returning to her room. He did not follow her. It was an old gesture and not a pardon.

---
The cistern mason brought Luceran a drawing of the blocked overflow. Edselsto remembered an old eastern outlet and the location of a mason's mark. The living mason found the mark exactly where the castle had said it would be. The passage beyond was buried, and no one could tell whether it still carried water.

"I can open the first length with timber and another man," the mason said. "I will not pull the face down alone."

Luceran authorized the bounded inspection and its wages. Edselsto had given a useful memory, not a survey of the present wall. He understood the distinction. It did not diminish the relief of receiving an answer without a quarrel.

After the mason left, he asked the castle whether it remembered another gutter above the eastern foundation. Edselsto answered promptly. It did not ask whether he ought to consult Ysabet, Rhyse or Damaris, or whether Miren had forgiven him. It had no grounds to know what a foreign bench would decide or what the people in the deaf room felt.

At supper fewer chairs were occupied. Ysabet had original records to secure. Rhyse had watches to relieve. Damaris was counting the cost of a wagon that would not depart. Miren had chosen her own room. Jalen ate in the infirmary under his own name. The foreign witnesses were still traveling, and the river benches had not yet spoken.

Luceran ate with those who remained and asked after the mason's timber. Later he passed through the family nave, where all seven candles burned. He was grateful for their steady light without understanding what sustained it.

Edselsto was ready whenever he wished to speak.
