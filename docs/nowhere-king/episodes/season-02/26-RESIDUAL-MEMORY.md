# Season Two, Episode 26 — Residual Memory

The wounded attendant could sit upright now, though he resented the help required to do it.

The cooper's widow had lent him the narrow chair from her kitchen. It was the only one with a back high enough for his injured shoulder. He had been sleeping above her shop since the attack, and her patience had lasted longer than his fever. She wanted the room back before the autumn barrels came in.

Yara Fen had brought the village watch clerk, not a royal notary. The clerk set his book on the widow's flour board, and the widow objected until he put a clean cloth beneath it.

"Tell it from the lane," Yara said.

"I told it before."

"You were fevered before. You may remember something differently. We will keep both accounts."

The attendant closed his eyes. He remembered Corven Sareth stopping at the bottom of the cooper's rear stair, testing the sunken step with the ferrule of his walking stick. He remembered the envoy making an impatient remark about the price of timber. He remembered men coming through the lane and his own shoulder striking the doorframe. After that, a horse's scream, somebody shouting to run, and a gap that no amount of questioning would fill.

"How many men?"

"Three where I could see them. There may have been another by the wall. I cannot swear to him."

The clerk entered the uncertainty. The other attendant, the one who had run for the watch, stood by the window and confirmed only what he had himself seen.

Yara asked about the stick. The wounded man said Corven had planted it to his right before taking weight on his old left knee. He had done it that way for years, even when he was trying to appear impatient rather than lame.

"Would he always?"

"No. If someone startled him, he'd reach for the nearest support. But when he had time to choose, that was his way."

"And you saw him do it that evening?"

"I saw him test the step. I saw the stick in his right hand."

The clerk read the statement aloud. The attendant corrected *fell* to *was driven against the doorframe*. He would not swear whether a man had pushed him or he had stumbled while escaping. The clerk made the correction without complaint.

"What becomes of this?" the widow asked.

"The original stays with your watch," Yara said. "My copy will say who spoke and who did not."

"And his wages?"

The wounded man turned his face away. He had been paid for escorting an envoy, not for the weeks in which he could not lift his arm. The widow had been feeding him broth on credit.

Yara entered the unpaid lodging and treatment claim separately from the attack account. A witness could tell the truth and still require supper.

The broken walking stick remained wrapped beside the original missing-person entry. Nobody had proved whose blood was on it. The clerk would not write a death where he had recorded a disappearance.

---

At Leora Sareth's house the rain had stopped, but her mother's cough had not.

Her niece had tied a ribbon around the kitchen table leg and declared it a horse. Leora's mother, from her chair near the hearth, told the child that a horse ought to be fed. The girl brought it half a crust, then demanded payment for the service.

"Your uncle would have argued about the rate," Leora said.

"Uncle Corven would have paid," her mother replied.

"He would have argued first."

It was the first time either woman had spoken of him in the past tense without immediately correcting herself.

The sealed private question had left several days earlier. Leora knew the courier's route and the ford at which a rain could keep him waiting. She had paid for a return receipt, not for speed that weather and horses could not provide. The latest news still came from the first attack warning. No one had answered whether her husband was alive.

She took the old letters from her chest. One was written entirely in Corven's impatient hand, its last two lines turned across the margin. Another had been dictated during a fever years before, and bore a clerk's elegant script with Corven's own crooked mark beneath. She laid them beside the unfinished joke about the rosemary pie.

Her housekeeper came in carrying linen and saw the papers.

"Do you want them sent to the court?"

"No."

"Then why have them out?"

"Because someone may ask when I last knew his hand from another man's."

The housekeeper had received the handwritten letter from Corven's messenger and remembered the seal being broken at this table. She could attest that much. She could not attest what Corven had thought while writing it. Leora asked her to mark a short inventory: which originals remained in the chest, which were written and which dictated, and who had handled them. The steward witnessed the marks. Neither servant was asked to swear that a change of handwriting meant a change of man.

Her mother watched without offering advice. At last she said, "You are preparing to be disbelieved."

"I am preparing not to have to remember a date through grief."

"Have you decided he is dead?"

Leora looked at the ribbon horse under the table. The girl had fallen asleep against its leg, her small fist still closed around the imaginary reins.

"No," she said. "I have decided that a stranger's assurance will not decide it for me."

She locked the originals away. She did not send her mother's household on a journey she could not yet justify. The question was already traveling.

Jalen Orris had discovered that Corven Sareth's knee was worst after sitting still.

He could march the length of the gallery with scarcely a limp if he began slowly. At council he was expected to sit, rise, and sit again while people whose names he had never heard addressed him as though their quarrels belonged to his life. The first step after a long argument could be agony. He had learned to keep the walking stick close, although the one given him was not the broken one found in the village.

Ysabet brought him a practice sheet for Merehaven's factor. It set out the difference between the man who owned a sack, the man who carried it and the man whose receipt established the hour of loss. Jalen had asked to understand it. He would not learn a false memory merely to satisfy a foreign bench.

"Suppose the sack is wet when it reaches the warehouse," he said.

"Then they will ask who received it, and when."

"And if the carrier says it was wet before he took it?"

"Then someone must produce the earlier tally."

Jalen worked through the sums with the patience of a soldier cleaning a weapon he had not chosen. He could understand the problem. He could not remember the afternoon when Corven had made the factor furious over it.

A paymaster's clerk appeared at the door. He had found a reference to Jalen's casualty entry in an older roll, but the corresponding discharge account was in another chest under a different keeper. He had not seen a receipt from Jalen's sister.

"Then you haven't found whether she was paid," Jalen said.

"No."

"Have you found where she is?"

"No."

The clerk looked mortified. Jalen knew the expression. He had worn it himself when ordered to report a missing piece of equipment to an officer who had lost friends.

"Bring me the account when you have it," he said. "Not a promise that it must exist."

When the clerk left, Jalen turned the practice sheet sideways. His hand folded the lower corner with a swift movement he had never learned in life. Corven's fingers seemed to know where a letter ought to crease. That small obedience of muscle troubled him more than the knee.

"If the body can remember a fold," he asked Ysabet, "why not a wife?"

"I do not know what it can retain. We know what it has not given you."

He smoothed the sheet flat again.

"Then don't tell them I remember."

Leora's courier reached Edselsto after a night spent waiting for a washed-out track to settle. The gate watch entered his name, his route, his charge and the seal on the packet. Pel Ordan stood with the other living guard. Iven Marrick watched from his own prepared threshold and did not receive the letter in either man's place.

The courier had been told to place the packet in Corven's hands. Yara came down to the gate and recognized Leora's seal. She did not open it.

"He is at the castle," she told the man. "I can bring you to him. You may decide what you have witnessed afterward."

The courier was tired enough to be frightened by the offer. He followed her through the inhabited corridors, past a carpenter carrying roof pegs and two kitchen servants disputing the use of a dry storeroom. Nothing about the place resembled a court waiting for a ghost.

Jalen received them in the south-gallery chamber whose listening bridge had been removed. Ysabet was present to record what occurred; Yara had asked for a witness whose papers would not be altered by a household messenger. The courier stood where he could see the seal broken.

The question was short. Leora asked where Corven had hung her mother's wet cloak in the first winter of their marriage after the old woman forbade him to leave it near the hearth, and what name he had given the crooked peg.

Jalen read it twice. A childhood memory of his own came to him instead: his sister scolding him for putting a wet coat over their father's tools. He could see the tools more clearly than the page.

He looked at Yara. "Do you know?"

"No. It is not my marriage."

"Could I have seen the answer in one of his letters?"

"Perhaps. She has kept the answer with her."

Ysabet waited. The courier shifted his weight and looked at the man whose face matched the description he had been given. He had no reason to know what a mortuary was. He had been paid to deliver a sealed question to a living hand.

Jalen set the paper down. "I cannot answer her."

"Do you mean you refuse?" the courier asked.

"I mean I do not remember. If I guess, she will be asked to trust a guess."

Yara asked whether he wished to put that statement in writing. Jalen looked at the page, at Corven's practiced fingers, and at the space where a husband's name would ordinarily go.

"Write what I said," he answered. "Do not put his name beneath it for me."

Ysabet recorded the hour, the intact seal as received, the opening in the courier's presence and Jalen's exact refusal to claim memory. She wrote that the person presented to the courier as Corven Sareth had spoken. She did not certify that he was Corven, or that his failure proved he was not. The courier marked that he had heard the words. Jalen permitted the account to be sent to Leora, but would not sign the dead envoy's name.

The original question remained under Ysabet's sealed custody pending a witnessed return or Leora's instruction. Yara prepared a separate copy of the exchange for Leora and another for the village watch. The courier accepted the return packet and a paid lodging place for the night. He would leave when the road could bear a horse.

No one in the room had supplied the private answer.

The second attendant came uphill from the village to ask after the wounded man's wages.

He had been told that the envoy had recovered. He did not know what that word was supposed to mean for the man who had carried Corven's bags, or for the companion still sleeping in a cooper's spare room.

Yara met him outside the receiving yard. A cart stood beneath the unfinished roof while carpenters fitted a board above the six sacks still stored there. Another six sacks waited beyond Dols. The attendant watched the men measure twice before cutting. Corven had liked that sort of delay. He had never liked paying twice for a mistake.

"I want him to hear what happened to us," the attendant said.

"He has heard that you were attacked."

"From you?"

"From several people."

"That isn't what I asked."

Yara understood him. He wanted the man who had ordered him into the lane to recognize that he had survived it. She could not offer the recognition he wanted. She arranged a short meeting in an ordinary reception room, with Rhyse's living guard outside and no claim that the attendant had come to identify a shade.

Jalen rose when the man entered. He rose too quickly and his left knee caught. He reached across his body for the nearest chair instead of taking the stick at his right.

The attendant stopped. Corven had often cursed that knee, but when he had a moment to choose, he planted the stick before he stood. The attendant had learned to wait without offering his arm. Corven hated being helped before he asked.

"Your shoulder?" Jalen said, seeing the man's torn sleeve.

"My companion's. Mine was only bruised."

"I am sorry."

The attendant had expected a sharper question: whether his companion's fever had broken, whether the widow had been paid, whether the men in the lane had been found. He had rehearsed answers to all three. Instead he was looking at a man who spoke with Corven's voice and watched him with the courteous uncertainty of a stranger.

"You used to say a man who carried your letters ought not be made to carry your excuses," he said.

Jalen searched the words for a memory. There was none.

"Then I should not make you carry mine."

It was a decent answer. The attendant hated it for being decent.

He asked for the injured man's wages and the widow's account. Jalen told him that Ysabet would receive both and that he would not promise payment from an account he did not control. The attendant could not fault that either. He left with a written acknowledgment of the claim, not coin.

On the way down he went to the watch clerk. He gave his own account of the meeting and of the familiar habit he had not seen. The clerk entered it as a new observation by a named witness, not as a correction to the original attack record. The wounded companion had seen Corven in the lane; this man had seen the apparent Corven at Edselsto. Those were different facts.

"Could the blow have changed him?" the clerk asked.

"Yes."

"Could pain make him reach for a chair?"

"Yes."

"Then what are you asking me to write?"

"That I knew the man before, and I did not feel known by him afterward."

The clerk read that sentence back without improving it.

Damaris found Luceran over the northern carriage account, with three prices for hiring men to take the far-bank sacks onward.

The cheapest offered only a dry-weather journey and would not answer for grain spoiled in an unroofed halt. The second required an advance the Crown could not yet spare without postponing the smith. The third offered guarded transport but demanded the horses Rhyse had already withdrawn from patrol. None was a contract. None put bread in the northern posts.

"We can ask the carriers to bid again," Damaris said. "We cannot make a horse by accepting the lowest number."

Luceran had been thinking of Leora's question. Ysabet had sent him a copy of the witnessed exchange, not the sealed original. The answer had not been supplied to him either.

"Could the messenger be held until I have spoken to him?"

"On what charge?" Rhyse asked from the doorway.

"Not held. Delayed for another letter."

"He has been paid to carry an answer. If you order delay, the courier master must enter the order."

Luceran looked at the account. He had given the lethal instruction that made the letter necessary. He could now make its delivery a second exercise of royal power. He could tell himself he needed another day to protect the grain. The north would still have no sacks tomorrow.

"Let him depart when the road is fit," he said. "Enter the packet and its witnesses. The Crown's own factual reply may travel separately."

Yara had come to hear the decision. She did not thank him. She asked that her watch copy be carried by a different messenger, so the loss of one packet would not erase both accounts. Damaris agreed to pay the ordinary charge and entered it against the envoy household, not the northern grain.

"Does the reply accuse me?" Luceran asked.

"It says what was seen," Yara answered. "Leora may decide what she thinks of it."

"And the foreign benches?"

"They will receive the facts relevant to their own warrants. None of them is entitled to Leora's marriage."

After she left, Luceran told Rhyse to see that the wounded man's lodging claim was examined without requiring him to testify again for his supper. Rhyse accepted the order and insisted on a named paymaster and a written decision. He had learned how much a good intention could cost when no one was made responsible for carrying it out.

---

Miren was at the far end of the gallery when Luceran found her.

She had come from the physically deaf room and carried her own key. The servants passed between them with linen and candles, careful not to listen more closely than their work required. She waited until they had gone.

"You let the letter leave," she said.

"Ysabet would have recorded it if I had not."

"Yes."

"I wanted to stop it."

"I know."

He looked at her then. She had not heard the private question and did not ask to. She knew he had murdered Corven, knew why Jalen's voice could not answer for the man, and knew that a wife far away had been made to wait for news no one dared send plainly.

"I could have had Edselsto answer for him," Luceran said. "It may remember something said in the rooms where he stayed."

"Would it remember his first winter with Leora?"

"No."

"Then you would have made the castle into another clerk copying a name it could not warrant."

He might have argued that he had resisted. He did not. His restraint had been prompted by a witness who could write down the hour of his interference. That was still better than interference, but it was not innocence.

"Do you think she will believe the reply?" he asked.

"I think she will know what it does not say."

Miren turned her key in her hand. She still wanted to reach for him when he looked so tired. Wanting was not an instruction she had to obey.

"The question was hers," she said. "Do not take her answer from her as well."

At the village watch house, the clerk put the attendant's new statement beside the original missing-person entry, with separate dates and names. In Leora's chest, the old letters remained under their witnessed inventory. Two paid couriers took different roads out of Edselsto, each carrying an account with its own custodian. Neither carried proof of a death or knowledge of the mortuary rite.

The first surety remained valid. The second advance was withheld. The Dols bridge had no general passage or military countersign, Teth had seized no ferry, and no sack had reached the northern posts. The undercroft's next turn remained unentered. No new dead keeper had been bound.

In the family nave, all seven candles burned.
