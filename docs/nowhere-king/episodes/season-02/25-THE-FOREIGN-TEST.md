# Season Two, Episode 25 — The Foreign Test

Yara Fen set three letters beside three warrants and put a stone on the corner of each.

The wind had found the gap in the envoy household's window frame. Every time it rose, the paper nearest the sill lifted as though somebody beneath it were breathing. Corven had complained about the draught. He had then refused the offered change of chamber because the clerks had already learned where to find him.

The first letter came from Dols. Its reeve wanted the name of the living officer empowered to attest the western approach account. The second, forwarded through a Merehaven factor, asked who could undertake a further surety now that the envoy was said to have recovered from an attack. The third was an acknowledgment from Teth, not a consent. Its bench had received Yara's caution and would continue the owner-witnessed ferry valuations under its own authority.

None of them asked the same question.

Aldren arrived with a fair copy of the household's proposed answer. It began, *The envoy has returned to his duties.*

"Whose duties?" Yara asked.

"His own, I suppose."

"That is precisely what they want proved."

He had copied the line from a royal message. She did not accuse him of inventing it. She showed him Corven's three warrants, each carrying a different seal, and made him read the clauses permitting recommendation. None empowered an absent or recovered envoy to certify a bridge captain's military passage, pledge a merchant's coin, or seize a ferry.

"Could he not sign that he is himself?" Aldren asked.

"He could sign a hundred times. A signature does not make a council surrender its seal."

"Then what shall I write?"

"That the factual accounts remain available for inspection. That the benches should name their own witnesses. That I hold the warrants and will not release them on a report of recovery."

He looked at the fourth paper, the village-watch copy she had brought uphill. It recorded an attack, a missing man, a wounded attendant and witnesses who had not seen the missing man return. The original remained in the village.

"Will you enclose that?"

"Not in a circular letter. Each recipient must know who made the entry and what the witnesses actually saw."

"His Majesty will say you are making this difficult."

"Corven would have said the same thing. Then he would have asked me to check his arithmetic."

Aldren smiled despite himself. Yara did not. The smile had hurt more than she expected.

She signed three different answers. She paid the messengers from the envoy household's remaining allowance, entered the routes and dates in her own book, and sent no claim that the letters would arrive together.

---

At Dols the bridge reeve had two documents before him: the last civilian passage entered in his watch book and a fresh royal description of the western approach.

The first bore the watch captain's hand. It allowed one wagon in one daylight interval. The second described the same crossing as evidence that the road could serve a continuing supply.

"Those are not the same proposition," the reeve said.

Captain Savelin stood with mud drying on his boots. He had inspected the approach more often than some of the council's own men. He did not defend the description.

"The wagon crossed," he said. "The next was stopped when the water rose."

"And you did not order your soldiers across."

"I did not possess the countersign."

The miller, called because the drainage cut still touched his garden, wanted his wall repaired before another loaded wheel passed. He had no interest in whether a foreign envoy had risen from a sickbed. The next cart would damage his property in exactly the same way regardless of who wrote the letter.

The reeve laid down a wax impression taken from the earlier Dols warrant. Its shape matched the seal on Yara's enclosed copy. He had a record of that original, and a record of the hour his own watch had permitted the wagon.

"I am asked to accept a new recommendation in Sareth's name," he said. "I am not asked to accept a new command from this bench. Yet the Crown's copy speaks as though one entails the other."

"Then return it for correction," Savelin said.

"That I shall. And I want the man who says he recovered to appear before a witness I appoint, or to send an attestation with a living witness who saw him give it. Not a clerk who copied the name from a prior sheet."

The watch captain scratched his beard. "Will that prove he is Sareth?"

"No. It will prove who made the statement. I shall compare his mark and ask about the terms he disputed here. I may still refuse the recommendation."

The reeve did not know of a mortuary, a shade, or a royal killing. He knew that an official who had been reported missing now purported to speak again, and that Dols would pay the cost of believing the wrong person.

He ordered the miller's claim copied with the crossing record. The ferryman received his fee for the earlier watch, not a promise of a future crossing. Savelin carried away the corrected account and the reeve's separate request. The gate remained shut to another grain wagon.

---

Merehaven's factor counted the six sacks in the Edselsto store from the signed receipt sent by his own witness. They were dry when counted. That was the extent of his certainty.

A merchant waiting beside the table had brought a proposed second advance. He wanted to know why the first cargo had reached the far bank and stopped there. The factor showed him the unpaid carriage line. Nobody had hired the northern carrier.

"The Crown says the envoy has recovered," the merchant said. "Surely he can guarantee the rest."

"The envoy did not guarantee the first lot," the factor replied. "The Crown bound coin against named receipts. My witness counted the sacks. Our bench accepted that limited surety."

"Then ask him for a larger bond."

"I have. I have also asked who is authorized to recommend it."

The merchant tapped the letter. Its final paragraph carried Corven's name in a fair hand, with no note of who had heard him dictate it. The factor had once argued with Corven for an afternoon about whether a spoiled sack counted as loss to a carrier or a loss to a warehouse. Corven had insisted that the answer depended on which man held the receipt at the hour of damage. He had been irritatingly right.

"If he is fit to negotiate," the factor said, "he can explain why the owner of the grain and the man carrying it require different protections. I want that answer witnessed, not polished into the language of this letter by a clerk."

"You have his earlier opinions."

"I have his earlier opinions. That is why I know the difference."

He would not call an unfamiliar hand proof of imposture. A sick man might dictate. A wounded man might change his script. A prudent factor could accept both facts and still refuse to place fresh merchant coin at risk.

He entered a hold against the second advance, not a repudiation of the first. The existing six-sack surety remained valid. The receiving roof still had work to finish. The merchant could take his proposal elsewhere, but would not be permitted to call Merehaven's silence assent.

A copy of the hold went to Yara, another to Damaris, and the original stayed under Merehaven's seal.

---

At Teth, Mistress Calven made the bench clerk read the proposed ferry valuation aloud before she would set her mark to it.

The two boatwrights had disagreed about the cracked gunwale. One priced the repair as timber and iron; the other included the days the ferry would be laid up. Calven had passengers booked on those days. She could not replace their fares with a handsome statement about royal necessity.

"The first valuation names my hull," she said. "The second names the Crown's possible need. Neither names who will pay if the captain takes her before the dispute is heard."

The clerk turned to the warrant Corven had carried. It permitted the envoy to recommend terms. It did not authorize requisition. He had already read that clause three times in other people's company.

"An assurance has arrived from Edselsto," he said. "The envoy is said to be attending council again."

"Then let him attend this bench," Calven answered. "Or let a man of ours watch him put his name to what he means."

"The journey is long."

"So is rebuilding a ferry."

One boatwright asked whether the bench would require an examination of the envoy's injuries. The clerk said a physician could describe a body but could not confer a commission. A witness to speech, a comparison with earlier correspondence and a question about the contested valuation might help them decide whether to receive a recommendation. None would substitute for Calven's right to challenge the price.

They sealed a request for an in-person appearance or a properly witnessed reply, with a copy of the valuation disputed by Calven. They did not cancel the boatwrights' work. The boats still needed honest measurements whether the Crown ever hired them or not.

Calven left with her own copy. She had no notion that a murdered man's body was walking at Edselsto. She knew only that men far away had begun speaking as though her property belonged to a promise she had never made.

---

Leora Sareth received the first warning while her mother was sleeping.

The messenger had taken a slower road after the lower ford flooded. His boots and the charge entered on his waybill were evidence enough of the delay. The paper he delivered had been written before the newer reports of a supposed recovery.

*Corven was attacked. His condition cannot yet be established.*

Leora read it at the kitchen table. Her youngest niece was under the table again, hiding from the physician who had come to see her grandmother. The girl had decided that all physicians carried bitter medicines, and that the safest way to refuse them was to become part of the furniture.

Corven would have laughed. He had laughed in his last letter about an onion pie served at Edselsto and the cook's alarming devotion to rosemary. Leora had begun a reply accusing him of treating foreign kitchens as a military danger. The unfinished page was still folded inside her household book.

Her mother woke and asked whether the news was good.

"I do not know," Leora said.

"Then why are you standing as though you do?"

Leora sat. She had married a man who could be stubborn for a whole evening about the proper place to hang wet cloaks and gentle with a frightened child before anyone else noticed the fear. His official letters were careful. His private letters had an untidy habit of turning the page sideways when he ran out of room. None of that proved he could not be injured, confused, or alive.

She sent for the household's older correspondence. She chose one letter in his own hand and one she knew he had dictated, because she would not mistake a clerk's script for a husband's identity. Then she wrote two requests.

The first went openly to Yara: who had seen the attack, who had seen Corven afterward, and who had examined his injuries? She asked for names, dates and the physician's own words, not a court assurance.

The second she sealed for Corven alone. It asked about a small matter from their life before the river commission, something no treaty clerk needed to know. She did not write the answer anywhere in the outgoing packet. She kept the older letter in her own chest.

Her mother watched her seal it.

"Are you asking whether he remembers you?"

"I am asking whether the man who answers has a reason to know."

"That is a cruel question to ask a wounded husband."

"Yes," Leora said. "I hope I have to apologize for it."

She paid the courier herself. Her letters would take days to reach Edselsto. Until then she had a warning, an unfinished joke about rosemary and no proof that her husband had returned.

The first two foreign replies reached Edselsto on different mornings.

Jalen Orris saw the Merehaven request on Ysabet's table. He recognized his borrowed hands before he recognized the name at the top of the sheet. Corven's left knee had stiffened overnight. Jalen kept the leg straight beneath the chair so that nobody would see him shift it.

"They want me to say why a merchant and a carrier should be paid differently," he said.

"They want to know whether the man who makes the recommendation understands the obligations," Ysabet answered.

"I can understand them if someone explains."

"You can. That is not the same as remembering why Corven argued about them."

Jalen read the next line slowly. The factor wanted a living witness to the answer. Dols wanted a man present when a mark was made. Teth had sent a separate request, still on the road. He could not remember an afternoon of argument in Merehaven because he had never been there.

"Would it help if I told them my name?"

"It would tell them something true."

"And ruin what the king says has saved the grain."

Ysabet did not tell him that the grain was saved. Six sacks remained beneath the receiving roof, six beyond Dols and none at the northern posts. The first surety and the paid wages were real. So were the hungry men.

"Have they found my sister?" he asked.

"The paymaster has not yet supplied the original casualty account. I have no confirmed answer."

He nodded. He had expected that answer and still found himself hating it. The kingdom could send a rider to ask a foreign bench whether a dead envoy had written a letter, but it had not yet established whether a soldier's sister had received the money due after his death.

Luceran came in before Jalen could rise. He had read the Dols demand and had brought a draft attestation with the royal seal left blank.

"Can you give them an answer?" he asked.

"About the bridge? I can tell them what the men measured."

"About your authority."

Jalen looked at the draft. It began with Corven's name.

"I did not receive his authority when I received his knee."

Luceran took the sheet back. For a moment he wanted Edselsto to remember the right words, to supply the old man's manner and save everyone the expense of another journey. He knew the castle had heard conversations in connected rooms. It had not been present in Corven's marriage or at every foreign bench. Even an exact recollection of a sentence would not be a living commission.

"Then I shall answer as king," he said.

"Tell them what I can do," Jalen replied. "And what I cannot."

Ysabet recorded the distinction. She did not record Jalen as having agreed to remain in the body indefinitely. He had asked how to leave it, and nobody had answered.

---

Damaris spread the three foreign papers across the council table with the northern account below them.

Dols would accept a witnessed statement of what had actually been said, but would not extend civilian passage or issue a military countersign on that basis. Merehaven would honor the first surety and withhold the second advance until its own witnesses and authority were satisfied. Teth would continue the owner-challengeable valuation and receive no undertaking in Corven's name without its own hearing. Two benches had explicitly withheld confidence in fresh claims of the envoy's authority; the third had refused to treat a reported recovery as a warrant.

"Their objections are different," Yara said. "That is why one handsome answer will not serve."

Luceran had expected difficulty. He had not expected to miss Corven's arguments so much. The man had been infuriating precisely because he knew which refusals belonged to which people. Jalen had obeyed a request to sit in council. He could not replace the knowledge that had made Corven worth opposing.

Rhyse asked whether a royal messenger might carry the physician's report. Ysabet said it could, provided the report was copied without adding a conclusion the physician had refused to make. The village watch would retain its own entry. Yara would identify which facts she had seen and which she had received from others.

Damaris turned the northern account toward the king. "We can pay the roof workers this week. We cannot call six sacks beyond Dols a delivered ration. Nor can I pledge another merchant's coin by writing that the envoy has returned."

"Then send the facts," Luceran said. "Let the benches name their witnesses. No new claim in Corven's hand."

Yara had wanted those words days earlier. Hearing them now did not make her grateful. She asked that the order be entered with the earlier claims so that nobody could pretend the correction had always been the Crown's position. Ysabet agreed.

Miren had said little. When the others rose she remained at the end of the table.

"You have stopped asking him to be Corven on paper," she said.

"I cannot compel three foreign governments to believe him."

"That is not what I said."

Luceran looked toward the doorway through which Jalen had gone. The king had ordered a man's death and accepted another man's service in the corpse. He had hoped a body that could speak would end an argument. Instead he had given the argument a widow, a witness book and three foreign jurisdictions.

"I know," he said.

Miren did not offer him her hand. She asked whether Leora had been told the truth. He answered that Yara's warnings were still being carried and that the Crown had not confessed the murder.

"Then your next letter will be harder than this one," she said.

At the lower gate Pel Ordan checked a messenger's pass beside the second living guard. Iven Marrick remained at his chosen threshold, not in either man's place. Below the wine cellar, Pell's principal braces held a passage nobody had been authorized to enter again. The unfinished roof kept six sacks dry; another six waited across the river. The foreign letters went out with separate seals and paid riders.

Leora's private question was still on the road.

In the family nave, all seven candles burned.
