# Season Two, Episode 28 — The Body Abroad

The horse had learned to dislike Jalen Orris before Jalen learned to dislike the saddle.

It was a patient bay, borrowed from a household that kept better animals for messengers. At every halt Jalen needed a living man's help to lower himself without twisting Corven Sareth's injured knee. He had been a foot soldier. He remembered wet leather and a gate he could not hold, but not the proper way to make a horse stand for a man whose leg hurt before his weight reached the ground.

Yara Fen had offered him the covered cart. He refused it at first because the escort would have to walk beside him and lose time. By the second afternoon he accepted. He was no longer certain that being brave and being useful were the same thing.

The physician sent with them from Edselsto checked his knee while the cart wheels were being tightened. The man had been paid to attend the journey, not to certify that the person he accompanied was Corven Sareth. He said so twice when the escort captain asked whether Dols would accept his word.

"I can tell them he has a pulse," the physician said. "They can count it themselves."

Jalen watched the man's fingers leave his wrist.

"Did you know the soldier whose name I use?"

"No."

"Then you have told them what you know."

Yara had ridden the first mile with the three river-bench warrants against her chest beneath a dry cloak. She now kept them in a locked case under her own seat. The seals had not become the property of the Crown because she had traveled with its guards. She had counted the guards before departure and again at the roadside inn, and required each to sign or make a witnessed mark for his own hours. Rhyse's order said they were an escort, not jailers.

At the inn, a woman tending the fire recognized Corven's face from an earlier League visit. She began to ask whether the envoy would still settle a dispute about a miller's toll. Jalen opened his mouth and found that he could not remember a single word of it.

"He is not here to settle it," Yara said.

The woman looked from one of them to the other. "Then why have you brought him?"

"Because Dols has asked to see the man who is being presented in his name."

That was an answer the woman could repeat without being asked to believe it. She went back to the fire. Her own daughter had a fever, and she had no patience left for another court's mystery.

Before dawn Jalen asked Yara whether she intended to stand beside him when the reeve questioned him.

"I will stand beside the warrants," she said. "You may stand beside your own words."

He had hoped for more comfort than that. He was also grateful she had not offered him a borrowed life.

---

The Dols reeve had ordered the hearing held in the bridge court, not in the chamber used for military countersigns.

Its shutters opened on the western approach. The mason was still rebuilding the miller's garden wall below, and every cart crossing the near bank could be heard over the water. The reeve had placed his own watch captain at the door, his own clerk at the table, and a chair for the physician he had appointed. Savelin attended as the Crown's road witness. He brought no armed men into the hearing.

Yara laid down the three warrants one at a time. She allowed the clerk to inspect their seals without surrendering the originals.

"Which of these authorizes this man to speak for Dols?" the reeve asked.

"None of them has been transferred to him," she said. "Corven Sareth was appointed to recommend. He could not issue your countersign even when he was alive."

The reeve noticed the tense. He did not interrupt.

Jalen rose to answer. The knee delayed him. A living guard moved a chair within reach, and Jalen took it with an embarrassed nod.

"What name shall the clerk enter?" the reeve asked.

"Jalen Orris."

"That is not the name in the commission."

"No."

"Are you the man who was appointed?"

"No."

"Are you the man whose face we see?"

Jalen considered the question longer than the clerk liked. "I am wearing his body. I did not know him before he died."

The clerk's pen stopped. The watch captain glanced at the door, where the escort had remained outside. The reeve did not ask for a sorcerer. He asked what Jalen could actually testify to.

"I remember dying at a gate during the coup," Jalen said. "I remember a voice asking whether I would help the northern soldiers. I agreed to try. I woke on a stone table with this knee and this face. I did not see Corven die. I cannot give you his memories."

"Who told you he was dead?"

"The king told me the body was dead. Others were present. I cannot swear to what they saw."

Yara watched the reeve enter that distinction. She had not witnessed the mortuary. Jalen's account could not make her a witness to it.

"Will you swear that you are Corven Sareth?" the reeve asked.

"No."

"Will you swear that you are Jalen Orris?"

"I can swear that I remember being Jalen. If you ask whether I understand how I stand here, I do not."

The reeve accepted that as testimony by a person of disputed identity, not a diplomatic recommendation. He ordered the record to say so. The Dols watch captain could still stop the next civilian grain wagon if the repaired wall failed inspection. Neither the man's confession nor his face altered that authority.

The reeve turned to the physician appointed by his own council.

"Examine what is before us. Do not examine a story someone wishes to be true."

---

Master Isera, the Dols physician, insisted upon a room with a door and a woman from the bridge infirmary to witness her instruments and the patient's consent. She did not want six councillors leaning over a body as though they could see a verdict in it.

Jalen agreed to the examination after asking whether it would hurt.

"Some of it may," Isera said. "You may stop me. If you do, I shall write what I could not inspect."

She examined the knee first. It was an old injury, with a stiff joint and a muscle weakened by years of compensating for it. Jalen flinched at a movement he had not anticipated. She examined his hands, his teeth, the bruising on his shoulder, and the pulse at his wrist. He breathed without prompting. His skin was warm. Those were facts.

The Crown physician produced his earlier note, made at Edselsto after the reported recovery. It described pulse, breath, bruising and the injured knee. It did not describe the man's back. Isera asked why.

"I was called to attend a patient," he said. "He was exhausted and in pain. I did not turn him fully. I wrote what I saw."

"Then you did not see it," she said, without accusation.

Jalen allowed her to look now. Beneath the shoulder blades and along the lower part of his back were broad, dark patches. They did not lie where the shoulder bruises lay. There was no swelling beneath them. Isera pressed a thumb against one and waited for the color to change. It changed less than she expected of a fresh bruise.

The infirmary woman drew the curtain a little wider. The Crown physician came close enough to see, but did not touch.

"What is it?" Jalen asked.

"I have seen marks like these on bodies laid out after death," Isera said. "Blood settles where a body has rested. It can leave a pattern unlike a blow. I have not before examined such a pattern on a man who speaks to me."

"Does that mean he died?" the Crown physician asked.

"It means I must not pretend to know the cause. Illness, pressure, injury and time may imitate one another. I would need the circumstances of his disappearance and the people who handled him. I can describe the marks. I cannot name the hand that made them, nor swear from a mark alone that life ended."

She made two drawings while the infirmary woman observed the position of Jalen's body. The first showed the patches as they appeared. The second marked where she had pressed and how little they changed. She recorded the room, the light, the date, the two physicians' separate observations and Jalen's permission. The Crown physician signed only that the marks had been present at this examination and absent from the portion of his earlier report because he had not inspected that portion of the body.

Jalen watched the drawings dry.

"Would a man with such marks be able to walk?"

"I have just watched you walk," Isera said.

"That isn't what I asked."

"No," she said. "It is not."

She offered him water and asked him to remain for another examination by a physician who had not seen the first drawings. She would not summon one in secret or hold him by force. He agreed to wait two nights if his knee permitted it. He wanted an answer, even if it was an answer he feared.

Outside the door, Yara heard the infirmary woman request a fresh piece of clean linen. The ordinary sound steadied her. Someone had finally examined the body without being told in advance what office it ought to hold.

---

The village watch clerk had been asked to keep too many things that people wished would disappear.

Corven's broken walking stick was still wrapped beside the original missing-person entry. The two attendants' accounts remained under their own dates. The wounded man was stronger, but his shoulder still would not carry a barrel. The cooper's widow had kept the room and food charges separate from his account of the attack. She wanted to be paid, not made a heroine.

Leora Sareth's new request reached the watch by an ordinary paid rider. Her housekeeper's witness mark and the steward's charge were entered on the outside of the packet. She asked that the original watch entries and the physician's first observations be preserved until a representative could inspect them. She did not ask the clerk to certify a death.

The clerk read it twice.

"She knows less than they say at the castle," the widow said.

"She knows what has been sent to her."

"That may be less."

"It may be."

The wounded attendant's companion came to see whether the wage claim had been answered. It had not. The clerk showed him the acknowledgment Jalen had permitted at Edselsto, which promised an examination of the claim but no payment from an account he did not control. The companion took it badly, then admitted that an honest unpaid debt was better than a false receipt.

They examined the watch book together. The original entry said Corven was missing after the lane attack. It did not say that the watch had seen him dead. The companion's later statement said the apparent Corven had failed to recognize him in the expected way. It did not say that a changed habit proved an impostor. The wounded man's own memory of the lane ended at a blow and shouting.

The widow had kept the key to the chamber Corven was meant to occupy. She showed the clerk the old rent entry and the unused bedding. She could prove that the envoy had not slept there after the attack. She could not prove where he had been taken.

The clerk copied the three records Leora had requested, leaving the originals under the watch seal. He marked each correction made by its own witness. He refused the companion's suggestion that he add the words *probably murdered* to the first page.

"If we learn that," he said, "we shall enter when and how we learned it. We shall not put tomorrow's knowledge into yesterday's mouth."

He sent an acknowledgment to Leora's household by the same rider who had brought her request, and made a second, independently sealed packet for the Dols watch captain. It contained the original missing-person wording, the dates of the two attendant accounts and the widow's room entry. The stick remained in the village. The injured man's unpaid lodging remained a claim against living people.

No one at the watch house knew what Ysabet had seen beneath Edselsto.

---

Leora received the answer to her private question while her mother was asleep.

The courier had been delayed by a swollen ford and a lame horse. He arrived with the witnessed copy of Jalen's refusal, not with the original question, which remained in Ysabet's custody. He could attest to the seal on the outgoing packet and to the words he had heard at Edselsto. He could not attest that the face he had seen belonged to Leora's husband.

Leora gave him a place by the kitchen fire while her steward compared the seals and entered the time of delivery. She read the answer standing up.

*I cannot answer her. I do not remember.*

There was more: the courier's description of the room, Ysabet's record, Yara's presence, the refusal to sign Corven's name. The words were careful enough to leave a man alive in one possible explanation and dead in another. Leora read them again. She had asked about the wet cloak and the crooked peg because Corven had made a joke of it for eighteen winters. He called the peg the Archbishop, because it leaned over everyone who entered the passage. Her mother had scolded him for teaching the servants the name.

A man could forget that after an injury. A man could forget a wife. She knew it. What frightened her was that the reply did not even try to claim the marriage.

Her niece was repairing the bark roof over the ribbon horse beneath the table. One piece kept falling. Leora knelt to hold it while the child tied it with thread.

"Will Uncle Corven know the horse?" the girl asked.

"I don't know."

"Then I'll show him."

Leora looked at the little structure and thought of a man who had once spent an hour arguing that the horse should have a better stable than the king's own.

Her mother woke and asked whether news had come.

"Yes."

"Is it good?"

"It is an answer."

She did not read it aloud until the girl had gone outside with the housekeeper. Then she told her mother what Corven had called the peg, and why. Her mother laughed once, painfully, before coughing into her cloth.

"He was insufferable about that peg," she said.

"He was."

Leora locked the return copy beside the original letters and had the steward witness its custody. She sent no proclamation. She prepared a second instruction for her representative: seek the village originals and the first physician's actual notes, ask who examined the returned man, and distinguish what they saw from what they were told. The representative would travel when the road and her mother's care allowed. She could not be in three places at once, and she would not let urgency make her abandon a sick woman.

The separate village-watch copy of the private exchange had taken another road. Leora did not know whether it had arrived. She would not pretend all the papers were already gathered because her own had finally reached her.

---

At Dols the reeve read Isera's examination account without asking her to use the word *resurrection*.

He asked the Crown physician whether he disputed what she had seen. The man said no. He disputed only that the marks proved a particular cause. Isera agreed with him. Their disagreement, to the reeve's surprise, was narrower than their employers might wish.

Yara placed the warrants before the council once more. She asked that their custody be entered separately from the examination. A man could have every bodily mark of Corven Sareth and still lack the authority of the three benches. A man could lack his memories because of injury and still be entitled to humane care. The two questions were not the same.

"Then what are we being asked to decide?" the reeve said.

"Whether you will allow someone else to answer them for you," Yara replied.

The reeve did not. He recorded that the visitor calling himself Jalen Orris had appeared voluntarily, declined Corven's name and commission, and permitted a physical examination. The Dols physician had observed markings she associated with bodies after death, on a person with breath and pulse. The cause was unresolved. He ordered a second independent medical examination if Jalen continued to consent, and requested the original Edselsto physician's account, the village watch's source records and the names of those who had custody of the body after the lane attack.

The word *body* troubled the clerk. He asked whether he should write *patient* instead.

"Write both where they are warranted," the reeve said. "Do not let a courteous word settle a question."

The watch captain proposed keeping Jalen inside the bridge court until the other physician came. Rhyse's escort officer objected that his orders did not permit detention. Jalen, who had listened through the argument, said he would remain by choice if he could leave for the road when he wished. The reeve accepted that and placed the lodging charge against Dols's own hearing account. The Crown would not purchase the witness's bed.

Savelin brought news that the miller's wall still lacked final acceptance. The mason had uncovered soft earth under the second course and needed a new footing stone. The reeve authorized the miller to inspect the proposed correction. The second civilian wagon could not present itself yet. No military countersign was issued.

"Your northern men have eaten six sacks," the reeve told Savelin. "That was worth doing. It does not make my bridge a royal road."

"I have never asked you to write that it did," Savelin said.

The reeve signed three separate letters. Edselsto would receive the hearing record and request for original testimony. Merehaven would receive notice that Dols had not recognized the apparent envoy's authority and had ordered further examination; no claim was made about its credit. Teth would receive the same limited jurisdictional notice without any instruction concerning its ferries. Each letter named its own clerk, seal, route and intended recipient. None purported to bind the other benches.

Yara would not carry them all herself. She had learned what happened when one person was asked to become the only road by which truth traveled.

---

At Edselsto, Luceran found an empty chair at the supper table.

It was the place where Corven had once sat to argue about grain, and where Jalen had later sat without knowing the argument. A servant had left the chair because no one had ordered it moved. Luceran could have done so with a word. He did not.

Damaris came after the meal with the receiving-yard account. The roof's exposed edge was finished, the guttering was not. The smith had been paid. Six sacks had reached the northern posts; six remained dry at Edselsto. The Dols wall repair was still unaccepted. Merehaven had released no second advance. The eighteen patrol mounts remained on their other duty, and the northern forage requests had not ceased.

"The men were fed," Luceran said.

"For a little while."

"Do you begrudge even that?"

"No. I am asking what pays for the next journey."

He looked at her figures and signed only the wages already earned. He had wanted a week in which a genuine achievement might stand without another demand. Damaris had no such week to give him.

Rhyse entered with the escort's last departure account. No message had yet returned from Dols. He had instructed the guards to protect Jalen on the road, not to keep him in Corven's role. Luceran asked whether the man could be recalled.

"He can be asked," Rhyse said. "If he is not under arrest, he cannot be ordered to come home as a prisoner."

Luceran said nothing. He had once believed the difficulty was making the dead obey. The greater difficulty was that a man who had agreed to serve might still wish to be treated as a man.

Ysabet arrived to collect the signed wage order. She still held the original private instruction that had caused Corven's death. She did not bring it into the supper room. Luceran knew it existed without seeing it.

Miren passed him in the gallery on her way to the physically deaf room. He told her Damaris's news about the grain. She said she was glad the northern soldiers had eaten. She did not ask whether Dols would be kind to the man in Corven's body.

"Do you think they will understand him?" Luceran asked.

"I think they will ask him questions you cannot answer for him."

He wanted to say that the kingdom had needed what he had done. The words were ready. They had grown easier to use every time a sack was counted or a wage paid. Miren watched him choose not to say them.

"Good night," she said.

He did not follow her into the room where the castle could not listen.

Far down the road, a Dols messenger rode with Isera's sealed drawings and the reeve's independent account. Another messenger carried the narrower notice toward Merehaven; a third took Teth's river road. The village watch's copies were traveling toward Dols by their own route, and Leora's household was preparing an inspection that had not yet begun. None of the riders knew of the mortuary command. Each carried something that could be examined without believing a king.

The next morning the miller's wall would still need stone. Jalen would still have to decide whether to submit to another physician. Luceran would still be responsible for the death that had made the examination necessary.

In Edselsto's family nave, all seven candles burned.
