# Season Two, Episode 14 — A Human Solution

The first Tervain wagon arrived at Edselsto with its rear wheel bound in wet rope.

The driver had brought twelve sacks from the lower barns, two hired horses, and a receipt bearing the estate steward's seal. He had not brought the rest of the purchase. Two more wagons were somewhere behind him on the hill, and the third team had lost an iron shoe before dawn.

Hesta Vale stood beside the receiving-yard scale while the clerk checked the seals. The timber that ought to have covered the entire yard had been divided into two piles: straight lengths for the roof, shorter rough stock intended for the bridge approach at Dols. She knew why. She also knew that the king's need had displaced two days of quarry hauling for which her family had paid men and horses.

"Put the Crown sacks under the finished bay," she told the clerk. "Not under the bare rafters. The rain is coming across the ridge."

The clerk looked toward the master carpenter, who was arguing with a tiler over the last run of battens.

"That bay was promised for stone."

"Then put the stone in the rain. It has been wet before."

Her brother, who had come to inspect the smaller blocks ordered for the League barges, took her aside. He thought the Crown's hiring payment would cover the missed haulage. Hesta asked him to count the lost days again, this time including the porter who had waited without a wagon and the oxen that still had to be fed.

"His Majesty has paid for the carts," her brother said.

"He has paid their owners. That is not the same as making everyone whole."

She sent Damaris a corrected account without asking the Crown to surrender the track right already promised for four seasons. She was not going to sell the same right twice, nor pretend the disruption cost nothing. At the scale, she made the driver watch while the clerk entered twelve sacks, not the thirty-six written on the purchase note.

A young stablehand asked whether the grain would feed the northern horses.

"Some of the men first," Hesta said. "The horses that are left will eat what can be carried after them."

The stablehand knew two of the mounts already sent south. He had helped break one of them. Hesta had no comforting answer for him. She gave the driver his receipt and sent a boy to watch for the delayed wagons.

By evening the Crown had taken possession of its first twelve sacks at Edselsto. The northern post was still days and roads away.

---

At Dols, the bridge council had arranged its benches so that the public could hear the argument and the river could not be ignored.

The water had fallen a little since Savelin's survey, but the western bank still opened under a probing staff. The reeve placed the measured sketch on the table, weighted with a stone. The carpenter had marked the proposed stakes in red. Savelin stood behind the visitors' rail, not beside the councillors.

The watch captain asked who would stop a wagon if a shored bank failed.

"I will," said the reeve.

"You will be at the east toll."

"Then I will name a man at the western turn."

"And if the king sends six wagons at once?"

"Then the sixth waits."

Savelin accepted that before the reeve looked at him. He had brought no troop orders, only the Crown's offer to provide timber and pay the bridge carpenter for the work. The councillors had also received Corven Sareth's letter. It recommended a limited civilian grain passage; it did not purport to command the council.

One member wanted the Crown to pay for a permanent stone approach before a single cart passed. Another wanted to reject the request entirely. The carpenter, who had spent the previous winter repairing the lower steps with his own hands, thought either answer would leave the bank worse when spring came.

"Timber will hold a season if it is set where I have drawn it," he said. "It will not hold a cavalry column, and it will not hold a cart whose driver refuses to wait."

Savelin said, "Then put the waiting in the warrant."

The council granted permission to prepare the civilian approach at Crown expense under the reeve's inspection. It agreed in principle to one daylight cart interval after the carpenter and watch captain had witnessed the shoring and marked the load limit. The permission would lapse if the bank moved or the river rose past the cut stone on the western pier. No grain wagon could use it yet. No troop, armed escort, or military supply column received a countersign.

The clerk read the conditions twice. The watch captain insisted that the word civilian appear beside the cart interval, not merely in the heading.

Outside, the ferryman held up his repaired hook for Savelin to see.

"Will it take your army now?" he asked.

"No," Savelin said. "It may take a sack of grain when your council says the bank is ready."

The ferryman grinned. "Then the council has learned something from the river."

Savelin carried away a narrow permission and a large bill for timber. It was more than he had possessed that morning, and considerably less than his king required.

---

Yara Fen had not expected the two Teth boatwrights to agree.

They stood over a ferry hull in a covered shed, one pointing to the cracked gunwale, the other to the ironwork beneath the bow. The ferry owner, Mistress Calven, had brought her own ledger and refused to let either man call her boat worn out merely because a foreign crown might one day need it.

"If you value the hull at what it fetched ten years ago," she said, "you must value my lost trade at ten years ago too. I have passengers now that I did not have then."

Corven had authorized Yara to observe the proposed valuation, not to promise requisition. Teth's bench had sent its own clerk. There was no Crown warrant to seize a ferry, and nobody in the shed had authority to issue one.

The younger boatwright gave the hull a lower value because its planking would soon need replacement. The older man counted the serviceable iron and the owner's recent repairs. Neither calculation included the wages of the crew or the loss of tolls during an emergency taking.

"Then there are two questions," Yara said. "What is the boat worth, and what does it cost the owner to be without it?"

Mistress Calven closed her ledger on one finger. "Three questions. Who brings it back?"

The Teth clerk wrote that down.

By the afternoon they had a form of account rather than an agreed sum: two independent assessments, the owner's own reckoning, a separate entry for crew and lost earnings, and a right to challenge the figures before the river bench. A ferry returned damaged would be inspected again. The bench would still decide whether any emergency seizure could be lawful. The Crown had agreed to no particular payment because no seizure had been authorized.

Yara sent the account to Corven and a copy to the Teth clerk. She added the disclosure he had required: her brother's warehouse on the lower river might receive return stone if Hesta's carriage plan went forward. She had an interest in the trade and no claim to speak for the League's public officers.

Mistress Calven watched her seal the letter.

"Your brother owns a warehouse?"

"A damaged one."

"Then he will know the price of having a thing that cannot work."

Yara thought of the flood line still marked on her brother's wall. She would have preferred not to be understood so precisely.

Merehaven's questions arrived on clean paper, with the small seal of its store bench impressed through blue wax.

The bench would consider a first civilian cargo only if the grain had a roof, the sacks could be counted by a witness it trusted, and money stood ready against spoilage or an unlawful delay. Its factor proposed a bond large enough to consume most of the coin Damaris had set aside for the next stage of repairs.

Damaris laid the letter beside the yard accounts. The completed roof bay could hold the first twelve sacks and more besides. It could not shelter an entire winter convoy. She refused to certify a roof that had not been finished.

Corven sat opposite her, easing his bad knee beneath the table. Yara had come with the Teth clerk's valuation form and the private carriage agreement. Luceran had asked for a practical meeting before he met Corven again in council.

"Your bench wants security," Damaris said. "So do my carters. I can bind coin for the first limited cargo, witnessed when the roof is inspected. I cannot pledge a season's supply against a building that is still open to rain."

"That is not a proposal my bench can reject for being dishonest," Corven said.

"It can reject it for being too small."

"It may."

They worked through the cargo by the sack rather than by the grand name of a treaty. A Merehaven witness could count the first lot under the finished bay; a second lot would wait until more roof was accepted. Damaris would not pay the bond before the relevant bench consent, the Dols shoring inspection and the Teth conditions were known. Corven could recommend this limited undertaking, but he could not pledge three river councils to it.

A messenger from Jorvan arrived before they finished. The lender had received Damaris's one-gate counteroffer and would consider a smaller advance, but wanted a second surety or a higher fee for the short term. The letter enclosed no coin. Damaris put it aside for Tomas to price against the gate repairs.

"That is a lender who knows you are cold," Corven observed.

"And a minister who knows that a gate without hinges collects very little," Damaris answered.

Yara showed them Hesta's signed private agreement. Corven added a note requiring the disclosed warehouse interest to accompany any petition to the benches. Merehaven could refuse the proposed return cargo without accusing Yara of having hidden it.

When they parted, Corven agreed to send the narrower first-cargo terms to Merehaven with his recommendation. He did not call it consent. Damaris did not call it money. They had found a way to ask a smaller question that the bench might answer.

Nessa Rell left her measured drawing on Pell Falbrecht's table with a stone on each corner.

"The opening is still unsafe," she said.

Pell did not dispute it. He had secured the timber for the first safe-side brace, but the remaining lengths were still promised to the receiving-yard roof and now the Dols approach. The brace could be fitted when the carpenter released his men. It would not authorize anyone to pass beneath the lintel without a fresh inspection.

Talla Rusk arrived carrying a shallow wooden tray full of mud scraped from the cellar drain.

"Your explorers left me a fine new shutter," she said. "They also left the water nowhere to go."

Vess had offered to have the drain cleared before another inspection. Talla wanted the casks moved out of the splash line as well, with their count witnessed. Her concern was not a theory about ancient architecture. Spoiled wine would be charged to a living keeper, not to a drawing of a forgotten gallery.

The injured worker's physician had allowed him light walking but not stone carrying. Pell entered that restriction beside the wages due. He did not call a man recovered because a stair could be climbed.

Nessa needed to leave for the mill foundation by the next morning. Vess offered to retain her for two days at Crown expense, but she had promised the miller an answer before frost and would not sell the same days twice. They agreed that a messenger could summon her with three days' notice once the brace, drain and cask protections were ready. She would be paid for the return journey and the survey, not for waiting indefinitely in a castle corridor.

Before she departed, she pointed to the openings drawn on the lower section.

"They face inward," Vess said.

"They face the passage we measured," she corrected. "We have not measured what lies beyond it."

He accepted the correction. There would be no second descent this week. The shutter remained fitted, the cellar remained in use, and the old stone kept its unanswered purpose.

Corven finished his letter to Leora before Rhyse came for him.

He had written about the boatwrights, because she liked disputes in which everyone was right about something different. He had not written about the guards outside his chamber. She would have asked what he feared, and he still did not know how to answer.

Rhyse waited while Corven folded the paper.

"Your men have changed the meal watch," Corven said.

"Yes."

"Is that because you expect a knife?"

"I have no report of another knife."

"You have learned to answer the words I do not use."

Rhyse accepted the rebuke. He had carried Corven's demand for warning to the king. Luceran had neither authorized disclosure of the sealed proposal nor forbidden Rhyse from asking again. That narrow silence was becoming intolerable.

"I am asking His Majesty for a decision before your next private audience," Rhyse said. "I will not call ignorance protection merely because it spares me an oath dispute."

Corven watched him a moment. "If I leave, the benches lose a voice. If I stay, my wife expects a living husband. I would like to make that choice with whatever knowledge you believe I lack."

"I understand."

"Then understand also that I will not thank you for a warning delivered after it could matter."

Rhyse went to Ysabet before the audience. She had kept the Episode Eleven record under seal and had witnessed Luceran refuse the proposal. She would not pretend that the refusal made the record harmless.

"If you tell him without the king's leave," she said, "you may break an oath. If we keep him ignorant, we make his hospitality a trap he cannot judge."

"Will you ask Luceran with me?"

"Yes. But not in the connected gallery."

They sought a private royal hearing in the south-gallery withdrawing room. Neither had disclosed the proposal to Corven, Yara or Damaris. They had, however, ceased treating the decision to withhold it as something that could remain undecided forever.

---

Luceran had wanted a signed treaty to lay beside the northern ration report. What he received was a council's narrow permission to shore a bridge, a disputed ferry account, a smaller bond proposal awaiting another bench, and twelve sacks of grain under a roof that was not finished.

He read the papers with Damaris and Savelin. The bridge timber would come from the rough stock first; the carpenter would not strip the roof of its accepted beams. The first Tervain collection would continue as the hired teams returned, with each load counted at departure and arrival. Damaris's next payment would cover carters and the displaced quarry porter before she offered Jorvan another security. No northern patrol mount was recalled. The two hamlets had received notice of their reduced circuit, and a foot party had reached only the nearer one before dark.

"This is not enough," Luceran said.

"No," Damaris answered. "It is also not nothing."

Corven entered with Yara and gave his account without pretending he had won the benches. Dols had agreed to a repair and a conditional civilian interval. Teth had a defensible way to value an emergency taking but had granted no seizure right. Merehaven had a smaller proposal to consider. His commission still lacked the military countersign the Crown wanted.

"You could return to your benches and argue for us," Luceran said.

"I intend to. I cannot make them my property by loving the argument."

Luceran nearly smiled. Corven's stubbornness was not a pose. It was the reason a recommendation from him might carry weight.

Afterward, Rhyse and Ysabet asked for the private hearing. Miren was with Luceran in the withdrawing room, looking at a loose thread on the cuff of his coat. She withdrew her hand when she saw their faces, but she did not leave.

Rhyse repeated Corven's request. Ysabet said that the record of the castle's proposal was sealed against casual use, not against the moral consequences of silence. The king could decide what to tell his guest. He could not honestly say the guest had been allowed to judge a danger nobody had named.

Luceran looked at the door. He remembered Edselsto offering him a man who could never say no. Corven, standing alive in the hall beyond, had spent the day saying no in several different and useful ways.

"I will speak with him," Luceran said. "Not in the gallery. I will tell him that an unwelcome proposal concerning his life was made and refused, and that its means have never been proved. I will not let the castle's claim become a charge against him."

"Will you tell him the whole proposal?" Miren asked.

"Not tonight. I need to decide how much can be said without turning a monstrous suggestion into a threat spoken by the Crown itself."

She did not approve the delay. Rhyse did not mistake the promise for a completed warning. Ysabet wrote down the undertaking and its deadline: before the next private audience, not at the king's convenience.

Luceran asked Miren to stay after they left. She did, and rested her hand against his neck. He did not ask whether that meant forgiveness.

In the family nave, the seven candles continued to burn. Beneath them the kingdom's most difficult work was still being done by people who could refuse one another, and who might yet find reasons to agree.
