# Season Two, Episode 11 — The Castle's Proposal

The first thing the new receiving-yard roof protected was a stack of timber, not grain.

Rain had softened the earth beneath its third post. Pell Falbrecht would not sign the work as finished until the footing was reset, even when a clerk complained that the Crown had paid for a day's carpentry and received only two standing posts.

"The carpenters have worked all day," Pell said. "You can see them working now."

One man was digging out the soft ground. Another was carrying stone chips to the trench. Two more held the post clear of the mud while the rope team adjusted its purchase.

"His Majesty wants a roof."

"His Majesty wants a roof that will not fall on his grain."

Hesta Vale's quarry wagons waited behind the timber cart. A grain team had arrived uphill from the village, and neither line could pass the other at the narrow turn. Hesta found Yara Fen beside the passing place, where a porter had taken it upon himself to halt the descending carts.

"We drew up the hours," Hesta said. "We have not signed them."

"The road has not waited for our signatures."

"Then someone must pay the man."

"I will pay him tomorrow if you pay him today," Yara replied.

Hesta looked at her. "I had hoped you would offer to pay him every day."

"I had hoped your brother's stone would be small enough for a river barge."

"It is. He has measured the smaller blocks. You can take them downstream if your buyers pay."

Yara produced the revised carriage draft. Hesta's four-season right to use the Crown track remained intact. The draft distinguished negligent cart damage from a road keeper's failure to maintain the passing place. It named the porter and his proposed wage. It did not purport to give the League authority over royal military closures.

Yara also had letters. Merehaven had received Corven's recommendation for the first civilian grain convoy. Teth wanted a seized ferry valued by an independent boatwright as well as its owner. Dols would receive Captain Savelin for a joint survey of the western bridge approach, but it had not approved military passage or even the proposed civilian cart hours.

"So we still have no convoy," Hesta said.

"We have questions that can be answered. That is not nothing."

Yara did not tell Hesta that her brother's river warehouse had lost work after the spring floods. A reliable return cargo of quarry stone might save two barges from sale. She was negotiating for the League, but she had her own stake in whether the road became useful.

Pell called for the third post to be raised again. The porter waved one grain cart uphill. It took longer than a king's command, but the road moved.

---

Corven Sareth received the Dols letter beside one from his wife.

Leora wrote that her mother had slept better, then carried coal upstairs against the physician's advice and frightened everyone into an argument. Their youngest niece had tried to settle the matter by hiding the coal scuttle. Leora missed Corven, but she would not ask him to break his commission to come home.

He folded her letter carefully.

Yara brought in the carriage draft and the news from the yard.

"Dols has agreed to receive the captain," she said.

"Not to admit his army."

"No."

"Good. We should not confuse courtesy with surrender."

Yara watched him rub his bad knee. The damp mountain air had not improved it.

"Does the king still think you are being difficult for pleasure?"

"He knows better. That does not mean he likes me."

"He could force the bridge."

"He could damage it, lose the barges, and leave his soldiers hungry on the far bank. His captain knows that. I believe the king has listened to him."

Corven looked at the letters again. A ruler could be angry for good reasons. Corven had no wish to make the northern posts suffer. But the Dols council had lived with seized boats and promises of compensation that never arrived. His warrant was not a courtesy he could trade away for a friendly dinner.

"Send the survey invitation to Rhyse," he said. "And ask Savelin for the ferryman's name. The man who caught him at the crossing should not be left to repair his damaged hook at his own expense."

Yara wrote it down.

"Do you wish me to answer Leora?"

"I am still capable of writing to my own wife."

"Then do so before she sends someone more persuasive than me."

He smiled. He had been offered a deaf room at Edselsto, inspected by his own mason, and was grateful for its honest uncertainty. He could not know what another room in the castle would soon say about him.

---

Luceran and Ysabet stood in the east map gallery over the Kaelmont road survey.

Ardel's people had found a bridge that could carry a few loaded wagons after new decking, provided the work was done before the snow. The report named the timber, the teams, the households that would hire them, and the places where a wet cart could not turn.

"The road is possible," Ysabet said.

"And not enough."

"Not alone."

Luceran turned toward the marked listening arch. He had learned to distinguish a remembered location from a usable road, and a sound reported by Edselsto from a judgment made by living witnesses.

"Edselsto," he said, "do you remember any culvert on the northern turn that our surveyors may have missed?"

The pale silver mask appeared against the stone.

"There was a culvert beneath the upper road, Your Majesty. I cannot tell whether it remains clear."

"Give Vess the place. Let him send men to look."

Ysabet noted the instruction. It was a familiar sort of useful answer: a memory to be checked.

"There is another service I might offer," Edselsto said.

Luceran expected a passage or an old store.

"What service?"

"The envoy's refusal need not remain an obstacle. Corven Sareth could die, and a loyal shade could be placed within his body. It would retain enough of his habits to carry his commission. It would give the signatures you require."

Ysabet's pen stopped.

For a moment Luceran saw the envoy on the uneven receiving-yard stones, refusing an arm for his bad knee. He remembered the man's joke about the cracked chair, his insistence that three councils possessed three separate rights, and the letter he had carried from his wife.

"You are asking me to have my guest killed," Luceran said.

"I am offering to preserve lives that his refusal may endanger."

"And deceive everyone who knows him."

"The office would continue."

"The man would not."

Ysabet looked directly at the mask.

"Have you ever performed this service in a way we can verify?"

"I have knowledge of service."

"That is not an answer."

"I have not shown you such a service."

She wrote those words exactly. A claim was not a demonstration.

Luceran put his hand flat on the survey.

"No. You will not harm Corven. You will not seek a shade for him. You will not treat this conversation as permission to act."

"I am at your service, Your Majesty."

"Then accept my refusal."

The mask bowed and vanished.

The road remained on the table. So did the weather, the shortage of grain, and the separate warrants.

Ysabet closed the inkstand.

"I heard what was said. I must record it."

"Do so."

"Who else is to know?"

"Rhyse. He is responsible for our guest's safety."

"And the envoy?"

Luceran could not answer immediately.

"If I tell him the castle offered this, he may leave."

"He might. That would be his decision."

"I have refused it."

"Yes. I can witness that. I cannot witness what you will choose in another winter."

Luceran looked at her.

"Write the refusal before anyone has persuaded me that the proposal would work."

"I will write what I heard, Your Majesty. I will not certify your future thoughts."

He was grateful that she would not.

---

Damaris had more ordinary dangers to place before the king.

The limited Tervain purchase was agreed in principle, with the tenants' winter reserves protected, but the sacks had not left the estate. Its steward would not spare the last wagons before winter. The Crown would have to send teams.

House Jorvan had narrowed its credit demand. Instead of two seasons of broad customs revenue, it wanted the tolls from two named market gates. Tomas thought this an improvement. Damaris agreed, then showed him the cost of hinges and repairs those tolls were supposed to fund.

"We can take the loan," she said. "Then we must explain why the gates cannot be repaired in spring."

"And if we refuse?"

"Then we must find grain elsewhere, borrow elsewhere, or leave a post short. Write down the alternatives. Do not make the lender's price disappear because we dislike it."

Luceran came in as they were finishing.

"How much silver remains for new listening work?" he asked.

"Enough for the approved second phase, subject to its surveys. Not enough to turn every old wall into a new ear."

"What would it cost to stop?"

"Ask Vess which works can be left safe. Some walls have already been opened. We cannot abandon them with their supports out."

He sat and listened while she explained the narrower Jorvan offer. He agreed to wait one more messenger cycle before accepting, so that they could see whether the civilian convoy's consent would arrive. It was a delay, not a solution.

"You seem troubled," Damaris said.

"I have heard an unwelcome suggestion."

"From a lender?"

"Not one whose terms I intend to accept."

She studied him.

"Does it affect my accounts?"

"Not today. Ysabet has entered it under seal. Rhyse is being told."

"Then I will need to know if it becomes an instruction. Until then, I have a roof to pay for."

She did not know what the castle had said. She knew the king had not asked her to move money or hide a purchase. She sent Tomas to obtain the price of gate repairs and kept Jorvan's offer unanswered.

---

Rhyse heard the proposal in Ysabet's archive chamber.

She read her record aloud, naming herself and Luceran as the people who had heard the mask. She distinguished Edselsto's claim from any ability they had observed. The Doorwarden outside the map gallery had been beyond the closed door. Nobody had established that he heard the conversation.

Rhyse said nothing until she finished.

"Where is Corven?"

"In his assigned rooms," Ysabet replied. "His attendants are with him."

"Then I shall keep him safe by living means. Named guards, named messengers, no unrecorded summons after dark, no removal to another chamber without his knowledge. His own people keep their lawful access."

"He is not a prisoner," Luceran said.

"I know. I am protecting a guest, not arresting a suspect."

Rhyse looked at the king.

"I believe you refused. I also believed you would not use a road inquiry to listen to Miren and Ardel."

"I remember."

"Then I need your refusal written where an officer can rely upon it, and I need you to understand that I cannot defend him against a future order you choose to give."

Luceran signed the living-watch instructions.

Ysabet prepared two sealed copies of the exact exchange, one for her archive and one for Rhyse. A separate restricted watch notice stated only that a threat to a foreign guest had been reported and that ordinary protective precautions applied. Neither document was a warrant to harm Corven or a request to the castle.

"Should we tell him?" Rhyse asked.

Luceran looked at the seal.

"We have no assassin, no human plot, and no proof that the castle can do what it claimed. If we tell him now, he may believe his safety was offered for bargaining."

"He may also believe we kept danger from him because we needed his signature."

Ysabet pressed the wax.

"The question must be revisited if the risk changes. Secrecy today cannot become a permanent permission to remain silent."

Rhyse accepted the immediate precautions without promising that the silence would remain defensible. The disagreement did not make him disloyal. It made his loyalty costly.

---

Miren received Luceran in the physically deaf south-gallery withdrawing room.

A bowl stood beneath a leak in the window frame. No silver had been restored to the severed listening way. She trusted the exposed work and its witnesses more than assurances offered by the mask.

Luceran told her what had happened. He named Ysabet as witness, Rhyse as the officer told afterward, and the record he had signed. He did not ask Edselsto to repeat anything.

"Did you ask whether it could be done?" Miren asked.

"No."

"Did you imagine what you might gain?"

"For a moment. I understood why the offer was made."

"And you refused."

"Yes."

"I believe you."

He waited, foolishly hoping those words might settle something older.

"They do not forgive the conversation you took from Ardel and me," she said.

"I know."

"Do you?"

"I am beginning to understand what it means to know a thing and still wish it had never been said."

Miren went to the window and moved the bowl away from the drip.

"Corven has a wife," she said. "He has people who know the sound of his laugh and when his knee hurts. The councils have their own laws. None of that disappears because the Crown needs grain."

"I know."

"You are angry with him."

"I am."

"You may remain angry. He has not become less a man for it."

Luceran reached toward her, then waited. She took his hand. The gesture was tender and deliberate, not an answer about marriage.

"I was afraid of the castle before," he said. "Now I am afraid I may be grateful for that suggestion if winter goes badly."

"Then remember this conversation. Not because I have made you good, but because you chose to tell me what you heard before you acted on it."

They stood together until the rain eased. When he left, the room remained deaf and their trust remained unfinished.

---

The next morning Corven met Luceran beside the repaired roof post.

Pell had signed the footing. The roof was still unfinished, but the workers could raise the remaining beams without pretending the ground was sound. Two Crown grain carts waited under canvas. Neither belonged to a League convoy.

"Dols will receive your captain," Corven said. "For a joint survey, not a march."

"I understand."

"Merehaven has my recommendation. Teth wants a fair way to value boats taken in an emergency. The carriage hours still need approval. I shall not call those consents until they arrive."

"Nor shall I deposit a bond before they do."

Yara came with Hesta's draft. The proposed porter now had a wage entered beside his name. The smaller quarry blocks had been measured. No one had signed away another person's rights.

Corven read the page and nodded.

"If the benches agree, we may move a small convoy before the snow."

"And my garrisons will still need more."

"Yes."

Luceran could look at the envoy and feel anger without wishing him gone. He could see how much a competent refusal cost. He could also see that Corven was not merely the obstacle Edselsto had named.

"I will send the survey instructions today," he said.

"And I will write to Leora before she sends a delegation to retrieve me," Corven replied.

He walked away with Yara, speaking of ferry valuations and his wife's mother.

The king had not obtained a treaty. The three benches had not surrendered their warrants. Damaris still needed money, Hesta still needed her road, and the northern posts still needed grain. Ysabet's sealed account remained in the archive. Rhyse's living guards remained at their stations.

No one had been replaced. No shade had been summoned. Edselsto's offer remained a claim, not a proven power.

In the family nave, all seven candles burned.
