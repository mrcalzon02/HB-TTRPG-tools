# Season Two, Episode 19 — Peace by Counterfeit

The first answer from Merehaven arrived four mornings after Yara had sent the river benches her warning.

It was addressed to the secretary, not the envoy. The factors would entertain a small first-cargo surety if the receiving roof was inspected and the sacks counted by witnesses chosen on both sides. No second advance was promised. No claim that Corven Sareth had recovered would satisfy those conditions.

Yara read the letter beside Damaris's grain tally. Twelve Tervain sacks stood beneath the completed roof bay. Six could travel on one hired wagon; the others would have to wait for money and a driver.

A second letter came from Teth. Its two boatwrights could begin valuation with ferry owners present and a right of challenge before any vessel was taken. It granted no right of seizure. Damaris put it beside, rather than beneath, Merehaven's offer.

"His Majesty will say the League has agreed," she said.

"Three benches have said three different things," Yara replied. "They are entitled to have them read separately."

A household clerk had prepared a reply beginning, *Upon the fortunate recovery of Envoy Sareth*. Yara struck out the sentence. She had seen a man who did not remember the terms Corven himself had insisted upon. She could not prove what had happened in the mortuary. She could prove that his warrants remained in her custody.

Damaris signed a factual account of the roof, the twelve sacks and the intended six-sack first load. Yara sent it without Corven's signature. She also sent the benches another notice that a living official's account was not an exercise of the envoy's commission.

The letter might cost her employment. She paid the messenger anyway.

---

At Dols, the miller's timber lip had held through the rain, but the earth behind it remained wet.

The reeve watched the carpenter drive a rod beside the rut left by the previous test. Savelin brought an empty wagon weighted with gravel to imitate a grain load. Its second wheel sank too far.

"Not today," the carpenter said.

The watch captain objected that he had already assigned men to clear the approach. The miller objected that a wider drainage cut would flood his lower floor. Savelin could have demanded another trial under the Crown's authority. He climbed down, saw the rod's mark and ordered the wagon back.

The reeve authorized a narrower cut and charged the extra day of labor to the Crown's existing shoring account. Savelin witnessed the entry. He did not sign it as a military crossing.

On the following morning the same wagon crossed and returned without widening the rut. The reeve granted one daylight interval for **one civilian grain wagon**, subject to the watch captain stopping it if the bank softened. The permission said nothing about soldiers or future traffic.

Savelin sent the signed measurements to Edselsto. He also wrote to the ferryman whose damaged hook he had repaid, asking him to watch the bank after the next rise. The ferryman agreed for the sake of his own boat and named a fair day's fee.

The bridge had become usable for one journey because living people had disagreed, measured, paid and tried again.

---

Bram could hold a spoon with his injured fingers, but he still could not grip the side of a loaded cart.

Hesta found him counting turns beside the receiving yard. He had marked the tally board with his good hand and was furious with the replacement porter for crossing out a number he had entered twice.

"You can count," she said. "You cannot lift. I have paid you to recover, and I intend to get what I paid for."

He almost laughed.

Pell Falbrecht inspected the completed roof bay and made the smith's apprentice drive home a loose nail before he marked it fit for the witness count. Damaris paid the apprentice's earned wages. The smith's remaining bill did not vanish with them.

Hesta's quarry work had lost another morning to Crown grain. Her brother had cut smaller blocks for possible downhill return carriage, but no League barge had agreed to take them. She showed Yara the cost of idle horses and the replacement porter's wages. Their signed private carriage agreement accounted for both; it did not pledge Teth's ferries or alter Hesta's four-season Crown-track right.

Six sacks were weighed and checked for dry seams. The other six remained beneath the roof. Merehaven's factor, who had travelled with the return messenger, tested the drip line and witnessed the count beside Damaris's clerk. He accepted the limited surety for those six sacks only.

The carter received his advance. Bram received his rest wages. The porter received his day's pay. Hesta's brother still had stone in his yard.

Yara thought of her own brother's flood-damaged warehouse. She had declared her interest to the benches; declaration had not made it a public contract. She refused Hesta's suggestion that the return cargo could be settled now.

"We can hope for a second journey," she said. "We cannot charge someone for one we have not been given."

---

Luceran had Jalen sit beside the council table when the three letters were read.

He did not ask the man in Corven's body to sign anything. The councillors saw the familiar face, the extended left leg and the borrowed clothes, and many seemed relieved. That relief was what Luceran wanted.

Damaris read Merehaven's limited surety. Rhyse read the Dols watch captain's power to stop the wagon. Ysabet read Teth's owner's right to challenge a ferry valuation.

"Then the League has yielded," a young household officer said.

"No," Damaris answered. "A factor has accepted one surety, a reeve has permitted one civilian interval, and two boatwrights may begin measuring. Those are three useful things. They are not a treaty."

Luceran let the correction stand. A false report could ruin the actual passage.

Jalen leaned toward him. "Are the men on the northern road still hungry?"

"Yes."

"Then why is everyone pleased?"

"Because some of the grain can move."

Jalen considered the letters. "Corven must have been patient."

Luceran could not decide whether it was praise or reproach. The dead soldier knew little of Leora, Yara or the three benches. His body knew how to fold a letter; he did not know why the letter mattered.

After the councillors left, Jalen asked again about his sister and the unpaid casualty roll. Luceran said a clerk was looking. It was true, though he had ordered the search only after being asked twice.

"If she is alive, may I see her?"

"I do not know."

Jalen accepted the answer. Luceran found himself grateful for the absence of argument, and was ashamed only when he noticed the gratitude.

---

The grain wagon reached Dols after sunrise on the appointed day. Its driver had stopped overnight rather than take a tired horse down the last slope in darkness.

The reeve, carpenter and watch captain checked the load: one civilian wagon, six sacks, no armed party claiming a military right. Savelin's men remained on the near bank. The watch captain walked ahead of the horse where the old rut had been.

The wheels pressed the gravel without breaking the new lip. On the far side a clerk counted six sacks, marked the carter's tally and directed them into a dry store pending a northern carrier.

The porter asked whether the return journey could take Hesta's smaller quarry blocks. The reeve said that was another load and another inspection. The porter complained, then accepted paid work stacking the sacks. The grain had crossed Dols. It had **not** reached the northern posts.

When the far-bank tally reached Edselsto, Damaris set it before Luceran.

"Six sacks," he said.

"Six beyond Dols. Six still here. No northern carrier hired."

Rhyse offered an order postponing an emergency forage seizure in two exposed hamlets for forty-eight hours. He would not recall the eighteen withdrawn mounts or promise full patrol coverage until fodder actually arrived. Luceran signed the narrow postponement. Two villages would keep their animals for two more days; the northern soldiers would still need feeding when those days ended.

A market crier praised the king's patience and the recovered envoy's cooperation. The smith's apprentice bought his mother a better loaf with his wages. The miller watched the river. The grain stood in a store short of its destination.

Relief was real enough to count. So was the distance between relief and peace.

---

Leora's reply arrived while Yara was preparing the next carriage account.

Her husband had been attacked; could he write in his own hand? Had a physician examined him? Why had Yara not named the wound? Leora's mother had worsened, and she could not travel immediately. At the bottom was a joke about Corven arguing with anyone who told him to rest.

Yara had heard a breathing man use his voice. She had seen him look to Luceran for answers Corven would have known. She had no evidence of the rite and would not pretend to possess it.

She wrote that a physician had recorded breath, pulse and injuries without certifying an ordinary recovery. She could not attest the man's memory or authority. Leora should not travel on the Crown's assurances alone. Yara enclosed a witnessed copy of the village watch entry and retained the three warrants.

Elsewhere, Beren Kalt's wife demanded that Rhyse explain why her husband could receive visitors but not leave. Rhyse allowed Beren to walk the outer yard with a named Doorwarden and send a letter outside the private courier service. He recorded the restriction that remained. Protection was not freedom.

At supper the household thanked Luceran for the first grain crossing. He named the reeve, carter, miller, Hesta, Damaris and the three benches. Then he thanked the recovered envoy for making cooperation possible.

Yara did not applaud. Jalen had settled no price, signed no warrant and approved no passage. His presence made the story easier to tell.

Miren left before the final course. Luceran found her outside the physically deaf room.

"The wagon crossed," he said.

"For six sacks."

"Two hamlets keep their animals."

"For two days."

He wanted her to say that it had been worth something. She knew that it had. That was not the same as saying Corven's death had been its price.

"He had already begun finding you a lawful road," she said. "You killed him while he was doing it."

"The household believes he helped."

"Then you are teaching the household a lie."

She went inside without asking him to follow.

Ysabet placed the far-bank tally beside the sealed private instruction without fastening them together. One recorded a public good. The other recorded the king's chosen crime. Both would survive.

In the connected gallery Edselsto offered to remember the new crossing arrangement. Luceran told it to remember only what the reeve had signed. It repeated the daylight interval accurately. It could not promise dry ground tomorrow, or tell him what Leora would do when she learned whose voice came from her husband's body.

In the family nave all seven candles burned.
