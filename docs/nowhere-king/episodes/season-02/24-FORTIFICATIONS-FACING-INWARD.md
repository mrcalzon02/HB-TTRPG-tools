# Season Two, Episode 24 — Fortifications Facing Inward

Talla Rusk poured a bucket of water across the cellar floor and watched it disappear through the repaired drain.

She did it twice.

Pell Falbrecht, who had come to ask permission to move the wine racks again, waited beside the new shutter. The shutter was oak, fitted into the opening behind the lower casks. Its iron latch had cost more than Talla thought reasonable. She had demanded it anyway.

"Is that sufficient?" he asked.

"For the water? Yes."

"For our men?"

"You haven't brought them down yet."

He put his hand against the shutter. It closed flush enough to keep the cellar air from drawing into the old passage. The nearest casks had been shifted away from the wall and their seals counted in Talla's book. She had insisted on the count before the first descent. The royal mason had now learned to ask for it before he touched anything.

"The lintel?" she asked.

"Braced from this side. We can work under it when the second prop is set."

"You said that before."

"I said I could not work under it before."

She disliked the distinction. It was nevertheless one she could verify by looking at the timber lying in the passage.

The man who had slipped on the old stair had been paid for his lost shift. Talla had seen the mark in the wages book, because she had made the clerk bring it. A bruised ankle was an ordinary enough injury until a man discovered that the Crown's account had no place for the hours he could not work.

"Are you taking him below again?"

"No. He's been put to sorting sound timber above ground until the physician clears him for carrying."

"Then you have learned something."

Pell considered telling her how much he had learned about bad stone. He decided she meant the man.

When she unlocked the shutter for him, she stood aside but did not leave. Her cellar had not become a passage merely because the king was curious.

---

Damaris found the second survey's account tucked beneath the receiving-yard roof bill.

It was a neat account. That made it more irritating.

Two days of wages were proposed, with six workers available by rotation, a surveyor's fee, replacement lamp oil, new timber for the lower brace and two Doorwardens. The men were not the same men whose pay had been entered against the roof. Pell had written their names and the work they would leave unfinished.

"Where are you taking the timber from?" she asked.

"The old store beside the south wall," Vess said. "Pell has sounded it. Nothing cut for the roof."

"Nothing cut for the roof," she repeated, marking the line. "And no second purchase of timber under a different name?"

"One new prop. The others are sound."

Rhyse stood at the window with the watch roster. He had brought it because a junior officer had proposed moving Pel Ordan from the inner gate to cover the lower stair.

"No," he said before Damaris could ask.

"The lower stair requires a witness," Vess answered.

"It will have one. Not by taking the man who caught the last error at the gate."

Iven Marrick still held the prepared threshold by choice. The roster now named two living guards beside him. Rhyse would not let an underground inquiry undo the correction he had signed the previous day.

Damaris drew a line beneath the total. "The northern posts have no grain. We have six sacks here and six beyond Dols. The receiving roof still needs paid work, and a second convoy has no permission to cross. I can pay for this inspection because it is bounded. I cannot pay for whatever you imagine is at the bottom."

Nessa Rell, standing behind Vess, laid her own copy of the earlier section on the table.

"I have not imagined a bottom," she said. "I have measured a turn that we did not pass."

Luceran had sent word that he wanted to see the new drawing before any further entry. Nessa was pleased he had not sent word that he wanted to lead the descent.

Damaris approved the named wages and materials, with one condition written in the margin: if the lintel moved, or the party met a second unsafe obstruction, they would withdraw. No new silver listening way was included. Rhyse assigned Sergeant Dain and a second Doorwarden without stripping the living gate watch.

"Bring back measurements," Damaris said.

"And the men," Rhyse added.

---

Nessa went down first after Dain had tested the line.

She had refused Pell's offer to put a mason ahead of her. The man setting the lower prop would need to see her chalk marks from the safe side; the person measuring the wall would need to be near enough to know whether the wall moved. Neither duty required a procession of rank.

At the first landing, the damaged tread remained crossed by the short lashed plank. They tested the lashing and passed it one at a time. The new shutter above was open, but Talla's man waited beside it with a lamp and the tally of everyone below.

The paired low slits stood where Nessa remembered them. She made Dain crouch behind one while a worker carried a lantern toward the lower turn.

"Can you see his face?" she asked.

"No."

"His hands?"

"Only when he lifts the lamp."

"Could you strike him?"

"If I had a short spear and he came close enough. Perhaps. It is a poor place to stand."

That answer mattered more than a handsome sketch of an arrow slit. Nessa recorded the height of Dain's shoulder, the thickness of the wall and the line of sight. A bow would have little room to draw. A thrusting weapon or a barred shutter might fit the old iron sockets. She could not name what had once occupied them.

At the unsafe lintel, Pell placed the first prop from the upper landing. A worker tightened its wedge with short, careful blows. They listened between strikes. The old mortar shed dust, but the bearing did not visibly open. The second prop went in from the other safe side. Pell marked both heads in chalk and tested the weight before permitting anyone beneath.

"One at a time," he said. "And no stopping under the stone to admire it."

The stair beyond descended six steps to a narrow landing. Nessa counted each one aloud while Dain watched the darkness ahead. The landing had a shallow groove running across its width. At either end was a squared hollow in the masonry, each with a dark stain left by iron.

"A second door?" Vess asked from above.

"Or a beam," Pell said.

Nessa looked back toward the first missing door. The groove was not aligned with the old hinge scar. This barrier, whatever it had been, had stood farther down and nearer the turn. It could have narrowed the passage to one man, or stopped a cart, or held a gate across a mine entrance. The stone alone would not choose between those uses.

She drew the groove. Then she noticed the bruising.

The lower face of the stone on the far side of the groove was chipped in several places. The upper face, where a person retreating toward the wine cellar might have struck it, was comparatively sound. A long shallow socket on the upper side could have held the end of a heavy bar.

"Look at this," she told Pell.

He did not lean across her shoulder. He took the lamp from the worker, crouched, and looked where she pointed.

"Something struck it from below," he said.

"Something may have. Or the stones were damaged while the iron was removed."

"The bar was set from this side."

"The socket is on this side," Nessa corrected. "That is what we can say."

Dain looked down the next turn. The passage beyond fell away too sharply for the lantern to reach its foot. He had men above him, an exit behind him and no knowledge of what lay around the corner.

"Enough," he said.

No one argued.

---

They had to wait for the last worker to clear the lintel before they could remove the wedges used to test its bearing. Pell left the principal braces in place. He would not trust a newly exposed passage merely because it had stood through one visit.

The worker carrying the drawing board caught his knuckle against a projecting stone. The cut was small, but he was holding the board with blood on his thumb when Nessa saw him.

"Wrap it," she said.

"It is nothing."

"Then it will be a very inexpensive dressing."

Dain entered the injury in the party's account. He had seen men conceal worse wounds because a foreman treated a report as an accusation. A clean record was easier to defend than a missing finger three days later.

At the upper landing Vess asked whether the two barriers made a deliberate defense.

"Deliberate construction," Nessa said. "Yes. A defense is a reasonable reading."

"Facing the lower passage."

"The fittings face it. The old slits do too."

"Against what?"

"That is not a measurement."

Pell thought a garrison might have been held in lower rooms during a revolt. Dain thought a mine entrance might need to be closed against raiders emerging from another gallery. Vess considered whether the old builders had expected armed men to come uphill from a concealed route. None of them had evidence for the identity of those men, or for any passage beyond the next turn.

Nessa had once watched a quarry owner call a cracked retaining wall sound because the words would save him a week's wages. She had also watched a magistrate call the same wall treacherous because a dramatic word sounded like a judgment. Both had wanted her drawing to speak for them.

She wrote beneath the new section: *Upper-side bar socket; lower-face impact marks; paired openings directed toward descending turn. Use uncertain. No passage beyond next turn surveyed.*

Vess copied the measurements for the Crown. Nessa kept her independent original.

When they reached the cellar, Talla counted eight people out and examined the casks nearest the shutter. She found no broken seals and no new seepage. Only then did she ask what had been discovered.

"Another door that isn't there," Pell said.

"Then you will not need to buy a key."

"Perhaps a bar."

"Buy it out of the king's account. My cellar has enough iron."

Nessa showed her the new section. Talla followed the lines with a finger and saw two places where people had once been able to stop someone coming up.

"Who were they keeping down?" she asked.

"We do not know that they were keeping anyone down," Nessa said.

Talla looked at her for a moment. "Then you had better not tell the king that they were."

---

Yara Fen received a Dols letter while Hesta Vale's brother was measuring stone beside the receiving yard.

The letter was addressed to the envoy's household, not to Yara personally. It asked whether the western approach account sent in Corven Sareth's name represented the views of all three benches or merely a recommendation made while he was away from them. Dols had not forgotten that it possessed its own authority over military passage.

Yara could answer part of the question. She held the three separate warrants. She knew which words permitted Corven to recommend and which required a bench's own seal. She would not let a convenient court clerk turn the former into the latter.

"Will you put it before him?" the messenger asked.

"I will put the question before the household," she said. "I will not give you an answer under a warrant I do not hold."

He wanted a signature before riding back. She gave him a dated acknowledgment of receipt instead.

Hesta's brother had brought two smaller quarry blocks to test the return load. The first was still too broad for the cart's rear boards. Hesta sent him back with a measuring cord rather than pay to have the stone cut again on a guess. The grain carts would not become profitable simply because someone at court called the arrangement sensible.

Yara watched him go. She had her own brother's damaged warehouse to think of, and letters to Leora whose delivery she still could not confirm. She could not ask the man now wearing Corven's face what her former employer would have written to his wife. The man had his own memories and a different grief. That was precisely the problem.

She took the Dols question, the old warrants and the village-watch copy into her locked case. A separate letter would go to the benches asking them to identify the living officer who could attest their own orders. She would not call that a test of a dead man's identity. Not yet.

---

The council heard the lower survey in the same room where it had priced the first descent.

Damaris put the account down before anyone could speak of the discovery. Two days' wages, one replacement prop, oil, a dressing, and no charge against the receiving roof or the northern grain. The injured worker would not lose a shift for reporting his hand. Talla's shutter and drain were finished and entered separately.

Nessa laid out both sections. She showed the first pair of slits, the unsafe lintel now braced, the second groove and the socket on the upper side. She placed a small stone at the lower turn to show where a person coming uphill would have been stopped.

Luceran studied the drawing.

"Two barriers facing the same way."

"Two arrangements that could close the passage," Nessa said. "Their fittings are directed toward the lower turn."

"That is an elaborate precaution against one's own cellar."

"Perhaps it was not a cellar when the work was built," Vess said.

Rhyse asked how many armed men could hold the upper landing. Dain said two might defend the narrow place if they had light and warning, but could not see beyond the turn. An attacker below could retreat out of reach. A defender above could be trapped if the upper stair failed. Neither advantage made the works proof of a prison or a siege.

Canon Merel asked whether the masonry bore an inscription. Nessa said none had been exposed. No one proposed scraping away sound stone to find one.

Luceran had been listening to the people who knew their trades. He nevertheless found himself wanting the one voice that might answer without wages, ropes or a second day's delay.

"Edselsto may remember who built it," he said.

Ysabet waited.

He looked toward the connected arch. There was no silver mask upon it.

"Not here," he said. "Not as a substitute for the measurements."

Damaris asked what the next request would cost. Pell said the second turn required another survey from a safe stance, and the lower passage might need a new timber platform before a man could stand where the drawing ended. He would not price a deeper descent until he had sounded the next bearing.

Luceran approved a drawing and a timber estimate, not entry beyond the next turn. Damaris would hear the estimate beside the roof and grain accounts. Rhyse would continue to name living guards without touching the inner-gate roster. Nessa's independent section would be kept with her; the Crown copy would be sealed with Pell's observations and the separate competing explanations. The new work would not carry listening silver.

"The openings face inward," Luceran said at last.

"They face the lower turn," Nessa replied.

He allowed the correction to stand.

---

Miren found him after the council, looking at the blank part of the section below Nessa's last measured line.

He had expected the discovery to make him feel closer to his ancestors. Instead it had made the castle seem built by people who had feared something enough to spend good iron and stone on two barriers in a passage nobody now used.

"Did you ask the house?" she said.

"No."

"Did you want to?"

"Very much."

She stood beside him, but not close enough to invite an embrace.

"Then you have learned how expensive an answer can be."

"I have learned how expensive not having one is."

"Those are different lessons."

He smiled faintly. She did not. He remembered the conversation he had stolen from her and the body he had ordered occupied. A careful refusal today could not undo either.

"Will you come below when it is safe?" he asked.

"Perhaps. If Nessa says it is safe. Not because you ask me to admire your inheritance."

"Fair."

She looked at the measured line. "There is a great deal of empty paper beneath it."

"Yes."

"Leave it empty until someone has been there."

Below them, Talla latched the cellar shutter. Dain signed the returned party's tally. Pel Ordan took his place at the gate beside another living guard and the dead keeper who had not agreed to become a second man. A roof carpenter covered the remaining grain against the night rain. Across Dols, six sacks still waited for a carrier the Crown had not hired.

Nessa put her independent drawing away without giving the lower passage a name.

In the family nave, all seven candles burned.
