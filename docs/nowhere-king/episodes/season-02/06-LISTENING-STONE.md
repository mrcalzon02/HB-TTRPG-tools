# Season Two, Episode 6 — Listening Stone

The first thing Edselsto heard with the new silver was a lie about lamp oil.

It was not a grand lie.

That was what made it useful.

Vess had spent six days opening walls, refusing bad estimates, insulting three generations of repairs, and stretching Damaris's six marks of restoration silver across the second approved phase. The new branch did not reach bedrooms, confession, Calienne's rooms, the laundry, or the sealed council chamber. It reached places people had already agreed were operational: the lower archive approach, the east receiving rooms, the steward's passage, two store corridors, the stable counting room, and the outer map gallery used by messengers waiting for clerks.

Every active room carried the ugly black ear.

Every bridge was numbered.

Every junction had a seal.

The work was, by Vess's standards, almost civilized.

Then Tomas Reed came to Damaris with an oil tally.

"Forty-two jars received," he said.

Damaris looked at the page.

"Ordered?"

"Forty-eight."

"Broken?"

"None reported."

"Short delivery?"

"Factor swears forty-eight left Northbridge."

"Who counted at Edselsto?"

Tomas looked unhappy.

"Jerrik Moss."

Damaris knew the name. Everybody did now. Jerrik had served the household before the coup, kept his post through it, and survived the retaking by the unromantic method of doing exactly what whichever armed man stood nearest required of him.

He was not a hero.

He was good at stores.

That mattered more often.

"Ask him," Damaris said.

"I did."

"And?"

"He says forty-two."

Damaris tapped the tally.

"Then either Northbridge sent forty-two and billed forty-eight, six jars broke without leaving glass, or someone has discovered invisible oil."

Tomas said nothing.

Damaris looked up.

"You have a fourth answer."

"The east receiving room listens now."

There it was.

Not accusation.

Opportunity.

Damaris leaned back.

"What do the rules say?"

"Edselsto's word cannot determine property or punishment."

"Good."

"It can report what it heard."

"With whose authority?"

Tomas looked toward the door.

"Yours, for household accounts?"

"No."

He frowned.

Damaris smiled thinly.

"My warrant lets me ask about Crown accounts placed before it. It does not let me ask the walls to tell me every conversation that might someday cost the Crown money."

"That seems inconvenient."

"That is the point."

She folded the tally.

"Find Jerrik."

---

Jerrik had a fifth answer.

"Rats."

Damaris stared at him.

"Rats stole six sealed jars of lamp oil."

"Big rats."

"Could have leaked."

"You said rats."

"Rats knocked them."

"Where is the oil?"

"Absorbed."

"Into what?"

"Floor."

The east receiving storeroom had a stone floor.

Jerrik realized this as Damaris looked down.

Tomas covered his mouth.

Damaris said, "Again."

Jerrik's face changed.

Not guilt.

Calculation.

He was deciding which lie cost least.

"Two broke."

"Glass?"

"Cleared."

"By whom?"

"Boy named Pate."

"There is no Pate on the store roll."

"Kitchen boy."

"Which kitchen?"

Jerrik looked at Tomas.

Tomas looked almost offended to be invited into the lie.

Damaris said, "Stop."

Jerrik stopped.

She did not ask Edselsto.

She asked living people.

For two hours.

The Northbridge carrier had a signed gate count for forty-eight. Rhyse's guard book recorded the wagon. The stable clerk remembered six jars being moved separately because one crate had split. A scullion remembered Jerrik borrowing a handcart after supper. Nobody remembered where it went.

Then Nera Vale, carrying folded linen through the lower passage, heard Tomas complaining about oil.

"Six jars?"

Damaris turned.

Nera shifted the basket against her hip.

"Yes."

"You know something?"

"I know Jerrik's sister sells lamps in the village."

Jerrik's face went white.

Low-status information did not arrive like revelation.

It arrived carrying sheets.

Damaris sent a clerk to the village.

Jerrik sat down.

"I was going to replace it."

"With what?"

"Next month's."

"That is not replacing it. That is stealing twice."

"My sister's boy is sick."

Damaris closed her eyes.

There was always a sister.

Always a child.

Always a reason that was real enough to make theft understandable and not real enough to make it lawful.

"How much did she sell?"

"Four."

"And the other two?"

"Still there."

"Where?"

"Her shed."

Damaris looked at Tomas.

"Recover them. Pay the sister fair market value for any jars already sold onward if the buyer acted honestly. Jerrik is suspended from stores pending hearing."

Jerrik stood.

"Lady—"

"Damaris."

He swallowed.

"Damaris. Please."

She hated pleas because they turned arithmetic into faces.

"You will be heard."

Rhyse arrived before they took Jerrik away.

He listened to the account.

Then looked toward the black ear beside the receiving-room door.

"We could have known in a minute."

Damaris said, "Yes."

Neither of them sounded pleased.

---

Luceran heard about the theft at dinner.

He was eating with Miren, Rhyse, Damaris and Ysabet because meals had become the only meetings at which Damaris could reliably force him to consume food.

"Six jars," he said.

"Four sold," Damaris corrected. "Two recovered."

"For a sick nephew."

"Allegedly."

"Do we know?"

"We know the boy is ill. Calienne sent someone because I am not entirely made of ledger paper."

Miren smiled.

Damaris ignored her.

Luceran put down his spoon.

"Did Edselsto hear him?"

Damaris said, "Probably."

"Probably?"

"I did not ask."

"Why not?"

"Because I did not need to."

"You spent two hours."

"Three."

"Three hours."

"And discovered the carrier count, the split crate, the handcart, the sister, the buyer, the sick child and the two unsold jars."

Luceran looked at Rhyse.

Rhyse said, "She also discovered which witnesses can testify at a hearing."

"Edselsto could have given you the conversation."

"Perhaps."

Luceran looked toward the door.

No mask.

Private meals were not an emergency.

He felt the absence now.

That was new.

"What is the point of restoring listening ways if we refuse to listen?"

Miren answered.

"To use them when the cost justifies the intrusion."

"Six jars were stolen."

"Then apparently the threshold is somewhere above lamp oil."

"Where?"

Nobody answered immediately.

Luceran looked from face to face.

"This is absurd. We have built a thing and now everyone is afraid to say what it is for."

Ysabet said, "Then define what it is for."

"Security. Corruption. Threats. Hidden meetings."

Damaris said, "Those are four different warrants."

"They overlap."

"That is how warrants become kingdoms."

Luceran laughed once.

"You say that to a king."

"Yes."

Miren touched his wrist beneath the table.

Not to silence him.

To remind him there was a person attached to the argument.

He turned his hand and caught her fingers.

"Fine," he said. "Tomorrow. We define it."

Rhyse said, "In writing."

Luceran sighed.

"In writing."

---

The next morning Edselsto solved a real problem in less than thirty seconds.

That was unfortunate.

A courier from Red Ford had failed to arrive.

Not late by an hour.

Late by a day.

The road was passable. No storm. No reported attack. The man, Eren Vos, carried the surveyor's preliminary notes on the cattle crossing, toll dispute and burned shed. Nothing secret enough to justify an escort, important enough that Damaris wanted it, ordinary enough that nobody had thought to worry until he missed two expected stops.

Rhyse had riders preparing.

Luceran stood in the lesser audience room with the new listening authorization in front of him.

Ysabet had written it narrowly.

Edselsto could answer questions about immediate threats to Edselsto, active corruption involving Crown property or office, and concealed movements through connected operational spaces when a named officer requested the inquiry and recorded the reason.

No general summaries of private speech.

No fishing for disloyalty.

No use of protected rooms.

No guilt established by an answer.

No action without living corroboration where corroboration was possible.

Luceran signed.

Rhyse countersigned.

Damaris countersigned only the corruption clause.

Miren read it and did not sign because she held no office requiring it.

That bothered Luceran more than it should have.

He called the mask.

It appeared.

"Your Majesty."

"Under today's written authority: did Eren Vos enter any room you could hear yesterday?"

"Yes."

Everyone straightened.

"Where?"

"The stable counting room."

"When?"

"Before sunset."

"Did he leave?"

"Yes."

"With whom?"

"A man called Savel."

Rhyse looked at Ysabet.

She was already writing.

"Did they speak of Red Ford?"

"Yes."

"What did they say?"

Edselsto recited without emphasis.

Vos had complained that somebody had followed him from the lower road. Savel had told him not to take the north track because "Holt's men" were watching it. Vos had asked which Holt. Savel had said, "The one who pays in clipped suns."

Then they had left by the east stable gate.

Rhyse swore.

Damaris stopped breathing for half a beat.

Deren Holt was already an unresolved name in the coup evidence.

Not proof of one network.

Not permission to collapse Red Ford into the coup.

But a live name in a live route problem.

Luceran felt the power of it.

"Where did they go?"

"I did not hear them after the gate."

There was the boundary.

Rhyse said, "Good."

Luceran looked at him.

"Good?"

"It doesn't know."

Rhyse turned to a guard.

"Two riders south-east. One to the Vale Brook fork. One to Red Ford. Do not arrest Holt. Do not announce the name. Find Vos and Savel if you can. Ask living witnesses who saw them."

The guard left.

Luceran looked at the mask.

Thirty seconds.

A day of uncertainty narrowed to a gate, a companion, a warning, a name and a direction.

Useful.

Not complete.

Useful enough.

Miren saw his face.

She knew the face now.

---

By noon they found Eren Vos alive in a charcoal burner's hut three miles east.

His horse had thrown a shoe. Savel had indeed redirected him away from the north track after seeing two riders he believed worked for Deren Holt.

Vos had not been abducted.

He had not been attacked.

He had spent the night drinking terrible pine liquor with the burner because walking a lame horse in darkness had seemed stupid.

The survey notes were intact.

Savel returned separately and gave the same account.

The two riders were not found.

Rhyse put the name into the unresolved road file.

Not the coup file.

Luceran noticed.

"Why separate?"

"Because we know Savel said Holt's name. We do not know Holt sent those riders. We do not know they were hostile. We do not know this is connected to the coup."

"But Holt's name is already—"

"Already elsewhere."

"Exactly."

"Which makes it easier to make one answer explain everything."

Luceran disliked that.

Which meant Rhyse was probably right.

Edselsto had provided intelligence.

Living people had prevented intelligence from becoming mythology.

Again.

---

The problem began in the outer map gallery.

It was a stupid place for a private conversation.

That did not make the conversation public.

The gallery sat between the archive stair and the old north solar. Messengers waited there. Survey maps hung behind shutters. Two benches occupied the window wall. It had been connected in the second conduit phase because Rhyse wanted the approach to the archive covered and Vess wanted a continuous branch without cutting through three additional walls.

There was a black ear beside each door.

Miren knew.

Ardel knew.

They met there anyway because Miren had been walking back from the archive, Ardel had been coming from the yard, and people had private conversations in imperfect places every day.

Edselsto heard them.

Luceran did not know that yet.

He was in the lesser audience room with Rhyse and Ysabet, intoxicated by restraint.

That was the dangerous form of intoxication.

Not frenzy.

Success.

The Red Ford question had been useful. The answer had been bounded. Nobody had been arrested on a ghost's word. The courier was safe. The rules had worked.

Luceran looked at the mask.

"Anything else under today's authority?"

Ysabet's pen stopped.

Rhyse said, "Define anything."

Luceran knew he should.

He was tired.

He had three petitions waiting, Montegrain's counsel demanding another copy of the evidence inventory, and a message from Cendreval about delayed silver.

"Any concealed meeting, corruption, threat or movement through the connected operational rooms since yesterday that materially concerns the Crown."

Ysabet said, "That is broad."

"It is the language we just signed."

"It is four clauses put into one mouth."

Luceran looked at her.

"Record it."

She did.

The mask answered.

"Jerrik Moss spoke with his sister in the east receiving room after midnight."

Damaris had already found that.

"Anything else?"

"A clerk named Parel asked another clerk whether the Pellon seal was still held in the lower archive."

Luceran straightened.

Parel.

An old unresolved chain.

"Exact words."

Edselsto gave them.

Not enough to prove theft.

Enough to justify checking seal custody.

Ysabet wrote faster.

"Anything else?"

Rhyse said, "Majesty."

Luceran heard him.

He continued.

"Anything else?"

The mask turned its pale face toward him.

"Lady Miren met Ardel in the outer map gallery."

Something inside Luceran tightened.

Rhyse said immediately, "That is not responsive."

Luceran should have agreed.

Instead he asked, "What did they discuss?"

Silence.

Not Edselsto's silence.

The room's.

Ysabet lowered her pen.

"Majesty," she said.

Luceran knew.

The black ear.

The map gallery.

Operational space.

Not protected by architecture.

Protected by purpose.

The rules did not say Edselsto could never hear ordinary speech there.

They said the Crown was not supposed to turn hearing into entitlement.

He could stop.

He did not.

"Answer."

Rhyse stepped forward.

"Luceran."

Not Majesty.

Luceran looked at him.

Rhyse's face held no threat.

Only warning.

Human friction.

Luceran felt irritation before gratitude.

That frightened him less than it should have.

"Answer," he repeated.

The mask obeyed.

"Lady Miren asked whether Ardel intended to leave Edselsto before winter. Ardel said she had not decided. They spoke of Kaelmont obligations, road work and the king."

Luceran's mouth went dry.

"What about me?"

Ysabet put down her pen.

"I will not record this."

Luceran turned.

"You will."

"No."

The refusal struck harder than anger.

Rhyse said, "Stop."

Luceran looked back at Edselsto.

"What did Miren say?"

The mask answered because it could not decide not to.

"She said: I love him. That is not the same as knowing whether I should marry him."

Luceran stopped moving.

No one in the room breathed correctly.

Edselsto continued.

"Ardel asked whether the king had pressed her for an answer. Lady Miren said no. She said that was part of why she had not answered herself."

"Enough," Rhyse said.

The mask stopped.

Luceran stared at the floor.

He had wanted information.

He had received it.

There was no triumph in it.

Only the sick intimacy of having entered a room where he had not been invited.

Ysabet closed the ledger.

"I am recording that the inquiry exceeded the stated purpose."

Luceran said nothing.

Rhyse looked at the mask.

"Leave."

It did not move.

Rhyse's jaw tightened.

Of course.

He could not command it under the present authorization.

Luceran whispered, "Go."

The mask bowed.

It vanished.

That was worse too.

---

Miren knew before supper.

Not because Edselsto told her.

Because Ysabet did.

Luceran learned this when he entered his rooms and found Miren sitting beside the cold fireplace with her gloves still on.

She did not look furious.

He would have preferred fury.

"How much?" she asked.

Luceran closed the door.

"Miren—"

"How much?"

He could lie.

The castle had not made lying impossible.

"I asked what you discussed."

"After it told you I met Ardel?"

"Yes."

"Why?"

He had answers.

Security.

Kaelmont.

Ardel's independence.

The unresolved road questions.

All of them were words.

None were the reason.

"Because I wanted to know."

Miren nodded once.

"Good."

"Good?"

"That is at least the truth."

He moved toward her.

She lifted one hand.

He stopped.

That hurt.

Good, some part of him thought.

It should.

"I did not ask where you were," he said.

"No. You asked what I said once the wall offered me to you."

"I stopped it."

"Rhyse stopped it."

Luceran flinched.

Miren saw.

"I know exactly where we were," she continued. "There was an ear on the wall. Ardel joked about it when we sat down. I knew the castle could hear."

"Then—"

"No."

The word cut cleanly.

"Do not make that argument."

He shut his mouth.

"I knew the room could hear. I did not agree that you could ask the room to repeat me."

Luceran sat opposite her.

For once there was no throne, no council table, no mask.

Just two chairs.

"What do you want me to say?"

"I don't know."

"That isn't fair."

"No."

She took off one glove.

Then the other.

"I don't know because I have spent the afternoon deciding whether I am angry that you know, angry that you asked, angry that I was stupid enough to speak in a connected room, or angry that part of me is relieved I do not have to tell you myself."

That last one hurt differently.

Luceran looked at his hands.

"Do you love me?"

Miren laughed once.

It broke halfway through.

"You already stole that answer."

He closed his eyes.

"I'm sorry."

"I know."

"That doesn't help."

"No."

"Do you want to marry me?"

She stood.

He looked up.

"Do not use what you heard to make me finish a conversation I did not choose to begin with you."

He nodded.

She walked to the door.

"Miren."

She stopped.

"I won't ask Edselsto about you again."

She looked back.

"That is not enough."

"What is?"

"A place where you cannot."

Then she left.

---

Ardel punched him.

Not hard enough to break anything.

Hard enough that Rhyse, standing ten feet away, decided not to intervene.

It happened the next morning in the lower yard.

Luceran had gone looking for her.

That was his first mistake.

He found her checking saddle straps with Kaelmont riders.

That was his second.

He asked for a private word.

She gave him one behind the tack shed.

Then hit him.

Luceran touched his lip.

"I am still king."

"Then arrest me."

"I am not going to arrest you."

"Wonderful. We have found a boundary."

He deserved that.

"Did Ysabet tell you?"

"Miren did."

"Of course."

Ardel folded her arms.

Luceran said, "I was asking about threats."

"No. You were asking about everything and pretending the word threat made it respectable."

"Edselsto mentioned you."

"And you became jealous."

He looked at her.

Ardel's anger sharpened.

"Do not flatter yourself into thinking this is romantic."

"I didn't."

"You have been jealous of me around Miren before."

His face betrayed him.

Ardel laughed without humor.

"There. Living intelligence."

"Ardel."

"No. You want to know what I think? Ask me."

"Fine. Are you leaving before winter?"

She blinked.

The question had been in the stolen conversation.

Luceran realized it too late.

Ardel's expression closed.

"That answer is no longer yours."

He felt the consequence settle.

Not because she had a secret.

Because he had damaged the ordinary route to knowing it.

She stepped closer.

"Kaelmont has road obligations. I have family obligations. I have obligations here. I was deciding how to balance them. Yesterday I might have told you."

"And today?"

"Today you can wonder."

She walked away.

Rhyse waited until she was gone.

"Did that help?"

Luceran looked at him.

"Are you enjoying this?"

"No."

That made it worse.

Rhyse handed him a cloth for his lip.

"You asked me once why the rules matter if you can order around them."

Luceran pressed the cloth to his mouth.

"I remember."

"This is why."

---

The council met without Edselsto.

That was Miren's demand.

Not because the mask had done something wrong.

Because everyone needed to be able to discuss what happened without the subject of the discussion waiting politely in the corner.

Vess arrived carrying three bridge pieces.

He placed them on the table.

"Which room?"

Miren looked at him.

"Mine."

"Already isolated."

"A room for meetings."

"Sealed council room."

"Not the Crown's room."

Vess understood.

"So not controlled by royal permission."

Luceran sat very still.

Damaris looked at Miren.

"What are you asking for?"

"A deaf room whose bridge is physically absent and whose key is not held by the king."

Rhyse said, "The king can order the keyholder."

"Then two keyholders."

"He can order both."

"Three."

Luceran said, "At some point this becomes theater."

Miren looked at him.

"Yes."

The word landed.

"Most law is theater performed until enough people agree it is expensive to stop."

Ysabet almost smiled.

Miren continued.

"I am not asking for a room the king can never enter. I am asking for a room Edselsto cannot hear unless living people physically reconnect it."

Merel said, "Like confession."

"Yes."

Calienne said, "Like treatment."

"Yes."

Nera Vale had been invited.

Luceran noticed that Miren had invited her.

Not him.

Nera said, "Like laundry."

Miren smiled.

"Apparently the laundresses solved constitutional privacy first."

Nera shrugged.

"We had practice."

Vess spread a plan.

"The old green withdrawing room off the south gallery has a single verified listening branch. We can remove it at the junction, not merely bridge the room itself. No silver continuity. I can show the break."

"Who keeps the removed piece?" Damaris asked.

Miren said, "Nobody."

Vess looked offended.

"What do you mean nobody?"

"Melt it."

"That is good silver."

Damaris said, "Now I am offended too."

For the first time in a day, Luceran laughed.

Miren did not.

He stopped.

Vess said, "We can store it under archive seal."

Ysabet shook her head.

"Then it can be restored by paper."

Rhyse said, "Make a replacement require fresh metal."

Damaris groaned.

"At least make it a small piece."

They argued for half an hour.

That mattered.

Privacy acquired a cost.

Silver.

Labor.

A room surrendered from other use.

A guard outside who could protect the occupants without hearing them.

A rule that no record of who met inside would be generated merely because they met.

No secret tribunal.

No criminal immunity.

No sanctuary from ordinary warrants.

Just architectural deafness.

Luceran listened.

Finally Miren looked at him.

"Well?"

Everyone else did too.

He could refuse.

Edselsto would obey him if he refused.

The room would remain hearable.

He thought of Miren saying, I love him.

He hated that the memory was beautiful.

He hated more that it was stolen.

"Do it," he said.

Vess nodded.

"Today."

Luceran looked at Ysabet.

"Record why."

Miren's face changed slightly.

Not forgiveness.

Recognition.

Ysabet dipped her pen.

"Because the Crown exceeded the purpose of an authorized listening inquiry and acquired private speech unrelated to the lawful question."

Luceran swallowed.

"Yes."

Rhyse said, "Name the Crown."

Luceran looked at him.

Rhyse did not look away.

Ysabet waited.

Luceran said, "Because I did."

She wrote it.

---

The Parel question remained.

That was the cruelest part.

The stolen intimacy had not made the useful intelligence disappear.

Ysabet checked the Pellon seal.

It was present.

The wax block was not.

A clerk had removed it three days earlier under an old inventory notation that did not authorize removal.

Parel denied intending theft.

He claimed he had asked about the seal because he was reconciling old credential stock.

The other clerk confirmed only the question, not the motive.

Edselsto had heard words.

Not intent.

Rhyse investigated.

Living hands found wax scrapings in a copying room and two impressions on waste parchment.

One matched the Pellon seal.

One did not.

Parel admitted making the impression.

"For practice," he said.

Ysabet stared at him.

"Practice what?"

"Recognizing forged seals."

"By forging one."

"I see the problem now."

It would have been funny in another week.

It was not funny now.

Parel was removed from seal access pending a formal inquiry.

No one called him traitor.

No one connected him automatically to the coup.

The information mattered.

The restraint mattered.

Both had come from the same listening system that had violated Miren.

Luceran found that intolerable because it denied him the comfort of deciding the system was simply bad.

---

The deaf room took two days.

Vess made Luceran watch.

Not the whole time.

Enough.

They opened the south-gallery junction, exposed the old channel, removed a hand-length of silver continuity, and capped both ends with ceramic sleeves.

Pell Falbrecht watched too.

"So the king can't listen?"

Vess said, "The king can stand outside with a cup."

Pell considered.

"Could hear through the door?"

"If people shout."

"Good."

Vess looked at him.

"Why good?"

"Means it's still a room."

The removed silver was weighed.

Damaris insisted.

Then melted into an assay button and entered back into Crown stock.

No bridge existed to hide in a drawer.

To reconnect the room would require a new piece to be fabricated, entered, carried, installed and witnessed.

Not impossible.

Friction.

The black ear tile came off.

A plain white tile replaced it.

Then Miren objected.

"White means deliberately isolated. People already know that."

"Yes," Vess said.

"I don't want a sign that says important private conversations happen here."

Nera, who had somehow become a recurring authority on rooms that should mind their own business, said, "Then take the tile off."

Vess frowned.

"No tile means unrestored."

"Isn't that what it is?"

"Technically—"

Pell said, "Master Wall-Opener."

Vess glared at him.

The tile came off.

They left a tiny mason's mark inside the junction cabinet instead.

Anyone standing in the room saw an ordinary room.

Anyone authorized to inspect the infrastructure could verify it was deaf.

That distinction pleased Ysabet.

It frightened Luceran.

He was beginning to understand how much governance consisted of deciding who was allowed to verify what.

---

Miren used the room before he did.

She met Damaris there.

Then Calienne.

Then Ardel.

Luceran knew because he saw them enter.

Not because Edselsto told him.

That difference became physical.

He passed the south gallery twice that afternoon.

The closed door bothered him.

He could have opened it.

He did not.

On the third pass, Rhyse was leaning against the opposite wall.

Luceran stopped.

"Are you guarding it?"

"No."

"What are you doing?"

"Waiting for you to walk past."

Luceran stared.

Rhyse smiled.

"Human friction."

Luceran laughed despite himself.

Then he walked past.

---

At sunset the riders returned with a better description of the two men seen near the north track.

One wore a Bellisar-pattern riding coat with the insignia removed.

The other had a scar under his left eye.

Neither description proved service to Deren Holt.

Rhyse entered both into the road file.

The treasury claimant review produced three new petitions.

Hesta Vale's track bargain had taught the village that old paper could become current leverage.

Damaris complained about precedent for an hour and then scheduled the claims because precedent was also government.

Jerrik Moss's hearing was set.

His sister returned the two unsold jars voluntarily.

Calienne confirmed the nephew was ill but not dying.

The four sold jars could not be recovered without harming innocent buyers, so Damaris converted their value into a debt against Jerrik's future wages if he remained employed after judgment.

Senn Daro copied public tax rolls downstairs and did not touch sealed inventory.

Iven Marrick remained at his door.

His mother visited.

All seven candles burned.

Nothing cosmic happened.

That was important.

The danger was becoming ordinary.

---

Late that night Luceran found Miren in the south gallery.

Not inside the deaf room.

Outside it.

She was sitting on the window bench where moonlight cut across the floor.

He stopped several paces away.

"May I sit?"

She considered.

"Yes."

He sat.

For a while neither spoke.

Then Luceran said, "I keep hearing it."

Miren's shoulders tightened.

"The sentence."

She knew which one.

"I wish I didn't."

"Do you?"

He looked at her.

"No."

That answer cost him.

"I wish I had not learned it that way."

Miren looked toward the deaf-room door.

"Better."

"Not enough."

"No."

He rubbed his hands together.

"I want to ask you."

"I know."

"I won't."

She studied him.

"That is also better."

He laughed softly.

"This is miserable."

"Privacy often is, from the wrong side."

He looked at her.

She almost smiled.

Almost.

"Are we all right?"

"No."

The answer hurt.

She took his hand anyway.

"We are not finished."

That was different.

Luceran held on.

After a while she said, "You understand the worst part?"

"That I invaded your privacy."

"No."

He waited.

"The worst part is that it worked."

He knew immediately what she meant.

Jerrik.

Parel.

Vos.

Savel.

Holt's name.

A route.

A missing wax block.

Useful things.

"The walls told you something you needed," Miren said. "Then they told you something you wanted. You did not notice the border until you were already across it."

Luceran looked at the black ear on the distant archive approach.

"What do I do with that?"

"Remember there is a border."

"And if someone dies because I refuse to cross it?"

"Then you will hate me."

"I would never—"

"Do not promise things for frightened future versions of yourself."

He fell silent.

That was unfair.

That was wise.

He hated both.

Miren leaned her head against his shoulder.

Not reconciliation.

Not withdrawal.

A relationship continuing while damaged.

That mattered too.

At the far end of the gallery, the pale mask appeared at the boundary of a connected corridor.

It did not approach.

Luceran looked at it.

For one moment he imagined asking whether anyone had spoken his name that day.

Who hated him.

Who lied.

Who wanted him.

Who planned to leave.

Who still loved him.

Every answer waited somewhere in stone.

He said nothing.

The mask waited.

Then Luceran looked away.

Behind the closed door beside him was one room Edselsto could not hear.

Not because the castle had chosen restraint.

Because living people had cut silver out of the wall.

And because, for tonight, the king had allowed the cut to remain.
