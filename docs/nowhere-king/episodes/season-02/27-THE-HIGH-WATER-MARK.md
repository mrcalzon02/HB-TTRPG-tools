# Season Two, Episode 27 — The High-Water Mark

Tomas had laid the three carriage offers on a board across two empty barrels. By the time Damaris reached the receiving yard, the board was damp and one of the offers had acquired a horseshoe-shaped stain.

"That one has been improved," she said.

"The horse had an opinion."

"Does it offer to pull a cart?"

"No."

The roof carpenters were working above them. Every fourth blow of the mallet shook dust from a beam that had been set the previous winter and never properly pegged. Beneath the new section of roof the six sacks from the Crown's first purchase were dry. Tomas had moved them twice as the work advanced, each move entered in the yard book because the receiving clerk refused to swear to the condition of grain he had not seen.

Damaris looked at the offers again. The cheapest man would take the six sacks waiting beyond Dols northward if the roads remained passable. He would not answer for a night's rain. The second demanded more coin in advance than the Crown could release without delaying the smith's wages. The third required patrol horses Rhyse had withdrawn for good military reasons.

"We have been treating these as three answers," she said. "They are three ways of telling us what we cannot buy."

Tomas knew the first carrier's brother kept the far-bank store. That was not a guarantee of honest dealing; it did mean the sacks could remain under an inspected roof until a dry departure. Damaris sent a rider with a different offer: a modest payment for the cart and team, a separate charge for each day of covered waiting, and the balance payable against a northern receiving mark. The carrier would not bear the loss of grain spoiled before his witnessed loading. He would bear ordinary negligence after it. If the weather made the road unsafe, the sacks stayed in the store and the Crown paid for the delay.

"More expensive than the lowest price," Tomas said.

"Cheaper than buying the same grain twice."

She entered the proposed charges beside the roof wages and the smith's bill. There would be no second merchant advance to disguise the expense. She authorized the final carpenter's wages from the already assigned roof account, leaving the smith's iron payment untouched, and asked the paymaster to bring the workers their coin while the foreman could still name each man.

A laborer on the ladder heard that and called down, "Does the king know we're to be paid before the roof stops leaking?"

"The king will be told," Damaris said.

The man laughed. His wife had been buying flour on credit since the last wage day. By evening he would have coin to put in her hand. He did not care which ancient chamber had supplied the Crown's breathing room. He cared that a real paymaster could count.

Beyond the yard, the road toward Dols remained shut to a second wagon. Six sacks were not yet a northern ration. Damaris left the word *delivered* out of her account.

---

At Dols, the miller wanted his garden wall before he wanted an apology.

The bridge reeve had summoned Savelin, the watch captain, and the mason who had measured the drainage cut after the first permitted wagon crossed. Water still ran against the miller's stones where the Crown's approach had narrowed the channel. It was lower now, but a dry morning did not undo a wet month's damage.

The mason set a cord across the broken length and named the stone he would need. The miller made him measure the foundation too. The last man had offered to build upon loose earth and had been surprised when the miller declined.

"If another wheel comes before this is made sound," the miller said, "the water will take the corner of my garden. Then you will call it a road expense and I will call it my supper."

Savelin had the Crown's corrected account in his hand. It admitted that the earlier passage was one daylight permission, not an enduring opening. He had been asked to persuade the reeve that a second wagon might cross when the water fell.

"I can tell you the approach is passable today," he said. "I cannot promise tomorrow's rain."

The reeve made two separate decisions. He accepted the mason's measured repair at a named price, payable from the Crown's road account after the miller and watch captain inspected it. He also allowed one civilian grain wagon to present itself at the bridge after the repair was accepted, in daylight, with the watch empowered to stop it for water or unsafe decking. He would not permit a military column, issue a military countersign, or turn the one-wagon entry into a continuing right.

The miller listened to the conditions twice.

"Who pays if the mason finds the ground worse when he opens it?"

"The Crown must be asked for the difference," the reeve said. "You need not surrender your garden while we argue."

"And if the wagon comes early?"

"It waits."

The watch captain marked the words in his own book. Savelin carried a copy, not the original, back toward Edselsto.

The miller watched the mason unload his tools. He had expected to spend another morning being told that hungry soldiers mattered more than his wall. Hungry soldiers did matter. He could see the damage to the northern road from his own window. But he had three children and a winter garden, and a sovereign's necessity did not make mud hold stone.

The first course was set before noon. The miller stayed to inspect it himself.

---

Mistress Calven found the Teth clerk waiting beside her ferry with the two boatwrights.

They had agreed about the cracked gunwale at last. They had not agreed about the days of trade she would lose while the repair was made. One boatwright had priced only the timber, iron and labor. The other had asked the ferry hands how many market crossings the work would interrupt.

"That is the one who understands what a boat is for," Calven said.

The clerk showed her a new letter from Edselsto. It offered Crown coin toward the repair and asked whether the ferry might be hired for a future civilian carriage, subject to Teth's hearing. It did not claim that Corven Sareth's recommendation had become a requisition.

"Who wrote it?"

"Aldren copied it. Yara Fen names the witnesses to the terms. The king's seal is on the undertaking to pay."

"And Sareth?"

"The letter makes no new claim in his name."

Calven did not say she trusted the Crown. She read the proposed hire aloud to her ferry master, who objected that the loading bank would need more hands than the price allowed. The clerk entered that objection. The boatwrights agreed to work in two short periods so that Calven could keep one market crossing each day, weather permitting. She accepted the repair price with the lost-fare allowance written separately, and retained her right to challenge any later valuation.

She did not surrender her boat. She gave the bench permission to discuss a single future civilian hire once the repair was witnessed and the loading charge settled.

The clerk smiled as though a treaty had been made.

"Don't put that in your book," Calven told him. "Put down that you may ask me again."

He did.

By evening the first repaired length of gunwale had been clamped into place. Calven went home with less trade than she wanted and a better chance of keeping her ferry afloat. The Crown had gained a possible route, not a vessel. Both were facts worth preserving.

Hesta Vale's brother came into Edselsto with a quarry block that fitted the return cart.

It was smaller than the stone he had hoped to sell. The first block had been too broad for the rear boards; the second had been cut down and weighed again before anyone yoked the team. He had brought the measuring cord with him and was proud enough of the result to be offended when Tomas asked to see it.

"Measure it," he said. "I'd rather be paid for stone than for your faith in my judgment."

The block came off without breaking the cart. Hesta had used her four-season toll-free Crown-track right for this journey, subject to the ordinary military closures. The cart's outward passage had carried tools to the quarry. Its return now carried one saleable stone instead of empty air. She showed Tomas the account for the extra cutting and demanded that he keep the old disputed wheel fee on a separate line.

"You'll say the free track makes up for the fee," she told him.

"It doesn't."

"Good. Then we're learning."

Tomas arranged a modest purchase for the block against the receiving-yard drain, not the undercroft. No one was authorized to take it below the wine cellar. Hesta's brother would make another trial when the cart boards had been strengthened. His team needed rest first.

Across the yard, the smith received the iron payment that Damaris had refused to postpone. The roof foreman counted the last pegs, watched his men finish the exposed edge, and entered the remaining work as guttering rather than calling the whole roof complete. The laborer who had shouted down from the ladder took his wages home in a tied cloth. He bought flour and a strip of smoked meat; the merchant wrote off the oldest part of his household's debt.

Etta, sent from the kitchen to collect a corrected tally, saw the laborer's wife waiting by the gate. She knew the woman from the washhouse. The wife took the cloth without counting it in public. Her expression changed when she felt its weight.

Etta carried that sight back with the tally. The household would later praise the Crown for a roof, a road and a northern delivery. The woman at the gate would remember a week's credit finally paid. Neither account was false.

The new stone lay in the yard with a chalk mark naming its intended drain. The undercroft's braced lintel remained below, its next turn unentered, and Nessa Rell's independent drawing remained in her own keeping. A cart fitting a stone did not make an unsafe passage safe.

Jalen Orris was asked to stand beside the king when the northern grain arrived.

He looked at the invitation, then at the name by which the household still presented him to strangers.

"I can stand there," he told Ysabet. "But not as the man who arranged it."

"You helped examine the carrier and merchant accounts."

"I learned them after the argument. Corven fought it before he died."

Ysabet did not correct the word. She had witnessed what had been done to Corven and kept Luceran's lethal instruction among her restricted originals. She also knew that a public ceremony could not give Jalen an office he had never held.

The paymaster's clerk had brought another report that morning. The original discharge account for Jalen's former unit had not yet been recovered. A reference to the roll remained in the archive; no one could say whether his sister had received the casualty money or where she now lived.

"Find the keeper of that chest," Jalen said. "If the record is lost, write that it is lost. Don't tell me it must have been paid."

He had once accepted a command to hold a gate because another soldier needed sleep. He could endure an unanswered question. He could not bear the courteous assurance that an answer existed somewhere when nobody had looked at it.

Yara came with a letter from the Dols reeve. He would receive the person publicly described as the recovered envoy for a witnessed hearing, but would not treat the appearance itself as proof of identity or authority. A physician appointed by Dols could examine him, and the reeve would keep his own record. The original recommendation warrants would remain distinct. No military countersign was offered.

Luceran had wanted the hearing delayed until the next grain cart crossed. Rhyse objected to making the man's presence a price for the bridge, and Yara pointed out that Dols had offered no such bargain. The king eventually authorized an escort of living guards and a paid physician's attendance for the road, with a statement that the visitor's capacity to speak and advise was disputed. The Crown did not order him to sign Corven's name.

Jalen read the order before agreeing to travel. He asked whether he could leave the party if he could no longer endure the borrowed body's pain. Rhyse promised he would not be tied to a saddle, but could not promise the journey would be easy. Ysabet would retain Leora's sealed original question; the witnessed reply and the separate watch copy were already on their different roads.

"Will they ask me who I am?" Jalen said.

"Almost certainly," Yara answered.

"Then I shall tell them what I know."

He did not know whether his answer would help Luceran. He had begun to suspect that helping the king and telling the truth were no longer the same duty.

The far-bank carrier arrived at the northern post on the third afternoon.

The six sacks had spent the first night under the far-bank store roof while rain crossed the road. The carrier's brother had marked the delay and the dry condition of each sack before loading. On the second morning the carrier set out with his own team and a hired helper. One wheel sank at the marsh edge, costing them two hours and a plank borrowed from a tenant who expected the plank returned.

At the post, the quartermaster would not sign until the sacks had been lifted down and inspected. He cut one seam to check the grain, then had it sewn again. The carrier objected to the cost of the thread. The quartermaster charged it to the receiving store rather than invent a loss in transit.

Six sacks were entered as received. The northern men had food they had not possessed the day before.

The quartermaster divided the first issue among the smallest posts and retained the remainder for the next scheduled ration. He did not announce that the northern supply problem was solved. The men who had been waiting on reduced portions cheered anyway, and a cook who had been thinning broth with wild greens gave each of them a thicker bowl that evening.

The carrier took the signed receipt and the damaged plank back toward the tenant. His helper wanted his share before they returned south. The carrier paid it, grumbling that a king's grain could make a man poorer if every promise waited for a clerk.

At Merehaven, the factor received a separate copy of the far-bank loading account and entered it against the first surety. He did not release the second advance. The first had been honored through named witnesses and a real receiving mark; the next would still require its own security. He told the merchant waiting beside his desk that the Crown had achieved something worth credit.

"Then you will lend again?"

"I said it was worth credit. I did not say whose coin would carry the next six sacks."

The merchant disliked the answer. He also knew it was the reason the first loan had survived.

Word of the northern receipt traveled by a paid rider to Edselsto. It did not travel faster than the carrier's cart, and it did not reach Leora's household with any news of her husband. The roads held separate messages, each with its own delay.

---

Leora heard that the northern posts had received grain from a trader who had stopped to warm his hands at her mother's kitchen.

He called it the king's triumph and said the recovered envoy had made peace among the river benches. Leora asked which bench had signed the carriage and whether he had seen the envoy himself. He had seen neither. The trader had heard the news from a cartman at an inn.

"Then you have brought us a report of a report," she said.

"A happy one."

"I hope it stays happy."

Her mother had been coughing all morning. The little girl was trying to make a roof for the ribbon horse beneath the table from scraps of bark. Leora helped her balance the pieces, then set aside the old letters without opening their chest. Their witnessed inventory remained intact. She had not received the reply to the private question, and she would not make a stranger's account of grain answer it.

She wrote another short letter, this one asking that the physician's original notes and the watch's own observations be preserved by their existing custodians until she could send a representative to inspect them. She did not instruct anyone to certify a death. Her housekeeper witnessed the outgoing seal and the steward entered the courier's charge. The girl asked whether Uncle Corven would come to see the horse.

"I don't know," Leora said.

The child considered that. "Then I shall make the roof stronger."

At Edselsto, the northern receiving mark was read aloud in the yard. The roof workers stood with the smith, the carters and the village families who had watched the accounts become wages. Luceran gave Damaris credit for the carriage, Savelin for the measured crossing conditions, and the far-bank carrier for the actual delivery. He did not claim that Dols had opened its bridge generally, that Teth had surrendered a ferry, or that Merehaven had lent a second advance.

For once the applause was not wholly courtly. Men who had been owed coin had been paid. Grain had reached hungry soldiers. A miller's wall was being repaired under his own inspection. A ferry owner had retained her rights and gained an enforceable repair price. Those were modest things, but a kingdom could be built from modest things done again.

Miren watched from the edge of the yard. She had once loved Luceran most easily when he seemed capable of making a room kinder simply by entering it. He was capable of doing good still. She could see the laborer's wife smiling beside the gate. She could also see Jalen, in Corven's face, waiting beside Yara for the road escort to be made ready.

"You should be pleased," Luceran said when he joined her.

"I am pleased for the people who have been paid."

"That sounds like a correction."

"It is a distinction."

He had earned the day's praise. That made the thing he had done to Corven harder to speak of, not easier. He wanted her to share the moment without setting the murdered man between them. She wanted him to understand that the man's absence was already there.

"Will you come to supper?" he asked.

"Not to celebrate his return."

"We are celebrating the grain."

"Then do not seat him beneath another man's name."

Luceran looked toward Jalen. The body had begun to hurt after the long standing. Yara offered a chair; Jalen accepted it without pretending Corven would have done the same. Rhyse checked the names of the living escort. The physician's bag was packed. Dols would make its own record when they arrived.

"I will not ask him to sign," Luceran said.

Miren did not thank him for declining another theft. She stayed beside him while the last of the wages were counted, and left before supper.

That evening Damaris placed the northern receipt in the Crown book. Six sacks had reached the posts; the other six still waited at Edselsto pending the one-wagon Dols inspection and repair. The smith had been paid, the roof's exposed edge finished, and the guttering remained on the account. The miller's wall was not yet accepted. Teth had granted a possible future hire, not a ferry. Merehaven's second advance remained withheld. The withdrawn patrol horses had not returned. There were still hamlets threatened by forage demands.

At first light Jalen departed toward Dols under living escort, with Yara carrying the distinct warrants and no false signature. He went beyond the rooms where Edselsto could answer a king's question.

Luceran stood in the family nave before the seven candles. He could hear the last carts leaving the yard and, faintly, the men who had received their wages laughing on the village road. The castle had been useful that day. It had not delivered a sack, repaired a wall, signed a receipt or persuaded a wife to trust a stranger.

All seven candles burned.
