# Season Two, Episode 9 — The Obstructing Envoy

Corven Sareth had been warned that the king's castle listened through its walls.

He had not been warned that the road to it would eat a wheel.

The axle cracked on the last turn above the village, where thaw water had found its way beneath a patch of new stone. The leading coach lurched, a trunk slid from the roof, and Corven's secretary caught the ink case against her chest with a noise that suggested she had saved a child from drowning.

"That," she said, "was the copy with the seals."

"Then I am pleased we have saved the seals," Corven replied. "The rest of the delegation can be replaced at considerably less expense."

Yara Fen gave him the look she reserved for jokes made before breakfast.

Corven climbed down into the mud. He was a broad-shouldered man with a narrow beard, a bad left knee, and a habit of examining broken things as though they had made an argument he was obliged to answer. His boots were good foreign leather. He regretted them immediately.

The wheelwright who came up from the village was young and furious with the road. He had been repairing the same turn for three mornings while the castle's supply carts undid his work each afternoon. The coach could be made safe by noon, he said, if someone would fetch a replacement pin and if the foreigners would stop asking whether the road was fit for a king.

"I am not asking that," Corven said. "I am asking whether it is fit for grain."

The wheelwright pointed downhill at two loaded wagons waiting behind a quarry cart.

"Not with that stone on it."

"Then we have discovered our first matter of state."

Yara had already taken out a notebook. She wrote the wheelwright's name, the damaged turn, and the number of carts delayed. Corven told her to add that the man had been willing to speak plainly to a stranger.

"That is not a treaty provision," she said.

"No. It is why I may believe the rest of his evidence."

Above them Edselsto stood dark against the pines. It was less magnificent than the sketches in the capital and much more alive. Smoke climbed from newly roofed cottages. Timber lay stacked on the village green. Men carried stone across a scaffold where an old wall was being made into a storehouse. A blacksmith's hammer sounded from a low shed with a roof that had not yet learned to keep out rain.

Corven had negotiated with kings who thought their capitals existed by decree. Here the decree had plainly arrived before the nails.

A mounted Doorwarden came down to meet the party, apologized for the road, and offered a second coach.

"His Majesty sends his welcome," the officer said.

"Tell His Majesty we accept it. Tell him also that his road has detained us without taking a hostage, which I consider an improvement on some borders."

The officer did not smile until he was turning his horse.

Yara watched him go.

"Do you intend to make jokes at the king?"

"Only if he proves capable of hearing them."

"That is precisely what I am afraid of."

Corven looked toward the castle.

"So am I."

---

Rhyse had been given a list of the foreign party's requirements. It was shorter than the lists he usually received from noble guests and considerably harder to satisfy.

The envoy required a chamber without silver listening ways, a second room for his secretary, a place for his own sealed correspondence, access to the kitchens through an identified servant, and an escort whose guards could be named. He would not accept the king's word that the rooms were private. He wanted to see the stone.

"How does he propose to see through a wall?" Rhyse asked.

Vess, standing beside the south-gallery drawing, tapped the exposed ends of the severed silver bridge.

"He does not need to. He can see where we have taken it out."

"That proves one break."

"It proves the one break we have surveyed. I have never claimed it proves every inch of this mountain."

Miren had come to watch the preparations. The withdrawing room had been hers before it was offered to the delegation; not by ownership, but by the unhappy history that had made it necessary.

"Give them the drawing," she said.

Ysabet looked up.

"The drawing marks the adjacent passages."

"Then let them see those too. A guest who knows where the guards stand may feel safer than a guest who suspects the guards are behind every wall."

Rhyse considered the risk. A drawing could also tell an enemy where a guard did not stand.

"We shall show the room and its immediate approaches," he said. "Not the whole watch."

"That is a boundary," Miren replied. "They may refuse it. At least they will know what we are offering."

Yara Fen arrived with a small brass probe, a foreign mason named Pellan Orve, and a written authority bearing Corven's seal. Orve examined the silver ends without touching them. He asked where the missing length had gone. Vess told him it had been melted into Crown stock. Orve asked who witnessed that. Ysabet produced the record.

"Can the house still hear through stone without silver?" Orve asked.

"We do not know every way it hears," Vess said. "We know this route was connected. We cut it. We have not observed it hearing through this room since."

Yara's pen stopped.

"That is not the assurance we requested."

"It is the assurance I can prove," Vess answered.

The foreign mason looked at him with something like approval.

They checked the window, the door, the hearth, the adjoining passage and the place where a serving hatch had once opened into a disused pantry. The hatch had been bricked up long before the silver was cut. Orve sounded the mortar with his knuckles. Rhyse allowed one of his men to stand outside each approach while the foreigners spoke in the room. Neither guard could hear more than the scrape of furniture.

"Men can listen at doors," Yara said.

"Yes," Rhyse replied. "And men can be dismissed, questioned, punished or made to swear what they heard."

"Your castle cannot?"

"No."

The difference settled over the room more heavily than any promise.

Yara asked whether Corven's own attendants might stand inside while the Doorwardens kept the outer corridor.

"Provided they do not move into the royal archive or take down the door," Rhyse said.

"And if your king comes himself?"

"I shall announce him."

"You would stop him?"

"I would ask him to wait. His Majesty has authorized me to challenge him in a danger. He has not authorized me to imprison him for impoliteness."

Miren almost smiled. The distinction was painfully familiar.

Yara signed the inspection sheet, but added a line in her own hand: *Physical isolation observed; the nature and full extent of the castle's hearing remain unproved.*

Ysabet let the line stand.

When Corven later read it, he said, "Good. A room with an honest uncertainty is preferable to a room with a dishonest guarantee."

He asked for the room.

---

The first audience began with a dispute over chairs.

House Lethayne's representative had placed Corven at Luceran's right, as custom required for an envoy of his commission. A lesser foreign delegate complained that the position belonged to the oldest city of the Three Estuaries League, not to the man temporarily carrying its authority.

Corven heard the complaint and stood.

"If the chair is the matter we have crossed the mountains to settle, I am prepared to surrender it. It has an alarming crack in the leg."

The lesser delegate flushed. Luceran invited him to examine the chair. He did not.

Corven sat again.

It was not an effortless triumph. Luceran saw Yara write down the complaint. The envoy had made the man look foolish in public, and foolish men sometimes became expensive in private.

"Your Majesty," Corven said, "I bring the votes of three river benches only where their warrants agree. I do not bring their obedience."

"I had not asked for obedience."

"Then we begin pleasantly."

Damaris laid out the Crown's need. The northern garrisons had to be provisioned before the winter roads closed. Red Ford could carry a portion of the movement if its survey and repairs held. Northbridge timber was promised but not yet in place. Grain purchased from Crown estates would feed the soldiers only by taking too much from the towns that supplied them. The League's barges could move grain, salt and fodder by a water route that avoided the weakest roads.

The numbers were not theatrical. Damaris spoke of draft teams, spoiled sacks, river depths, the time required to unload a barge into wagons, and the fact that a horse could not eat a royal order.

Corven listened without interruption.

"You have described why you need our barges," he said. "You have not described why we should send them."

"Payment," Luceran said.

"Payment is one reason. There are others."

He produced three sealed instructions. One from Merehaven, whose merchants would extend short credit against named Crown receipts. One from Dols, whose bridge council would permit civilian cargo but feared troops crossing behind it. One from Teth, whose ferrymen wanted a guarantee that no Crown captain would seize their boats in an emergency and offer an apology after the boats had been burned.

"Those are separate questions," Luceran said.

"They are separate governments. That is the inconvenience of asking a league for help."

"Your commission is to negotiate."

"Yes. Not to sell the rights of three cities for a supper and a ribbon."

Rhyse shifted in his chair. A river passage useful for grain would also be useful for soldiers. He would not accept a foreign inspection of every royal movement near the border. An officer who knew the garrison's stores, the roads and the watch could infer too much from a tally.

Corven acknowledged the objection before Rhyse made it.

"Your captains cannot publish their dispositions to every toll keeper. My councils cannot permit armed men to call every crossing an emergency and carry off boats at will. Both concerns are real. Neither disappears because we dislike the other."

"What do you propose?" Luceran asked.

"Named crossings, sealed cargo rolls, a distinction between grain and troops, and compensation held where both parties can reach it. For the military passage you seek, I require a countersign from our bridge council. For the boats you might take in peril, I require an account that can be enforced when the peril has passed."

"By a foreign court?"

"By a jointly named hearing. You may appoint a judge who distrusts us. We shall do the same. The third must be someone neither of us owns."

Luceran leaned back.

"You ask me to let a foreign magistrate examine the actions of my captains."

"I ask you to make a promise worth more than the paper upon which it is written."

The words were courteous. The refusal beneath them was not.

A Lethayne courtier suggested the League might accept a royal assurance under the great seal. Corven turned toward him.

"A royal assurance is precisely what we are trying to make useful. If it were already sufficient, Your Majesty would not need me."

Luceran did not dismiss him.

He asked for the actual quantities the League could move without taking bread from its own towns. Corven gave ranges rather than a boast, and named the autumn market weeks when the barges would be occupied with their own harvest. Damaris asked about storage at Merehaven. Yara supplied a tally with losses from the previous year's floods. Rhyse asked how many guards the League would allow beside the cargo. The lesser delegate objected again, this time with figures of his own.

By the end of the audience, no one had been insulted beyond repair.

No one had agreed to the treaty.

That was worse than an easy quarrel. It meant the disagreement would have to be worked through.

---

Hesta Vale met Yara Fen beside the east receiving yard, where a man was trying to persuade a mule that a new plank bridge had always been part of the road.

Yara had come to see the cart ground. Corven had asked her to discover whether the castle could unload what the Crown wanted to buy. The envoy's papers contained a generous figure for transport. The road outside his coach had suggested a less generous truth.

Hesta watched her measure the turning space.

"You can fit six grain carts there," Hesta said. "Seven if you do not intend to turn them round."

"Who are you?"

"Someone who intends to turn her carts round."

Yara introduced herself. Hesta introduced her family's quarry bargain, the four seasons of Crown-track passage, and the wheel fee she had recently forced the household to return.

"Four pennies?" Yara asked.

"Four wheels. It was a very learned calculation."

Yara laughed despite herself.

The two women walked the yard. Hesta knew which approach flooded when the thaw came, which wall was held by timber that had not yet been paid for, and why the newly closed service recess had slowed the lime carriers. Yara knew how long damp grain could sit under canvas before its owner began counting losses instead of sacks.

They found common ground in the fact that both disliked men who promised passage without counting what must pass.

"If the League sends the barges," Yara said, "will the quarry carts have to move aside?"

"Not without another argument."

"Then perhaps I should tell my envoy that your agreement is older than his proposed agreement."

"You should tell him that our road is not empty land on a map."

Hesta hesitated, then showed Yara the signed Crown-track bargain. It allowed military closure. It did not allow a foreign factor to displace the quarry traffic for private convenience. A temporary grain convoy would need loading hours and a route that did not break the four-season promise.

Yara copied the relevant line.

"You are giving a foreign delegation information about a royal road," Hesta observed.

"You have given it to me."

"It is my bargain. I may show it to a woman who is measuring where her carts will stand."

"Then I shall write that as well."

Hesta considered her.

"Does your envoy mean to stop the grain?"

"He means to keep his councils from discovering, after the grain has passed, that their boats and laws were taken with it."

"I have met men who said much the same about tolls."

"Did they win?"

"Sometimes. Mostly they learned to keep copies."

Before parting, Hesta asked whether the League's barges could carry cut bridge stone on their empty return passage. Yara did not promise anything. She asked for the weights and the proposed landing.

That evening Hesta would send her brother to measure the quarry's dressed blocks. The possibility mattered to her whether or not the king ever signed a treaty. A new buyer might keep their crews employed after the castle's urgent repairs ended.

Yara returned to Corven with a less convenient cargo plan and a more credible one.

She had also learned that the royal court's rules about witnesses were not mere ornament. Hesta had already used them to get her money back.

---

Luceran found Miren in the withdrawing room after supper.

She had not been waiting for him. A folded letter lay on the table beside a small bowl of pears, and she had the weary expression of someone who had spent the afternoon being asked to interpret the intentions of men who could have spoken to one another.

"Did the envoy charm you?" she asked.

"He made my council laugh while he refused me."

"That is an expensive talent."

"He is not wrong about the boats."

"Nor is Rhyse wrong about the soldiers."

"I know."

Miren cut a pear and offered him half. He took it. The gesture was ordinary enough to hurt.

"The castle could tell me where the foreign attendants go," he said.

She stopped cutting.

"Could it?"

"Only in the connected passages. It might tell me who met whom, or whether someone carried letters to the wrong room."

"That is not what you want to know."

He looked at the fruit in his hand.

"I want to know what Sareth will accept."

"Then ask him."

"He will not tell me everything."

"Of course not. Neither will Damaris, if you ask her what she can spare before she knows what you intend to spend."

"He has three cities behind him and three reasons to say no. I have soldiers who need food."

"And a minister who can count it. A captain who can defend them. A secretary who knows which promises you have already made. You are not without people."

He put the pear down.

"People can refuse."

Miren regarded him for a moment.

"Yes."

He knew how easily the word might have become an accusation between them. She did not make it one.

"Have you forgiven me?" he asked, and regretted the question as soon as he heard its shape.

"No."

He nodded.

"Do you still wish me here?"

She moved closer.

"At this table? Yes."

She rested her hand on his sleeve, away from the scar the knife had left. He wanted to lean into her and have the difficult day reduced to the warmth of her palm. He did lean, a little. She allowed it.

"I am afraid of losing the agreement," he said.

"Then be afraid. It is not a royal failure to be afraid of a real cost."

"And if I could know the answer before he gave it?"

"You would know what he said when he thought you could not hear. You would not know what his three councils would do when he went home."

He kissed her forehead. It was a familiar liberty, freely offered and freely received. He did not ask her to promise anything about the future.

After a while she said, "Ardel sent the road answer. It has been delivered to the proper clerk."

"Did she tell you her winter plans?"

"She told me something of them."

He waited.

Miren did not continue.

For once he found the silence easier to bear.

Outside the room, a Doorwarden changed watch. The silver break remained open in the wall. The privacy between them was not a gift of the castle. It was a boundary men and women had chosen to keep, with inconvenience attached.

Luceran left without calling Edselsto.

---

Corven's secretary found him writing to his wife by candlelight.

The letter began with the broken coach wheel and an account of the king's half-repaired village. It contained a description of a supper at which the cook had served an excellent onion pie and an alarming amount of rosemary. It did not contain the terms of the negotiation, which were written separately under seal.

"Leora will accuse you of going abroad only to criticize other people's kitchens," Yara said.

"She knows I am right about rosemary."

"She says you are wrong about everything that grows."

"That is why our marriage has endured."

Corven sanded the page.

Yara laid her notes beside him. The cart ground could not take the entire proposed grain movement in a single arrival. The Crown would need staged unloading, protected storage and agreed priority over its existing quarry traffic. The League could make money carrying bridge stone back downriver, but the arrangement would have to be contracted separately from the treaty.

"Good," he said.

"Good? It makes our offer smaller."

"It makes our offer possible."

Yara sat opposite him.

"The king listened."

"He did."

"He also looked as though he wanted to throw you out of a window."

"That is sometimes what listening looks like."

She was not amused.

"The benches may refuse your compromise even if he accepts it."

"They may."

"And Merehaven's lenders will blame you for the lost business."

"Quite possibly."

"Why do you keep asking for the hearing clause? We could get the grain through with a handsome bond and a royal seal."

Corven folded his letter.

"Because the first boat taken under a broad emergency order will be taken by an officer who believes he has a good reason. The boat's owner will have no remedy until the emergency is over, and perhaps not then. If we cannot give the owner a place to make his claim, the next captain will call the next inconvenience an emergency. That is how a privilege becomes a custom."

Yara looked toward the door.

"You think the king would do that?"

"I think the king might forbid it. I think he might sincerely mean the prohibition. I think one frightened captain might ignore him when the road is blocked and men are hungry. A promise that depends on every future captain being virtuous is not a promise I can take home."

He touched the sealed warrants.

"Nor may I trade Dols's bridge right because Merehaven wants a profit. They sent me together. They did not become one people on the journey."

Yara opened her notebook again.

"Then what do you want tomorrow?"

"An honest refusal, if that is what he has. A smaller agreement, if he can bear it. And I want him to understand that I am not punishing him."

"Will you tell him that?"

"Perhaps. I suspect he would hear it as a greater insult than the refusal."

When she had gone, Corven added a line to his letter home. Leora's mother had been ill before he left. He asked after her sleep, and whether the old woman had finally agreed to let someone else carry coal upstairs. He wrote that the king was younger than the stories made him sound, and that his advisers were not fools.

He did not write that the castle frightened him.

Leora would know from the care with which he described its doors.

---

The second audience took place in the receiving yard, not the council chamber.

Damaris had asked to show the envoy the actual space through which the grain must pass. Rhyse had asked to show him the guard route. Vess wanted to demonstrate why a temporary timber landing could be built beside the east wall but not against the weakened foundation. Hesta arrived with the dimensions of her quarry blocks and the stubborn certainty that no foreign treaty could erase her family's road.

The day was cold enough that breath hung above the carts.

Corven walked the proposed route with Luceran. He did not wear his ceremonial chain. Luceran had left his crown inside.

"Your Majesty," Corven said, "I can offer the first grain passage under the civilian cargo provisions, with named wagons, named barges and a bond deposited before loading. I can recommend a short credit term to Merehaven. I cannot promise military crossing without the bridge council's countersign."

"Not even if my garrisons are in peril?"

"Especially then. That is when the right will be tested."

Rhyse's jaw tightened.

"The bridge council might refuse until the road is lost."

"It might," Corven said. "You may send your captain to make the case. I will not pretend my people have surrendered a right because it is inconvenient to yours."

"Would you surrender it if the threat were yours?" Luceran asked.

"No. I would ask you to help me bear the cost of keeping it."

It was a better answer than Luceran wanted.

Damaris brought out the revised tally. A first movement of grain could be made without claiming the full route, provided the wagons were staggered and a storehouse was roofed before the autumn rain. The Crown could pay for the first convoy from its defensible treasury share. Later convoys would need either credit or a different source of money.

"That is relief," Luceran said. "Not security."

"Relief is often what can be purchased before security," Damaris replied.

Corven pointed to the road.

"I am willing to be the man who recommends this to the benches. I am not willing to be the man who tells them their consent was unnecessary."

Luceran studied him.

"You know I can find other routes."

"Yes."

"Other lenders."

"Yes."

"Other allies."

"I hope you do. A king with only one road is a danger to everyone who owns it."

There was no triumph in the answer. Corven seemed genuinely to wish the king had more choices.

Luceran turned to Rhyse.

"Send a captain to inspect the crossing under the League's escort. No troop movement. He is to report what it would take to make a military passage lawful and useful."

Rhyse bowed.

"Yes, Your Majesty."

"Ysabet, prepare a letter asking the benches to receive him. No promise of a treaty. Damaris, price the first convoy and the roof before you release coin."

Corven inclined his head.

"Then we have a beginning."

"We have a beginning of a beginning."

"That is the usual length of diplomacy."

A porter came through the arch with a stack of planks and waited for the royal party to clear his way. Luceran stepped aside. So did Corven. The porter looked astonished to find two sovereign representatives making room for him.

Hesta caught Yara's eye across the yard.

The women had already begun discussing return cargo.

The envoy's refusal had not stopped all work. It had divided the work into things that could be done, things that required consent, and things nobody had yet agreed to risk.

Luceran disliked the division. He understood it.

---

After the foreigners withdrew to their inspected room, Edselsto's mask appeared in the outer gallery.

"Your Majesty. The visitors have passed through the east receiving arch."

"I saw them."

"They spoke there."

Luceran looked at the black ear tile. The passage was marked. The report was not, in itself, forbidden.

"Was there an alarm?"

"No."

"Then I have no inquiry."

The pale face inclined toward him.

"I could speak of their words."

He thought of the crossing. Of the hungry garrisons. Of the half-built storehouse. Of Corven Sareth smiling as he refused to surrender a right that belonged to other people.

He thought of how much easier it would be to know which of the three cities might be turned against the others.

"No," he said.

"As you command."

The mask vanished.

Luceran stood in the corridor for a while, annoyed by the sense that he had left a useful tool untouched. There had been no struggle, no threat, no consequence anyone could see. The temptation was made worse by its smallness.

In the guest room beyond the severed silver, Corven was reading Yara's revised cargo notes. He crossed out a figure, wrote a smaller one, and asked her to send the amended estimate home by the first safe rider. He did not know the king had been offered his words. He would not have believed a promise of privacy that required the king to be generous every hour of every day.

In the family nave, Canon Merel's keeper tended seven steady flames.

All seven burned.

The king had not been defeated by an enemy.

He had met a man who could say no, and whose no had a government behind it.

By morning the price of that refusal would begin to appear in the accounts.
