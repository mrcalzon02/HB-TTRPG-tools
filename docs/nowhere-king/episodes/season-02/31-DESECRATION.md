# Season Two, Episode 31 — Desecration

Etta placed the receiving book at the entrance to the small audience chamber. The two Dols witnesses had traveled with separate records, and she refused to let a royal seal make their accounts indistinguishable. Senna could attest to the date of the king's letter. Harven could attest to what he had witnessed at the miller's lane. Neither claimed knowledge of events outside his or her presence.

The kitchen staff brought water. A servant went back for dry charcoal. The room was cold, and the business before it would take hours. Etta paid attention to such things because testimony was given by tired people who needed to eat and sit down, not by documents floating without owners.

Senna had spent the ride rehearsing how she would answer a sovereign who called a receipt an insult. She had no wish to provoke him. Her reeve had sent her because she knew the difference between accepting a letter and believing it, a distinction that had saved the bridge court from more than one quarrel between merchants. Harven was less practiced at argument. He had helped clear the lane and still remembered the frightened apprentice. He hoped the audience would end before he was asked to describe the boy's face to strangers. Neither considered himself a hero. They were people carrying records whose dates would matter after the people in the room had changed their minds.

Etta had seen a false seal before, once on a petition for roof timber, and had watched a good man lose wages while the clerks argued over which office should investigate it. This seal was genuine. That made the question harder. A truthful copy could preserve a false claim perfectly. She ruled a narrow line down the page to keep each witness's capacity separate from the words recorded. The device was no innovation. It was the same practice her predecessor had used when neighbors disagreed over a boundary stone. The scale of the disagreement had changed; the need for a legible record had not.

---



Jalen chose to attend, but only after Rhyse promised he could leave whenever he wished. The physicians arranged a supported chair. Yara took her place within his sight and made no claim that her three warrants entitled her to decide his movements.

Rhyse reported that an old casualty roll mentioned the name Orris. The original discharge and payment receipt were still missing. Jalen asked him not to describe the search as complete.

"My sister deserves an answer that can be checked," he said.

Rhyse agreed. The service Jalen had offered the Crown did not end his own affairs or turn them into royal property.

The physicians had warned Jalen that the journey to the audience would exhaust him. He had accepted the cost because he feared being spoken about in a room from which he was absent. When a porter offered to cover the carrying chair with a royal cloth, he refused. It was a generous offer, perhaps even an attempt at dignity, but the cloth bore the arms of the office he no longer claimed. He wanted a plain covering and his own name. Yara saw how much effort it took him to make these small decisions. She had once treated him chiefly as a complication among three warrants. Now she understood that each question of his custody was a question about a person who might reasonably want to live beyond the use anyone found for him.

Isera did not promise Jalen that the physicians would agree after the hearing. She told him that her observations were available for another competent person to examine and challenge. He found comfort in that, though it offered no remedy. It meant the account did not belong to whoever paid for the physician. The Crown physician admitted that he had never examined the earlier marks she described. They had learned different things at different times. Jalen insisted that both be written. He could bear uncertainty more easily than having another person decide which parts of him counted as evidence and which could be left out.

---



Ysabet showed the king the original private instruction and the later denial. Damaris had not known of the earlier order. She examined the seals, then the dates.

"You asked me to defend a truthful grain account while keeping this from me," she said.

"The grain was delivered."

"Yes. That receipt may be the only royal paper a foreign factor still trusts."

Miren declined to lend the king her presence as an endorsement. Rhyse insisted that the foreign visitors retain safe conduct. Ysabet proposed a witnessed copy of the older instruction, with its original still traceable in her custody.

Luceran considered the alternatives. A second denial would ask more people to put their names beside words he knew could be contradicted. He authorized Ysabet to read the instruction.

Damaris had expected the king to conceal a weakness in the northern negotiations. She had not expected him to conceal the source of the negotiations themselves. Her anger was not only moral. She could picture the carter's unpaid waiting hours, the northern posts expecting another delivery, and the factor who would now demand a second witness for every figure. Those were people who had done their work properly. Luceran asked whether she would continue to manage the accounts. She said she would, provided she could mark which assurances were hers and which belonged to the Crown alone. He had never before heard a trusted ally require that distinction so plainly.

Luceran asked Ysabet whether an admission would leave him any means to govern. She answered that he would still be king tomorrow, but not because the witnesses forgave him. The roof would need fixing, soldiers would need paying, and the houses would continue calculating what service they could afford. His office survived a shame his reputation might not. That answer angered him more than a threat of deposition. He had imagined his authority and the faith of those closest to him as one thing. Ysabet's reply separated them. Damaris watched him discover a distinction she had lived with in every contract she had signed.

---



Senna presented the dated receipt. Harven described only the events he had witnessed. The physicians kept their observations separate and refused to supply certainty about what they had not seen.

Ysabet read the royal instruction. Senna asked Luceran whether he acknowledged it.

"I gave that order," he said. "My later denial was false."

Aldren copied his words. Etta recorded the hour and the people present.

Jalen asked to speak. He distinguished his voluntary service from any right to claim Corven's personal relationships or public authority.

"I can answer for my own consent," he said. "I cannot answer for his."

The Dols witnesses requested attested copies and a way to compare them with the original. Ysabet agreed to a recorded process, not an unmarked transfer of custody. Senna and Harven would report to their reeve, not announce a verdict of their own.

The silence after Luceran spoke lasted long enough for Etta to hear a shutter knock against the wall. A courtier began to object that the audience had no authority to question a king. Senna answered that she had asked a question, not issued a command. Rhyse asked the courtier to be silent. Jalen did not look at the objector. He watched Aldren write, because a word lost in a copy could later become a different truth. Luceran asked that the record state his acknowledgment in the first person. Ysabet agreed. She did not praise him. A confession made after a false denial was an event to preserve, not a service for which the people injured by the lie owed him thanks.

Senna's clerkly training kept her voice steady, but she was not indifferent. Her reeve had asked for an explanation of an envoy's doubtful authority; she was returning with a king's acknowledgment that his earlier answer had been false. She knew the reeve would need the original source offered for comparison, the date of each statement and the names of those who heard it. She did not know what punishment, if any, another court might lawfully demand. Harven would have preferred to leave immediately. He stayed because he had promised to account for the separate accident and would not let another person's admission change the boundaries of what he had seen.

---



Yara prepared separate notices for Dols, Merehaven and Teth. Each had its own bench and interests. No message could reach all three at once.

The Merehaven factor agreed that the first witnessed grain receipt remained valid. He withheld the second advance pending instructions. The wall at Dols was still unaccepted, and the second wagon remained stopped. Damaris entered the lawful waiting pay and the fodder consumed while the team stood idle.

Rhyse preserved the foreign witnesses' right to leave. Aldren and Etta signed only for copying and receipt. The king's acknowledgment did not give them knowledge of the earlier event.

The factor's position was narrow but real. His masters had pledged against a delivered load, and he would not permit the Crown's scandal to turn an honest carrier's receipt into waste paper. He also would not advance money against a future passage that Dols had not opened. Damaris recognized a competent opponent in him. She offered a fresh witnessed inventory of the six sacks in storage, not a claim that the river benches would cooperate. He accepted the inventory as evidence of goods, not security for payment. They parted with no bargain, but with one fact both could defend. Outside, the carter fed horses that had been waiting through another day of rain.

At the receiving yard the six waiting sacks were inspected for damp. Two had to be raised from the stone floor onto fresh boards, and a cooper repaired a loose hoop on a storage barrel. Damaris paid the workers from the same constrained account that had bought the first northern delivery. The men asked whether the next wagon would depart. She said no date had been authorized. It was a poor answer and the only defensible one. The workers could plan around uncertainty; they could not plan around a promise made because a king disliked uncertainty.

---



Leora received a notice sent before the audience. It described the accident and Jalen's return, not the king's new statement. She stored it separately from her old letters and the earlier private-question reply.

Her representative's report from the village watch had not yet arrived. She asked the steward to inquire whether Jalen could receive a letter in his own name.

Her mother needed broth. Her niece was repairing the ribbon horse. Leora tended to both before sealing her request. The rest of the world could wait for a courier without becoming less real.

Leora's niece asked why the steward had sealed another letter when the last one had brought no comfort. Leora considered saying that adults must sometimes write because they cannot act. Instead she said the letter would ask a question no one else could answer for her. The child wanted to know whether Corven would come home. Leora did not pretend to know. She had learned that a gentle false answer could become a promise the child would carry for years. After supper she checked the lock on the chest of original letters. Her representative had not yet returned, and no court's newest news had traveled backward along the road to reach her before it was sent.

The steward suggested sending Leora's next letter under the family seal, which might compel a quicker answer from Dols. She considered the idea and refused. She wanted a named receiving clerk and a dated reply, not a display of rank that might make a frightened witness say what he thought a noble widow wished to hear. Her mother called from the next room. Leora put the pen down and went to her. In that small interruption she felt how much of marriage had consisted of shared, ordinary obligations. No account of extraordinary events could replace the person who had once carried the broth while she settled the child's ribbons.

---



Miren found Luceran above the cistern where two masons were measuring a blocked overflow.

"I am glad you spoke plainly," she said.

"Does it change us?"

"It changes what I can say you did today."

He understood what she had left unsaid.

The mason asked Luceran whether he wanted the overflow opened immediately. The king almost said yes. Then he saw the chalk marks showing where the old stone had shifted, and asked how the mason intended to support it. Two men and a half-day's labor would clear the visible obstruction; any deeper work would require a second inspection. He authorized only the first task. The decision was ordinary, but he found himself relieved to make it. Edselsto's remembered channel might help later. For now the living workers had measured something the castle had not seen. Their questions were tiresome, expensive and necessary. Miren's absence hurt more because he understood the same was true of her refusal.

Later, in a connected passage, Edselsto answered a narrow question about an older drainage route. The castle remembered a former channel but could not inspect its present condition. A living mason would still have to survey it.

The castle's answer came without hesitation, and that was part of its danger. It did not tell Luceran he had been brave to confess. It did not ask whether the Dols witnesses had arrived safely or whether Jalen had eaten. It offered the remembered route, a useful fact bounded by old stone and incomplete knowledge. Luceran could have asked for another fact and another. Instead he left the passage and went to the table where Ysabet's copied records waited to dry. He knew he would have to read them in the morning. Edselsto would still be ready when he returned, which was precisely what frightened him least.

In the family nave, all seven candles burned.
