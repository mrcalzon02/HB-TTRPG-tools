# Season Two, Episode 12 — Below the Wine Cellars

The cellar keeper had been counting barrels for twenty-six years, and she did not care whether the king's ancestors had buried a treasure beneath them.

Talla Rusk cared about the two casks that had begun to taste of wet timber, the north wall that sweated when the weather turned, and the floor drain that would not take a bucket of water quickly enough. She had kept the old wine dry through the years when there was barely a household to drink it. Now the castle was full of people who called for wine at inconvenient hours and expected the cellar to have become larger with the court.

Vess stood beside the lowest rack with a measuring rod. Pell Falbrecht was on his knees examining a patch of newer mortar.

"You can have that wall," Talla told them. "You cannot have the whole room."

"We haven't asked for the room," Vess said.

"Men with rods generally do."

The mason had found a seam behind a stack of empty racks. Its mortar differed from the wall above it, and a slight depression in the floor ran beneath the barrels toward a blocked stair. Talla remembered her predecessor calling the place the old cold way. He had never opened it. A cellar was not improved by discovering more ways for damp air to enter.

Pell scraped a pinch of mortar onto his thumb. "Someone closed this after the main wall was built. Not yesterday, and not in the same manner as the repairs over our heads."

"Is it holding anything up?" Talla asked.

"That is the question I must answer before anyone pulls out a stone."

Vess wanted a sightline down the blocked stair, not an adventure. He marked where the racks could be shifted without losing access to the drain and where a temporary brace would stand if the mortar proved loose. Talla required the cellar inventory to be witnessed before her people moved a single cask. A broken seal could become a charge of theft, and she had seen men lose wages for less.

"One aisle," she said. "And if the air spoils my wine, the King's Works will buy what it ruins."

Pell looked at Vess. "Write that down."

For once, the first argument about a buried passage concerned the price of wine.

---

Damaris had the receiving-yard roof in one column and the northern posts' grain in another when Vess brought his request.

"Three days," he said. "Six workers below, two Doorwardens on the stair, a surveyor who knows how to draw a section rather than a handsome map, and enough rope and oil to come back by our own light. Pell will not let us open the blocked way without timber for a brace."

"And if you find another blocked way?"

"We stop and ask again."

Damaris studied the figures. The yard's third footing had been accepted, but the remaining beams still required paid hands and dry weather. The limited Tervain purchase was contracted and undelivered. Jorvan's narrower offer against two market-gate tolls lay unanswered beside the cost of repairing those same gates. There was no treasury in which every urgent request could be satisfied by turning over another chest.

"The Crown can pay for a look," she said. "It cannot pay for an indefinite descent."

Luceran had joined them without the silver mask. He had been asking for the northern culvert to be inspected by ordinary surveyors; Vess had sent two men to find its surface approach, but neither had yet returned with a condition report. The king wanted both inquiries pursued.

"Could the old stair tell us where the culvert runs?" he asked.

"Perhaps," Vess said. "Or perhaps it will tell us only where a stair once went. I will not sell you a connection I have not measured."

Pell spread a rough drawing across the table. The upper cellar wall was load-bearing. Opening the blocked arch without a brace might damage the wine vault and the kitchen passage above it. The king's wish to learn was not a reason to endanger people eating supper.

Damaris took the cost of three days' wages, braces, rope, lamps and the surveyor from the discretionary works purse. That meant the north parapet's decorative stone dressing would wait. It did not take coin already promised for the roof, battle compensation, the Doorwardens' pay or the surveyed silver-conduit work. She wrote the boundary into the order.

"If they discover something worth another three days, they must come back," she said.

Luceran signed. He did not ask Edselsto to guide them. He could have asked the castle what it remembered of the stair, but its answer about the culvert was already awaiting living verification, and another untested memory would not hold a ceiling up.

Ysabet asked who would keep the drawings.

"Vess and the surveyor each keep a copy," Damaris replied. "Pell signs what he has seen. If one is lost, we do not invent the missing measurements."

The king found that more comforting than he had expected.

---

Corven Sareth noticed the extra guards because they had begun learning his household's habits.

A Doorwarden had stepped aside for Yara's morning letters before she asked. Another knew that Corven walked more slowly after a damp night and no longer tried to hurry him across the polished stones. They were considerate men, but their presence was new, and their orders were evidently particular.

He asked Rhyse about it beside the guest-room hearth.

"Your king has decided to protect me from my own knees?"

"Your knees are better qualified than I am to judge their danger."

"Then from what?"

Rhyse had the sealed account of Edselsto's proposal in his custody. He also had Luceran's signed refusal and a guest whose commission might be lost if the reason for the protection were revealed. Silence was becoming an act with a cost, not a neutral absence of speech.

"There has been a knife attack within this house," he said. "I will not have another guest exposed to an unaccounted approach. Your attendants keep their access, and you are free to leave or refuse an escort."

"That is true," Corven said. "I do not think it is all you know."

"No."

Corven watched him for a moment. He had negotiated with enough captains to recognize a man obeying one obligation at the expense of another.

"I shall not demand your sealed business in the corridor. But if you ever believe my safety depends upon something I have not been allowed to judge, tell me before the choice is gone."

Rhyse gave him no false assurance. He said he would carry those words to the king.

Yara entered with a dispatch. Dols had set a day to walk the western approach with Savelin under civilian survey terms. The council still had not countersigned a military crossing. Teth had named two boatwrights acceptable for the ferry valuation, provided the owners could challenge the figures. Merehaven had sent questions about the roofed store and the convoy bond, not consent.

"Questions are better than silence," Corven said.

"Sometimes," Yara replied. "They still need answers."

Her brother's warehouse could use the work a return cargo might bring. She had not told Corven the full extent of his trouble. She would not falsify a League warrant to help him, but she intended to find a lawful carriage bargain before his creditors sold the barges.

Corven took his cane and went to answer Merehaven. Rhyse returned to his watch with a warning he could no longer pretend had no human voice.

---

Nessa Rell arrived with a slate, a plumb line, and a refusal to use Vess's older floor plan as anything more than a starting guess.

She was a surveyor trained to measure mine galleries for the League of Stone and Beam. Her father had taught her to draw the shape of an opening before deciding what it had been built for. She had spent the previous spring disputing a quarry owner's claim that a retaining wall was sound; the wall had fallen in the rain, fortunately before anyone had been sent beneath it. She would rather be called troublesome than attend another funeral she had warned against.

Talla's casks were counted and shifted under her eye. Pell braced the upper arch. Two workers loosened the later mortar a stone at a time while Vess watched for movement above. When the first gap opened, the air beyond smelled of old dust and damp lime, not decay. A cold draught moved toward the cellar.

Nessa held a candle at the gap, then drew it back. "There is air. That is all the flame tells us."

They widened the opening only enough for a guarded descent. The party was small: Vess, Pell, Nessa, two workers carrying tools and spare oil, and two Doorwardens under Sergeant Dain. A third worker remained at the top with the rope and the agreed signal. No one went below without being named on Talla's inventory sheet, because it was the only list already in the cellar and she would not allow another.

The first stair was narrow and worn on the inner edge. The stonework changed after twelve steps, from dressed blocks to rougher courses that did not align with the wine vault above. Nessa marked the count. Pell sounded the next landing with the butt of a hammer. A loose tread shifted under one worker's boot, and the line at his waist took his weight before he could slide into the dark. His lantern struck the wall and went out.

Dain called the party to a halt. They checked the man's ankle and hands. He could walk, shaken and angry at himself. One lantern was broken; no one called that a trivial loss simply because there were others.

"We can turn back," Vess said.

"Or take the landing from the other side," Nessa answered. "But we do not use that tread again."

They laid a short plank over the gap, lashed it in place, and made the workers test it from above before anyone crossed. Pell marked the failed stone in chalk. Dain sent the injured man's companion back to the top with the broken lantern and a request for a fresh one. The expedition did not proceed until the rope signal was answered.

Below the landing, the passage widened enough for three people abreast. Nessa saw the first narrow opening in the wall and assumed it was a ventilation slit. Then she crouched and found a second opening opposite it, set at the same height, with a shelf of stone below the aperture.

"Do not stand in front of that," Dain said.

He was looking at the other side of the wall. The slits did not face the stairs by which the party had entered. They faced a lower arch and the turn beyond it.

Pell ran his hand over a squared socket beside the nearer slit. There had been an iron fitting there once. The stain remained in the stone, but the iron was gone.

"A shutter?" Vess asked.

"Perhaps. Or a mounting for something that turned. We can say where it sat, not what it carried."

Nessa drew the wall in section. The slits were too low for a standing man to see through comfortably. Someone crouched behind them could watch the descending turn. The narrowest place in the passage lay beyond the slots, not before them. Whatever had been defended, the work seemed arranged to contest movement from farther below toward the upper cellars.

Dain asked whether the passage had been made against miners coming up from a lower quarry. Pell thought it possible. Vess suggested an old fortress arrangement for containing armed men in the lower rooms during a revolt. Nessa wrote both guesses on a separate leaf rather than putting either on the measured drawing.

At the next turn the masonry was older again. A long scar in the stone showed where a heavy door had once hung. There were no hinges left, only sockets and a line of dark rust beneath the lintel. The floor fell away on the far side, not as a cliff but as a second stair whose lower end was lost beyond their light.

Pell examined the lintel. "The weight above this is wrong. The left bearing has opened."

"How far?" Vess asked.

"Far enough that I will not put a man under it until I can shore it from both sides."

Nessa measured what she could from the safe side. She had come to draw a passage, not to prove courage by entering an unsafe one. Dain counted the people, the lamps and the time remaining before the watch change.

They withdrew with the second stair unexplored.

---

Talla found them in her cellar with mud on their knees and one less sound lantern than they had taken.

She counted the party before asking what they had seen. The worker who had slipped was sitting on an upturned crate while a physician's assistant checked his ankle. The swelling was slight, but Pell ordered him off heavy carrying until he could put weight on it without pain. The man's lost shift would be paid from the expedition wages; the accident had occurred in Crown work under Crown direction.

"Did you find my drain?" Talla asked.

"No," Vess said.

"Then the king owes me a drain as well as a new hole."

The opening was boarded for the night and the braces left in place. Talla demanded a proper shutter and a way to keep cold air off the nearest casks before the next descent. Pell agreed. The temporary works would not be left as an invitation to children, thieves or curious courtiers.

Nessa laid her drawing on the long cellar table. Vess could see the defensive arrangement now: a high approach, a lower turn, paired narrow slots, a missing heavy door, and the damaged lintel before another stair. The direction of the slits was the disturbing part. They were not arranged to fire upon anyone trying to enter from the wine cellar.

"They face down," Talla said.

"They face the lower turn," Nessa corrected. "That is what I measured."

"What was down there?"

"I do not know."

Talla considered the answer. She had spent half her life beneath the king's dining rooms without imagining a battle could once have been fought below the wine. Now a stranger had opened her wall and returned with a drawing that made the ordinary floor seem temporary.

"Then do not call it a fortress against demons when you tell the court," she said. "If the king hears a grand name, he will send twenty men through my casks tomorrow."

Pell smiled despite himself. "I was going to call it an unsafe stair."

"Good. That sounds expensive enough."

Nessa kept one copy of the measurements in her own case. She was paid for a drawing, not for surrendering her judgment. Vess did not object. He wanted someone able to tell him when he was wrong.

---

Miren found Luceran studying the sketch in a room where Edselsto could hear him.

He had asked for the drawings, not for an answer from the mask. The paired slits and the lower arch made him think of a castle built to protect its inhabitants from something beneath their feet. He knew the thought was an interpretation, not evidence. It nevertheless sat beside the proposal about Corven, another possibility he wished he had never been given.

"Has anyone been hurt?" Miren asked.

"A worker twisted his ankle. He was caught by the rope. Pell has stopped the descent until the lintel can be supported."

"Then they have already earned their wages."

Luceran showed her the drawing. She studied the orientation of the slots and the empty hinge sockets, then asked whether anyone had measured the lower stair. No one had.

"Edselsto might remember it," he said.

"Perhaps."

"I did not ask."

"That is not the same as never asking again."

He set down the sheet. Miren had not forgiven the stolen conversation from the map gallery. She could be tender with him and still insist that his promise about the deaf room mattered more than his embarrassment.

"I told you what the castle offered about Corven," he said. "I did not tell Damaris. She is paying for the work without knowing why I have become afraid of what the castle says."

"She knows the work costs money. She knows how to refuse an unpriced request. That is her work. The secret is yours, and Ysabet's and Rhyse's, until you decide what you owe the man whose life was discussed."

Luceran reached for her hand. She allowed it, then drew him toward the window so he would stop looking at the map as if it could make the choice for him.

"I am not asking you to praise me for saying no," he said.

"Good. I would have had to disappoint you."

They stood close enough that he could feel her warmth through her sleeve. There were still rooms in the castle where they could be together without making every silence into a royal question. She did not speak of marriage. He did not ask.

Outside, carts moved through the unfinished yard beneath a roof that still did not cover the grain.

---

The following morning Nessa, Pell and Vess stood before Luceran, Damaris, Ysabet and Rhyse with the measurements.

Pell gave the bad news first. The lower lintel needed bracing from a safe side before anyone could pass. Talla's cellar needed a fitted shutter, a drain repair and protection for the wine. The broken lantern must be replaced, and the worker's ankle would be examined again before he returned to the stair. Nessa showed the orientation of the slots without naming the enemy for which they had been built.

"The work seems arranged to resist movement from the lower passage," she said. "That does not tell us who was expected there. A fortress can be made to hold its own garrison in, as readily as an enemy out."

Luceran looked at the drawing for a long time.

"Can we go farther?"

"With money, timber and time," Pell said. "Not by ordering a man under that stone today."

Damaris placed the first three days' account beside the proposed next expense. The north parapet dressing could wait longer, but the roof could not. Nor could the garrisons eat a drawing. She would authorize a second bounded inspection only after Pell produced the brace design and Talla's shutter was fitted. Nessa would retain her independent copy, Vess would keep the Crown copy, and a Doorwarden would witness each entry and withdrawal. No listening silver would be carried below without a separate surveyed authority.

Rhyse added that guest protection would not be reduced to staff the descent. Dain could command the next party only if the ordinary watches remained filled. He had spoken to Corven, who had noticed the extra guards. Rhyse repeated the envoy's warning that safety withheld from the person at risk was a choice made on his behalf.

Ysabet did not open the sealed account in the room. Luceran understood that her silence was not consent to keep it sealed forever.

"Then the survey continues," he said. "Within those limits. We will learn what stone and living people can tell us before asking a wall to tell us what it remembers."

The order established a small paid undercroft survey under the King's Works, with separate drawings, a named watch, a limit on expenditure and a return to council before deeper entry. It did not establish what lay beneath the second stair. It did not provide a new way to move grain, nor did it give the king a military countersign at Dols.

Corven was alive in his rooms, writing to Leora between dispatches to the three river benches. Hesta and Yara had not yet signed their carriage bargain. The roof was still unfinished, Tervain's sacks were still on their estate, and the northern culvert had not been proved passable. The castle had been useful without being asked to make another promise.

In the family nave, all seven ancestral candles burned.
