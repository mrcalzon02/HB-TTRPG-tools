# Season Two, Episode 4 — Silver in the Walls

Vess found the first argument before breakfast.

It was six inches long, black with age, and buried behind the plaster of Lady Miren's dressing room.

He stood on a stool with one sleeve rolled past his elbow while a mason held the lamp and a clerk tried not to breathe dust onto the inventory sheet.

The silver line ran horizontally through the wall.

Not bright silver. Not court silver. It was almost charcoal beneath the lime, flattened into a narrow channel and pinned to the stone with little black nails.

Vess touched neither metal nor nail.

"Bridge," he said.

The mason leaned closer.

"Where?"

"Here."

He pointed with the wooden end of his measuring rod.

A thumb-wide interruption separated one length from the next. Between them sat two empty sockets.

The clerk wrote.

Miren, still in a robe because they had arrived at an indecent hour by her standards and a perfectly reasonable one by Vess's, said, "And if you put silver there?"

Vess looked over his shoulder.

"I don't know."

"You have spent four days telling everyone these are listening ways."

"Some are."

"And this one?"

"I don't know."

Miren folded her arms.

"Good."

The mason glanced between them.

Vess climbed down.

"That was not the response I expected."

"It is the response you are going to learn to enjoy."

She stepped past him and looked into the opened wall.

"Does Edselsto hear this room now?"

"No."

"Can it?"

Vess looked toward the pale mask waiting in the doorway.

Miren did not.

That was deliberate.

Luceran had authorized structural questions during the survey, but the new rules had made the order of asking almost as important as the answer.

Vess asked the room first.

"Master Hale. Does this channel connect to the north junction?"

The mason scratched his beard.

"Probably."

"Probably is useful. Why?"

"The stone course turns toward it. Unless some bastard jogged it around the flue."

"Good."

Then Vess looked at the mask.

"Edselsto. With the king's standing permission for this survey: where does this line go?"

The mask answered.

"To the north junction."

Miren said, "Does it hear this room?"

"No."

"If the bridge is restored?"

"Yes."

"Then mark it closed."

The clerk hesitated.

Vess did not.

"Closed by occupant request."

The clerk wrote.

The mask said nothing.

Miren finally looked at it.

"Does that trouble you?"

"No."

"Would you prefer to hear?"

"I do not prefer."

She stared at the silver face.

Luceran had told her the same thing after the first rules were written: it did not sulk. It did not bargain. It did not punish caution with wounded pride.

That was part of the problem.

"Convenient," she said.

The mask inclined its head.

Vess packed the opening with temporary cloth instead of plaster.

"Next room."

Miren caught his sleeve.

"Vess."

He turned.

"If anyone bridges that without my knowledge, I want to know who."

He looked at the clerk.

The clerk looked alarmed.

Vess said, "Then we need seals."

Miren smiled without humor.

"Now you understand why this is political."

---

By noon there were twenty-seven red marks on Vess's survey and nine blue ones.

Red meant physically found.

Blue meant Edselsto remembered a route they had not yet exposed.

He refused to let the clerks draw blue lines as solid.

"They are claims," he said.

Damaris looked at the wall map spread across the old breakfast table.

"They are the castle's own walls."

"They are still claims."

Luceran leaned over the map.

"How often has it been wrong?"

"Wrong?"

"About a route."

"Twice."

The mask floated near the shuttered window.

Luceran glanced at it.

Vess put a finger on the first blue line.

"It said this ran straight from the west gallery to the stair junction."

"It does," Edselsto said.

Vess ignored it.

"We opened the wall. The channel had been cut by a chimney repair perhaps sixty years ago."

"I remembered the way."

"Exactly."

Damaris looked at Luceran.

"The distinction is expensive."

Vess pointed to the second.

"This one exists, but someone removed three yards of it when they robbed the old bath fittings. The house remembers continuity. The wall contains a gap."

Luceran rubbed his forehead.

"So we verify every span."

"Every span we intend to use."

"How much?"

Damaris opened her ledger.

Vess visibly regretted the question.

She read without sympathy.

"At present prices, merely surveying the accessible inhabited wings costs less than repairing the south roof and more than feeding the village labor camp for twelve days. Restoration is another matter."

"How much silver?"

"We do not know."

Vess said, "Because we have not surveyed it."

"How much if every blue line were real and every break modest?"

He looked offended.

"That is not a useful number."

"It is useful to a treasurer."

"It is fiction."

"Most budgets begin as fiction."

Luceran sat down.

Miren entered with two cups and gave one to him.

None to the mask.

He noticed that too.

Damaris continued.

"The Crown has coin obligations already committed to battle compensation, funerals, roof timber, Northbridge repair, guard recruitment, grain contracts and the Red Ford settlement we have not yet finished negotiating. Silver is not simply coin. House Cendreval controls most reliable mine supply close enough to reach us before winter."

Luceran looked up.

"Controls?"

"Produces."

"Those are not the same word."

"No. Which is why I chose the second."

Miren sat beside him.

"What do they want?"

Damaris smiled thinly.

"To be asked."

---

House Cendreval arrived before the formal request reached them.

Not the house itself.

A house would have required more horses.

Their factor, Mistress Alys Cendreval, arrived with two covered wagons, six guards, a metallurgist, a nephew with excellent boots, and a letter expressing deep familial concern for the restoration of the ancestral seat.

Damaris read the letter twice.

"The concern weighs approximately four hundred pounds," she said.

Luceran looked at the wagons through the window.

"Silver?"

"Some. Lead. Wire. Sheet. Assay weights. Samples."

"How did they know?"

Damaris looked at him.

"Your Majesty, Vess has had masons opening walls for four days. Smiths have been asked for fine silver rivets. A clerk requested old mine-price rolls from the archive. Three village carriers were sent to price charcoal. Unless you intended to murder everyone involved, House Cendreval was going to know."

Luceran disliked the sentence because it was true.

"Did Edselsto tell them?"

Damaris closed the letter.

"No."

He looked at her.

"I didn't ask."

"Good."

She held out the letter.

"Try governing."

Mistress Alys was perhaps fifty, dressed in dark wool with silver thread only at the cuffs. Her nephew, Corvin, was younger than Luceran and possessed the serene confidence of a man whose family owned the thing everyone had suddenly discovered they needed.

Alys bowed correctly.

"Your Majesty. Lady Miren. Lady Damaris."

"Damaris is not a title," Damaris said.

Alys smiled.

"Today it is."

They liked each other immediately.

Luceran distrusted this.

Alys did not offer the silver as a gift.

Luceran respected that.

"We can deliver sixty marks refined within ten days," she said. "More if the mountain road remains open. Sheet, wire, or bar to specification. Lead as required. Two journeymen familiar with silver-lead joining. Master Ors here can assay whatever old metal you uncover."

Vess, invited because this was his problem, said, "I do not need sixty marks."

Alys looked delighted.

"Excellent."

Damaris asked, "Why?"

"Because a man who asks for all the silver offered has no idea what he is building."

Vess almost smiled.

Corvin did.

Miren noticed.

Luceran noticed Miren noticing.

That irritated him for reasons unrelated to procurement.

Alys continued.

"What do you need?"

"Six marks immediately. Perhaps twelve after survey. Fine sheet. Some wire. No alloy unless Ors and I agree on it."

"Eight immediately," Alys said.

"Six."

"Eight at six-mark price."

Damaris said, "Why?"

"Because I would like our metal in the walls when the king's miraculous house begins hearing again."

There it was.

Not charity.

Position.

Luceran leaned back.

"And in return?"

"Payment."

"Only payment?"

Alys looked mildly wounded.

"No one believes 'only payment' when a noble says it, Majesty. I will save us time. House Cendreval wants the assay warrant for recovered wall silver, first right to bid on replenishment for one year, and a seat for one technical observer whenever restoration standards are revised."

Vess said, "No."

Alys looked at him.

"Which?"

"Observer."

"Why?"

"Because you sell silver."

"Yes."

"You do not decide where it goes."

"I asked to observe."

"You will hear what rooms are being connected."

Alys's smile vanished.

That was the first useful moment.

Miren said, "Master Vess is right."

Alys considered her.

"Then technical sessions stripped of room identity."

Vess said, "Possible."

Damaris said, "No first right."

"Right to match?"

"No."

"Preference when price and quality are equal?"

Damaris paused.

Luceran watched the pause.

Alys watched it too.

That was politics: two people seeing the same half-second and pricing it differently.

Damaris said, "For six months. Limited to metal used in the conduit program. No mine concession. No tax privilege. No exclusivity."

Alys said, "Nine months."

"Six."

"Eight."

"Six."

"Seven."

Damaris looked at Luceran.

He understood this was the part where being king could make him useful or stupid.

"Six," he said.

Alys sighed.

"Your Majesty wounds commerce."

"Commerce appears healthy."

Corvin laughed before he could stop himself.

Alys turned her head.

He became solemn.

Miren covered her mouth with her cup.

---

The workers refused the night shift.

Not dramatically.

They simply did not arrive.

Vess found six empty places beneath the west gallery and one apprentice sitting on a bucket.

"Where is everyone?"

The apprentice, Lio Henn, looked miserable.

"They went down."

"Down where?"

"Village."

"Why?"

Lio looked toward the opened wall.

"Because Pell said the dead man can hear through doors now."

Vess closed his eyes.

"Iven hears his door."

"I know."

"Do you?"

"Yes."

"Does Pell?"

"No."

"Then why are you here?"

Lio stared at the floor.

"My father says a royal wage is a royal wage."

Vess sat on the opposite bucket.

"What do you think?"

"I think I don't want the wall knowing when I piss."

Vess nodded.

"Reasonable."

Lio looked up, surprised.

"You are not going to tell me it doesn't?"

"No."

"Does it?"

"Not from the privy we tested."

"Tested?"

"Yes."

That was apparently worse.

Vess rubbed his face.

"Come with me."

They found the missing workers at the Bent Nail, which had become a tavern by the simple method of putting benches in what had once been a cooper's shed.

Nobody ran when Vess entered.

That was almost insulting.

Pell Falbrecht, a charcoal carter with shoulders like a gate beam, raised his cup.

"Master Wall-Opener."

"Master Rumor-Maker."

"Fair."

Vess sat.

He explained the channels.

Not the magic.

The work.

A line was dead unless connected. A bridge could be removed. Some rooms would remain intentionally isolated. Every restored branch would be marked. Workers could refuse to work alone beside an active line. Nobody would be disciplined for asking whether a room was live.

Pell listened.

Then said, "Who decides?"

"The Crown."

"That isn't an answer."

Vess hated that everyone had become better at this.

"The rules require authorization."

"Whose?"

"Depends on the room."

"Who decides whether my workroom listens?"

Vess opened his mouth.

Closed it.

Pell drank.

"That's why we didn't come."

Vess looked around the shed.

Masons. Carriers. Two smiths. A plasterer's daughter. Men and women who had rebuilt the castle while nobles argued over who deserved which staircase.

"Come tomorrow," he said.

"Why?"

"Because you found a hole in the rules."

Pell grinned.

"Do we get paid for that?"

"No."

"Then it's not a hole. It's advice."

Vess stared at him.

"Come tomorrow and I will put your name on the grievance."

"That pays worse."

"It lasts longer."

Pell considered.

"All right."

---

The next morning the old council chamber contained a king, a treasurer, a priest, a guard commander, a noblewoman, a master mason, three clerks, two representatives of House Cendreval, four workers, one laundress, and a dead man in a gate three corridors away who had been specifically excluded because nobody could agree whether his presence would reassure anyone.

Luceran looked around the table.

"This is absurd."

Ysabet said, "Which part?"

"All of it."

"Useful. I will record that."

He gave her a look.

She dipped her pen.

Miren said, "The workers want to know who decides whether the rooms where they work can hear them."

Luceran said, "Edselsto hears rooms, not workers."

Pell Falbrecht said, "Majesty, with respect, rooms contain workers."

Luceran looked at him.

Pell looked terrified.

He had said it anyway.

"Yes," Luceran said. "They do."

Calienne had sent Nera Vale, a laundress who had spent the coup carrying bandages because the healers ran out of hands.

Nera said, "The laundry should be deaf."

Rhyse asked, "Why?"

"Because people talk when they think nobody important is there."

"That sounds like a reason to hear it."

She looked at him.

Rhyse winced.

"Yes. I heard it."

Nera continued.

"Women bring clothes down. Guards bring clothes down. Servants cry there. People change there. If the castle hears it, they will stop talking or stop coming."

Merel nodded.

"Confession by another name."

"Not holy confession."

"No. Which may make it more honest."

Luceran looked toward the empty place where the mask usually waited.

They had asked it not to attend.

It had obeyed.

The absence felt like pressure.

Damaris said, "We cannot create a private chamber for every occupation."

"No," Miren said. "But we can create categories."

Vess spread a new plan.

"Active. Inactive. Isolated."

Alys Cendreval said, "Isolated from what?"

"The nearest junction."

"Physically?"

"Yes."

"Visible break?"

"Where possible."

"Sealed?"

Vess looked at Miren.

She said, "Sealed."

Alys nodded.

"Then House Cendreval can stamp bridge pieces by batch."

Pell said, "Why should your house stamp them?"

Alys turned toward him.

"Because we make them."

"Then your mark tells everyone who sold the ears."

Corvin coughed.

Alys smiled.

"Your name?"

"Pell Falbrecht."

"House Falbrecht?"

"Charcoal branch."

"Ah."

That one syllable contained generations of hierarchy.

Pell heard it.

So did Luceran.

Pell said, "Still a name."

Alys's expression changed.

"Yes."

She looked at Vess.

"Royal stamp beside ours."

Ysabet said, "Archive number."

Damaris said, "Weight."

Rhyse said, "Tamper mark."

Nera said, "And a notice."

Everyone looked at her.

"On the wall," she said. "If a room listens, say so."

Luceran almost laughed.

Not because it was foolish.

Because it was so simple.

Vess said, "A plaque."

"No," Nera said. "Plaques become furniture. Something people can see."

Miren said, "Red thread."

Merel shook his head.

"Red already marks quarantine."

"Blue?"

"Water."

Pell said, "Silver."

Damaris said, "That will be stolen."

Pell looked at her.

"Paint."

They settled on a small black-and-white tile beside the door: white field, black ear for an active listening room; plain white for a deliberately isolated room; no tile where no restored connection existed.

Luceran hated the ear.

That was why they kept it.

---

The first restored public branch connected the lower receiving hall, gate clerk's station, west stair landing, and cistern approach.

Nobody chose the throne room.

Luceran noticed.

He could have ordered it.

He did not.

Vess installed the bridge himself.

Alys's metallurgist watched.

Pell held the lamp.

Lio Henn kept the inventory.

Rhyse posted a living guard at the junction cabinet.

Ysabet sealed the bridge count.

Merel said no prayer.

When Luceran asked why, Merel said, "Because I do not know what I would be blessing."

That answer survived.

Vess tightened the final retaining screw.

"Ready."

Luceran looked at the mask.

"Edselsto."

"Your Majesty."

"What can you hear?"

"The receiving hall. The gate clerk. The west stair. Water below."

"People?"

"Yes."

"How many?"

"Nine in the receiving hall. Three at the gate clerk. Two on the stair. Five below."

Rhyse sent runners.

Eight in the receiving hall.

Three at the clerk.

Two on the stair.

Five below.

Luceran looked at the mask.

"You said nine."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"There are nine."

The runner from the receiving hall returned irritated.

"Eight, Majesty."

Nera, who had stayed because she wanted to see whether the ear tile meant anything, said, "Count the child."

The runner frowned.

"What child?"

"The woman with the basket."

They went back.

Nine.

A vegetable seller had brought her daughter, asleep under a folded cloak behind the bench.

Luceran felt the room change.

Not because Edselsto had been right.

Because everyone understood what right could mean.

Miren looked at the black ear tile.

"That," she said quietly, "is why the laundry stays deaf."

---

The price rose that afternoon.

Not the Crown's price.

Everyone's.

A silver merchant from the village bought three months of expected Cendreval delivery before supper.

Two smiths raised their rates.

A carter demanded hazard pay for transporting refined metal.

Someone stole one of the sample bridge pieces from Master Ors's locked chest.

Rhyse found it under a stable boy's mattress before midnight.

The boy had not been bribed.

He had wanted to show his sister.

Rhyse believed him after three separate people confirmed he had spent the entire evening boasting that he possessed "castle magic."

The bridge was returned.

The boy lost two weeks' stable privileges and gained the permanent nickname Archbishop.

Damaris did not find it funny.

"Six marks of silver," she said the next morning, "and we have already created a speculative market, wage pressure, theft, noble positioning and a transport premium."

Luceran buttered bread.

"Efficient."

She stared at him.

He smiled.

She did not.

"House Cendreval's price is fair."

"Today."

"Lock it."

"For the first delivery, yes. After that?"

"Competitive bids."

"From whom?"

He stopped buttering.

Damaris leaned forward.

"This is what leverage looks like before it becomes extortion. They do not need to threaten us. We need something they are unusually good at supplying."

Miren entered and kissed Luceran's cheek before sitting down.

Damaris waited until she had poured tea.

"House Cendreval has also invited Lady Miren to dine."

Luceran looked at Miren.

She looked into her cup.

"When?"

"Tomorrow."

"Were you going to tell me?"

"Yes."

"When?"

"Before tomorrow."

Damaris ate bread.

Luceran said, "Why?"

Miren looked up.

"Because Alys likes me."

"Alys likes contracts."

"People can contain two things."

He heard himself before the next sentence escaped.

"Does Corvin?"

Miren became very still.

Damaris put down her knife.

There were moments when a room acquired edges.

This was one.

Miren said, "Would you like to ask the castle?"

Luceran's face went hot.

"No."

"Good."

"I didn't mean—"

"You did."

He looked away.

She let him.

That was worse than anger.

Damaris stood.

"I have suddenly remembered silver."

She left.

Luceran waited.

Miren drank tea.

"I saw you notice him," he said.

"Yes."

"That is not an accusation."

"It arrived dressed as one."

He rubbed his eyes.

"I am tired."

"So am I."

"I'm sorry."

"Good."

He looked at her.

She reached across the table and took his hand.

Not absolution.

Contact.

"I am going to dinner," she said.

"I know."

"You are not coming."

"I gathered."

"Alys wants to know whether I will support a Cendreval cousin for the vacant deputy mint office."

Luceran blinked.

"Ah."

"Yes. Politics. Disappointing."

He laughed.

She squeezed his hand.

Then released it.

"Corvin is pretty."

Luceran groaned.

Miren smiled.

"And now you know without architectural espionage."

That hurt exactly enough to teach.

---

The dinner produced no appointment.

It produced something more useful.

Miren returned after midnight with a folded list.

Luceran was awake.

He pretended he had been reading.

She did not pretend to believe him.

"Alys's cousin is qualified," she said.

"That sounds dangerous."

"Very."

She handed him the list.

Six names.

Three mine engineers. Two assayers. One widow named Sera Cendreval who managed transport accounts from the northern pits.

"What is this?"

"People willing to work under Vess without requiring Cendreval control of the program."

Luceran read it again.

"What did you promise?"

"Nothing."

"What did she ask?"

"That I remember who was helpful."

"That's something."

"Of course it is."

He looked at her.

"Will you?"

"Yes."

"Should I?"

"You should remember everyone who is helpful. You should not confuse remembering with owing them whatever they ask."

He set the list down.

"Did Corvin flirt with you?"

She laughed.

"Appallingly."

He stared.

"His aunt stopped him."

"Why?"

"Because she has a contract to win."

Luceran laughed too.

Then Miren leaned over and kissed him.

The jealousy did not vanish.

It became smaller than the person in front of him.

That was better.

---

Three days later, the restoration program had a budget.

Not the budget Luceran wanted.

The budget Damaris would sign.

Six marks of new silver immediately. Two held in reserve. No further purchase without surveyed need. Cendreval received the six-month equal-price preference and assay work under Crown supervision. Falbrecht charcoal carriers received a short haul contract after Pell's branch underbid two established firms. Vess hated that Pell now called himself "consultant to the ears."

Workers received ordinary wages plus wall-opening hazard rates where unstable masonry required it.

No magical premium.

"Why not?" Lio asked.

"Because you are not doing magic," Damaris said.

Lio looked at the silver line.

"Feels magical."

"You are removing plaster."

"Near magic."

"Near money is not money."

He repeated that sentence for days.

The first approved network remained small: gate administration, public receiving spaces, cistern access, two major stairs, the archive exterior corridor, and selected alarm points.

The private bedchambers remained isolated.

So did Calienne's treatment rooms.

So did confession.

So did the laundry.

The sealed council room remained disconnected except for a removable emergency bridge stored under two keys, one held by Rhyse and one by Ysabet.

Luceran objected.

"Why not me?"

Rhyse said, "Because you can order either of us to open it."

"Then what does that accomplish?"

"You have to order us."

Luceran looked at Miren.

She smiled.

Human friction.

Again.

He signed.

---

The first emergency came from the cistern.

Of course it did.

Vess was halfway through supper when the mask appeared at the dining-room threshold.

It had been told not to enter private meals unless there was immediate danger to life, fire, structural collapse, or water supply.

Luceran put down his spoon.

"Which?"

"Water."

Vess stood before the king did.

"Where?"

"Lower cistern. The west feed is loud."

Vess ran.

Luceran followed with Rhyse and three guards.

Miren followed because nobody successfully told her not to.

At the cistern stair, the black ear tile seemed suddenly less theoretical.

They found Henn waist-deep in water, screaming at two laborers to hold a timber brace against a cracked stone lip.

Not Lio.

His father.

Old Henn had worked the mill spring before the court returned.

"Gate!" he shouted.

"What gate?" Vess shouted back.

"The old feed! It's taking the wall!"

Edselsto said, "The west throat is open."

Vess rounded on it.

"Can it close?"

"Yes."

Luceran said, "Do it."

Nothing happened.

For one terrible second Luceran thought the castle had refused.

Then Vess shouted, "No! Ask what it needs!"

Luceran caught himself.

"How?"

"Silver reaches the throat. The closing weight is caught."

Vess splashed into the water.

"Where?"

"Behind the third stone."

"Which third stone?"

"From the old mark."

"What old mark?"

The mask turned toward the wall.

It could remember.

It could not point.

Henn swore with magnificent creativity.

Then he shoved his hand underwater.

"This!"

His fingers found a carved notch no one else had noticed.

Third stone.

They opened it.

Behind it, a corroded iron chain had jammed around a counterweight.

Living hands freed it.

The feed slammed shut.

The water stopped rising.

Henn leaned against the wall.

Vess sat down in the cistern.

Luceran stood ankle-deep in water, breathing hard.

The mask said, "The west throat is closed."

Henn looked at it.

"Very fucking helpful."

Rhyse laughed.

Nobody else could help it.

Even Luceran.

Especially Luceran.

---

The report the next morning was more important than the rescue.

Vess wrote the first line himself:

**Edselsto identified the condition. Living workers identified the physical reference and cleared the mechanism. Neither function substituted for the other.**

Luceran read it.

"That sounds pointed."

"It is."

"At me?"

"At whoever reads it next."

Damaris added the cost of the cracked lip.

Rhyse added the names of everyone present.

Ysabet added the authorization sequence.

Calienne added Henn's bruised hand and one laborer's strained shoulder because apparently even infrastructure now required medical annexes.

Pell added nothing because nobody had invited him.

He complained anyway.

The program continued.

Not everywhere.

That was the victory.

And the failure.

Every restored line made Edselsto more useful.

Every useful answer made the next restoration easier to justify.

By the end of the week the mask could hear the gate clerk, the lower receiving hall, the west stair, the cistern approach, the archive exterior and three alarm corridors it could not hear before.

It could not hear Miren dressing.

It could not hear Calienne telling a wounded man he might never walk correctly.

It could not hear Merel take confession.

It could not hear Nera Vale and three laundresses laughing about the king's jealousy.

Luceran knew that last one only because Miren told him.

He considered asking what they had said.

He did not.

At sunset he walked the west stair with Vess.

Fresh plaster covered the opened channels.

Beside each active room sat the ugly little black ear.

Beside each deliberately isolated room, a plain white tile.

At the junction cabinet, two seals crossed the latch.

One was Ysabet's.

One was Rhyse's.

A third mark belonged to Vess.

Not authority.

Work completed.

Luceran touched the cabinet.

"How much of the castle could be restored?"

Vess did not answer quickly.

"Eventually?"

"Yes."

"Most inhabited parts, perhaps."

"Most."

"Not all. Some lines are gone. Some routes are buried. Some rooms may never have had them."

"And if we did?"

Vess looked down the stair.

"You would have to decide what kind of house you wanted to live in."

Luceran looked at the black ear.

"What kind do you think I want?"

"Today?"

Luceran smiled.

"Today."

"A useful one."

"And tomorrow?"

Vess met his eyes.

"That is why we made the bridges removable."

The mask appeared at the bottom of the stair.

It did not climb.

It waited where the restored line reached.

"Your Majesty."

Luceran looked at it.

"What is it?"

"There is a chamber below the east foundation whose door has not opened in one hundred and eighty-three years."

Vess closed his eyes.

Luceran felt interest move through him like heat.

"What chamber?"

"I remember silver."

Vess looked at him.

"Do not ask how much."

Luceran laughed.

The mask waited.

Damaris would hate this.

Which meant, Luceran thought, that tomorrow would be interesting.

Behind the plaster, silver ran through the walls again.

Not because the castle had grown it.

Because miners dug it, merchants priced it, carriers hauled it, smiths shaped it, masons exposed the channels, clerks counted it, workers argued about it, nobles bargained over it, guards sealed it, and living people decided where the house was permitted to hear.
