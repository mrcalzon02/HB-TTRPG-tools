# Season Two, Episode 18 — The Man Who Returned

The cooper's widow had kept Corven's room ready through the night.

The broken step was wedged as she had promised. The bed had not been slept in. On the small table stood a pitcher, two cups and a candle burned nearly to its socket. Yara had paid for all three things, including the candle, because the widow had already lost the use of the room to a royal guest who never arrived.

At first light Yara took the widow to the village watch clerk. The widow described the shouting in the lane and the wounded attendant brought to her door. She could identify the man who carried him; she could not identify the attackers. The clerk read back the account, scratched out a phrase implying that she had seen the attack, and made her mark beside the correction.

The surviving attendant was angry about the word *surviving*. His companion lay upstairs with a bandaged shoulder and a fever that had not yet broken.

"I ran for the watch," he told Yara. "I left them."

"You were sent to bring help."

"I left them."

Yara did not tell him he had done well. She asked him to repeat what he had seen before he ran: three men in the lane, perhaps a fourth by the wall, a hand on Corven's walking stick, a pack animal pulled toward the west. He had heard a blow but could not say who struck it. She wrote the uncertainty as carefully as the rest.

The watch clerk had entered Corven as missing. During the night a household messenger had demanded that he alter the entry to *returned to the castle*. The clerk had refused without the name of a person who had seen him return alive.

"Did the messenger bring him?" Yara asked.

"No."

"Then keep your first entry."

She had Corven's three bench warrants, his unfinished letter to Leora, and his instruction that no new undertaking leave under his name without a living voice and the benches' countersigns. She could obey all three without knowing whether her employer was dead.

She sent one letter to Leora saying that Corven had been attacked and that she could not yet establish his condition. A second went to the three river benches, asking them to hold any new use of his authority pending independent confirmation. Neither letter accused the Crown of murder. Neither said he was safe.

She paid the watch clerk for two copies of the night's entries. One remained in the village. She carried the other uphill herself.

---

Rhyse found Beren Kalt seated outside the courier master's office, with two household men standing where they could prevent him leaving.

The veteran had not been bound. He had also not been permitted to go home.

"I refused the errand," Beren said before Rhyse could ask. "His Majesty knows it."

"Did you see the men depart?"

"I saw the roll. I saw the packhorse. I did not see the road."

Rhyse ordered the guards to keep Beren under their protection, not in punishment, until his account could be taken before Ysabet. The distinction was one he intended to make real: Beren could eat with his own company, send for his wife, and speak to a priest. He could not yet leave the castle, because the king had already given an order whose consequences reached beyond ordinary discipline.

In the mortuary Rhyse saw the man standing beside the washing table. Corven's clothes hung badly on him; the left leg was stiff. The man put his weight on it and winced with the surprise of someone who had not expected pain.

"Envoy Sareth," Rhyse said.

The man looked toward Luceran before answering. It was a soldier's glance, brief and unmistakable.

"Captain."

Corven had never addressed him that way without adding a dry observation about guards who could not make a man feel free by standing closer to his door.

Ysabet took Rhyse into the adjoining room. She showed him the king's signed private instruction, the courier roll, the refusal she had recorded and her own account of what she had witnessed in the mortuary. She did not give him the original papers to carry away.

"You saw him dead?" he asked.

"I saw the body brought in and laid out. I saw it breathe after the king gave the command."

"And the man in it?"

"Calls himself Jalen Orris. He remembers the north gate."

Rhyse had known Jalen only by the casualty roll. He did not pretend recognition.

"I cannot guard a murder as though it were a private errand," he said.

"You are not being asked to forget it."

"I am being asked to keep the people who can prove it alive."

Ysabet said nothing. The two of them knew that a guard posted outside the mortuary might serve the king's concealment as easily as the witnesses' safety.

Rhyse made three orders in his own name. The wounded attendant would have a physician and a free visitor chosen by Yara. Beren's refusal and the village watch book would be preserved without alteration. No man from the private road service would be placed alone with a witness. He sent the orders through living Doorwardens, not the household courier office.

He had not yet decided whether to confront Luceran before or after speaking to Yara. That indecision cost him an hour. He entered it in his own watch account.

---

Jalen could hold a cup, but the cup was heavier than he expected.

He had spent the morning learning what the body would do. The old knee resisted stairs. A bruised shoulder made reaching for a shelf difficult. His hand knew how to fold a letter without showing him what the letter meant.

The physician examined him under Ysabet's eye. He found a pulse, breath, bruising and the older injury at the knee. He had been summoned to examine a living patient and was not told to certify the manner of the patient's return. He declined to write that Corven had recovered from an ordinary illness.

"I can describe what is before me," he said. "Not what you wish had happened before I entered."

When he left, Luceran set three sheets of river correspondence before Jalen. One concerned the Dols bridge, another the Teth ferries, the third the smaller Merehaven bond.

"Read them," the king said.

Jalen read slowly. He understood the words *grain* and *soldiers*. He did not understand why a ferry owner might challenge a valuation after an envoy had signed it.

"Because it is the owner's boat," Luceran said.

"Then why not pay him?"

"We have offered to."

"Then why is he angry?"

"He fears we will take it before the payment arrives."

Jalen considered that. "I would be angry too."

Luceran had wanted the man's obedience. It was there. What he had not anticipated was having to teach him the objections Corven had spent weeks defending.

"Do you remember a woman named Leora?" he asked.

"No."

"She is Corven's wife."

Jalen put the correspondence down. "Will she come?"

"Not today."

"If she does, what shall I say?"

Luceran looked at the old knee and the borrowed hands. "We will decide that when we must."

"I had a sister," Jalen said. "She mended my boots. I do not know whether she is alive. You said the gate was held."

"It was."

"Then I would rather know whether she was paid what she was owed."

Luceran could promise to have the casualty accounts examined. He could not promise that Jalen's sister had ever received them, or that she would welcome the voice now speaking from another man's body.

Edselsto's pale mask waited in the connected wall. It offered to repeat the names of the river benches. Luceran declined. A name was not a reason, and a reason was what Corven had taken with him into death.

---

At Dols the carpenter had returned to the miller's ditch before sunrise.

The timber lip held. The earth above it was still wet. Savelin put a narrow board across the softest place, walked it himself, and watched it sink a finger's width under his weight. The reeve refused to call the crossing ready. The miller insisted that the Crown pay for the next inspection if another rain spoiled the work.

Then the messenger came with two accounts of Corven. One said he had been attacked and was missing. The other, carried from the royal household, said he had returned to Edselsto and would shortly resume negotiations.

Savelin read both twice.

"Which account came from the envoy's secretary?" the reeve asked.

"The first."

"Then I will not put the second in our bridge book as a fact."

Savelin had once wanted Corven to make the benches move faster. Now he wanted him alive enough to refuse an unreasonable crossing. He sent the royal message uphill with a question written beneath it: *Who saw the envoy return, and in what condition?*

The reeve kept the drain consent separate from the matter of Corven's life. The miller had allowed the cut; the carpenter had done the work; the western approach still needed a measured load test. None of those facts became false because a king's household sent contradictory news.

Savelin ordered the paid timber left in place and no cart admitted. He also wrote to Damaris that the next inspection would cost another day of wages. He did not ask the castle to settle a question the riverbank could answer under a man's boot.

---

Yara arrived at Edselsto with the village watch copy beneath her cloak.

Rhyse met her at the outer stair. He did not ask for the warrants. He told her that a man resembling Corven was inside, that Ysabet had witnessed events he could not yet explain without breaking a sealed royal record, and that Yara would not be compelled to treat the man as her employer.

"Is he injured?" she asked.

"Yes."

"Can he speak?"

"Yes."

"Does he know me?"

"I do not know."

She accepted the distinction, though it frightened her more than a rehearsed assurance would have done.

The audience took place in the south-gallery withdrawing room. Yara inspected the severed silver junction before entering, as she had done when Corven first arrived. Luceran stood by the hearth. Rhyse remained at the door. The man in Corven's clothes sat in a chair with his left leg extended.

Yara had watched her employer endure that knee for years. He ordinarily settled it with one hand before he sat. This man had to look down and move it with both.

"Master Sareth," she said.

"Yara."

The voice was nearly right. He spoke her name as a man speaks a word supplied for an examination.

She placed the three warrants on the table but kept her hand over their seals.

"What did we decide about the Merehaven undertaking before you left the castle?"

"The smaller bond," he said.

"And the condition that made it smaller?"

He looked to Luceran. Yara saw the glance. The king saw her see it.

"The roof," Luceran said.

"The roof was one condition," Yara replied. "The other was that the first cargo be witnessed and accounted for before anyone promised a second. My master insisted on it because he knew the benches would not pay for sacks they had not received."

The man lowered his eyes. "I did not remember."

"Nor did you remember to ask me whether the letter to Leora had gone."

He looked up sharply. "Has it?"

Yara did not answer. She had not come to test a sick man with private grief. She had come to discover whether someone meant to use his hands.

"You may recover," she said to him. "You may have lost memories. I cannot judge either matter. But the warrants remain with me, and no recommendation in his name will leave this room."

Luceran said, "The northern posts cannot wait indefinitely."

"Then send the bridge measurements, the roof tally and the paid carriage terms under the names of the people who made them. The benches may answer those facts without pretending that my master's judgment has returned."

Rhyse offered to witness the dispatch. Yara accepted him as a witness, not as an owner of the letters.

The man who wore Corven's face asked whether he might read the papers again. Yara allowed it. She did not let him touch the seals.

---

Miren found Luceran at supper with a plate he had scarcely disturbed.

He had sent the factual dispatches Yara permitted. They would take days to reach the river benches. The northern garrison would not eat the king's relief at having moved paper from one table to another.

"Rhyse says Corven is back," Miren said.

"His body is here."

She waited.

"I ordered the attack," Luceran said. "He died. Edselsto brought Jalen Orris into him."

She sat down slowly. She knew about the earlier refusal, the question and the absence of proof. She had not known that the king had crossed from considering murder to commanding it. He told her where Ysabet had placed the instruction. He did not ask her to hide it.

"Corven warned you that his people could refuse," she said.

"Yes."

"And now a dead soldier has to learn why."

"Yes."

She pushed his untouched bread toward him, then stopped herself. Feeding him had become a way of remaining in the room when she could not bear what he was saying. Tonight she did not want that habit mistaken for forgiveness.

"Does Leora know?"

"Yara has written."

"Does she know what you did?"

"No."

"Then you have made her a widow and denied her even the knowledge that she is one."

Luceran could have argued that the body breathed. He did not. Miren rose and took the plate with her, not as punishment but because she needed something ordinary to do before leaving.

"Will you stay?" he asked.

"Not tonight."

She went to the physically deaf room she had demanded after he stole her words through Edselsto. For the first time, she locked its door against the man she loved.

In the copy room below, Ysabet's clerk entered three separate dispatches: Yara's bench notices, Savelin's bridge question and the Crown's factual account of twelve sheltered sacks. None bore Corven's signature. In the village, the watch clerk kept the missing-man entry unchanged. The injured attendant's fever had not yet broken. Beren Kalt remained under a guard whose written order permitted visitors.

The man wearing Corven's body slept uneasily, troubled by a knee he had never injured and a wife he had never met. The treaty had not been made. A narrow exchange of facts had begun.

In the family nave all seven candles burned.
