# Season Two, Episode 30 — Half a Man at the Gate

The king's letter arrived at Dols in a dry pouch, though the man carrying it had ridden through two days of rain.

The bridge-court clerk entered the hour, the Crown seal and the name of the rider. She did not enter the contents as true. The reeve had asked for the Crown's account of Corven Sareth's supposed recovery, and the letter answered with a promise to produce the original physician's observations, a withdrawal of the returned man's commission, and a sentence so particular that the clerk copied it again on a separate leaf.

*No officer of Our Crown was instructed by Us to bring about the attack upon Corven Sareth.*

The reeve asked whether the rider could attest to that.

"I can attest I took the pouch from the royal receiving clerk," he said. "I wasn't in the lane."

"Then I shall not ask you to have been."

The accompanying physician's copy described a man with a pulse, breath, bruising and a damaged knee. It contained no examination of his back. The reeve set it beside Master Isera's account, not upon it. Two people could have examined the same patient at different times and each have written the truth about what they saw. That did not make their observations identical.

The clerk asked whether to send the royal denial to the watch captain.

"Send him its arrival date. The sealed answer stays here until we have entered the physician's copy and the carrier's testimony."

"Do we send it to Merehaven?"

"Not as though it were our judgment. They have their own bench."

Outside the court, a new footing stone waited at the miller's lane. The mason had refused the first load because the cart stood too high and the lower wheel had sunk into the wet verge. The quarry carrier demanded the extra hire that a second journey would cost. A narrow wall and an ordinary quarrel over who paid for caution kept the repair from proceeding.

Dols had received the Crown's words. Its wall had not received a stone.

---

The second physician arrived late, angry with the road and embarrassed by the anger.

He was Master Orenna, sent at the reeve's request from the upstream infirmary. A broken culvert had delayed him. He washed, read Isera's observations without seeing her drawings, and asked to speak to Jalen Orris before he examined the body whose name was in dispute.

"Will you answer questions about the attack?" Orenna asked.

"Only what I know."

"And what do you know?"

"I know I was not there to see it. I remember dying somewhere else. I remember waking here."

"That is an account," Orenna said. "I can record that you gave it. I cannot certify it from your pulse."

Jalen sat on the lower bed he had chosen. His knee hurt after the short walk from the inn door. Yara stood with the three separate warrants in her satchel, neither physician's assistant nor guardian of Jalen's person. She had already told the reeve that no river bench had given her power to detain him.

Orenna examined the dark marks independently, with a woman from the infirmary witnessing his notes. He asked Jalen to change position and waited to see whether the discoloration moved or blanched. Isera had drawn what she saw two days earlier. Orenna saw the same broad pattern and wrote that he could not account for it by ordinary recent bruising. He would not call it proof that Corven had died before Jalen began speaking.

Jalen listened as they disagreed over whether the edge of one mark had altered.

"Will you put down that you disagree?" he asked.

"Yes," said Isera.

"Good. I don't want two men agreeing because a reeve sent for one of them."

Orenna looked at her, surprised, and then laughed without pleasure. "A sensible demand."

The watch captain came to say that the mason had stopped the new stone cart again. A boy apprenticed to the quarry carrier had been told to hold the second horse while its master argued over payment. The captain ordered the narrow lane cleared until the load could be divided. The order went to the cartman and the mason separately; the reeve's clerk entered it because an argument about an order was likely to follow.

Jalen asked whether the original casualty discharge from his own death had arrived.

It had not. The Crown escort's clerk had found another reference to the entry, not the account itself.

"Then write that you haven't found it," Jalen said. "If my sister was paid, I want the name of the person who took the coin. If she wasn't, I want the sum."

He had submitted to a second examination. He had not agreed to spend the rest of his borrowed life as an exhibit.

---

The miller's lane bent around a low storehouse before it reached the bridge approach. The wall under repair stood on the outer side, above a fall to the watercourse. There was room for a cart and a man to pass, but not for a loaded cart to turn while a second horse stood across the road.

The mason had ordered the stone unloaded below the bend and carried up in two smaller loads. The carrier had brought it up whole because he had been promised a day's hire, not two. Both men could show the words that favored them. Neither could show permission to put a heavy cart against a wall whose footing had not yet been accepted.

The apprentice boy tried to lead the spare horse out of the way. A wheel settled into the wet verge. The horse shied at the grinding of the axle, and the cartman's attempt to pull the team straight drove the load against the bank.

The watch captain shouted to clear the lane.

Jalen was there because the infirmary stood beyond the bend and he had walked out with Yara after the examination. He saw the boy's rein trapped beneath the spare horse's hoof. The boy could not move without bringing the animal down upon himself.

Jalen had been a soldier. He understood horses well enough to know that a frightened one would not wait for a perfect command. He caught the boy by the back of his coat and dragged him toward the storehouse door. His bad knee gave way as the boy came free.

The cart's upper lashings parted. A dressed stone, brought for the miller's wall, slid across the tilted bed. The mason had time to shout that it was moving. The watch captain reached Jalen's shoulder but could not lift him clear.

The stone struck the low parapet and broke its upper edge. It carried Jalen with it into the narrow gap between the cart and the masonry.

There was a sound like wet timber splitting.

Yara remembered afterward that she had not seen the stone land. She had been watching the boy, who had fallen on his hands and was trying to stand. When she looked back, the cart had stopped and the watch captain was on his knees beside a man who ought already to have been dead.

The stone had crushed and divided Jalen's body below the ribs. His lower half lay trapped beneath the dressed block. The torn middle was open to the rain. The watch captain put his cloak over it because there were people in the lane who did not need to see what he had seen.

Jalen moved his hand.

"Did he get out?" he asked.

The captain looked at the boy, who was sobbing against the storehouse wall.

"Yes."

"Don't let them put my name on the cart," Jalen said.

Isera, who had followed at the first shout, knelt on the dry side of the fallen stone. She could not find a pulse she trusted. She could see the injury and the absence of any bodily means by which the man should continue to speak.

"Jalen," she said, using the name he had given her, "do you know where you are?"

"Dols."

"Can you feel your hands?"

"Yes."

She did not ask whether he felt his legs.

The carrier began saying that the wall had been unsound before he arrived. The mason said he had forbidden the load. The watch captain ordered both men kept for separate statements, not chained together and not beaten. The apprentice was taken to the infirmary to have his bruised hands dressed. The wall remained unaccepted. The second grain wagon did not cross.

A man had been cut almost in two in front of living witnesses, and the first thing Dols did was make room for the injured and prevent another cart from entering the lane.

---

They carried Jalen to the infirmary on a door taken from the storehouse. Orenna insisted that the lower remains be recovered separately, with the cart, stone and witnesses recorded before anyone moved the load. He had seen men live for minutes after injuries that no physician could mend. He had never seen one continue to answer questions after such a division of the body.

Jalen continued.

There was no ordinary circulation left to sustain him. His voice weakened when the physicians moved him and strengthened again when he rested. He did not grow new flesh, close a wound or recover the part of himself beneath the stone. Isera cleaned what could be cleaned, covered what could be covered, and refused to call the dressing a cure.

Yara remained beside the bed because Jalen asked her to stay. She had no warrant to command his movements, and for once the distinction pleased him.

"Will they keep me?" he asked.

"The reeve wants an account."

"He has one."

"He will want to know what happens next."

"So do I."

The reeve arrived with the watch captain's first statement and the Crown's sealed denial. He did not show Jalen the king's sentence until Jalen asked whether it concerned him.

"It concerns the attack upon Corven Sareth," the reeve said. "You have told us you did not witness that attack."

"I didn't."

"And this injury?"

"Everyone saw it."

"Yes."

Orenna's new account certified the anatomical destruction he had personally observed and the continued speech and movement that followed. Isera made a separate record of the same event and of the earlier unexplained markings. Neither document certified who had killed Corven, who had placed Jalen in the body, or who had commanded a mortuary rite. The witnesses to the cart accident could not become witnesses to a different killing merely because one event made another more dreadful to imagine.

The reeve proposed a witnessed journey to Edselsto. A Crown officer could be asked to explain the body that had left the castle, and Dols would be entitled to preserve its own findings.

"Do you intend to send me as evidence?" Jalen asked.

"I intend to ask whether you will go."

"And if I won't?"

"Then you will remain here as long as you choose, if we can provide you shelter. I have no charge against you."

Jalen thought of the casualty account he had not found, and of a sister who might have been told he was dead and paid nothing. He thought of the gate where he remembered dying, and of the castle where he had awakened with another man's aching knee.

"I'll go back," he said. "Not as Corven. I want my own papers. And if I can no longer walk, I still decide where I am carried."

The reeve agreed to a carriage paid from Dols's hearing account for the first stage, with the Crown escort paying its own men and horses. Two Dols witnesses would accompany the records; neither would become a royal prisoner or messenger. The physicians would send signed originals of their observations to the Dols court, carry attested copies, and retain independent drafts. The damaged lower remains would travel covered and identified, not be hidden or displayed.

Yara wrote to each bench in the terms its own authority required. She did not say the three courts had reached a common verdict. The reeve sent a separate notice to Leora's household, addressed to her steward and not to the Crown. The notice described the witnessed injury and the journey. It did not announce that Corven had been proved murdered.

The apprentice's father came to the infirmary with the boy. Jalen asked that the child not be made to look at him. The father thanked him from behind the screen. Jalen heard it and turned his face away.

He had wanted to be useful without pretending to be someone else. He had not expected usefulness to cost the body he had been given.

---

At Edselsto, Damaris learned that the second Dols wagon had not crossed from a carter demanding his waiting pay.

The man had been hired to bring the six sacks still beneath the receiving-yard roof to the bridge once the wall was accepted. It had not been accepted. Now he wanted to know whether he was to hold his horses another day.

"You were not hired to cross a closed bridge," Damaris said.

"I was hired to wait for a bridge that was nearly open."

"Then show me the waiting clause."

He did, and she paid the lawful day. She would not charge the six sacks for a failure that belonged to the wall and the weather. The northern posts had eaten the first six. That receipt remained good. So did the unpaid need for the next six.

Etta brought word of the returning Dols party, but not yet its medical account. A rider had reached the lower road house ahead of the carriage. He said there had been a stone-cart accident and that the man calling himself Jalen Orris was alive, though badly injured. He had not seen the infirmary record and could not say more.

Damaris asked for the road cost. Etta gave it. Neither knew of the king's sealed denial, and neither claimed that an injury proved a murder.

Rhyse received the same advance notice in the guardroom. He ordered the gate cleared for a litter, two living Doorwardens on duty and no crowd in the receiving passage. He did not ask Iven Marrick to decide who might enter. The dead keeper's finite voluntary service remained at its own threshold, under the same living roster as before.

Ysabet asked the messenger whether the Dols reeve had enclosed a reply to the Crown's letter.

"He has sent two witnesses with the carriage," the man said. "They have the papers."

"Then we will hear them."

Luceran came down from the gallery as the preparations were made. He had hoped the word *injured* meant a splint, a fever, perhaps the old knee. When Rhyse said the escort had requested a door broad enough for a covered stretcher, Luceran asked why they could not use the ordinary infirmary entrance.

"Because the Dols witnesses require a public arrival record," Rhyse said. "And because the patient asked to be brought to the gate."

"Is he still capable of asking?"

"That is what the messenger reports."

Luceran did not ask Edselsto to tell him what the man looked like. The carriage was beyond the connected stone, and the king knew that a voice in the wall could not witness a road it had never heard.

Miren heard the carriage bells from the south gallery. She had been spending her evenings apart from Luceran, and the distance between them had become a household fact that even a discreet servant could notice. She asked Rhyse whether anyone had been injured on the road.

"Yes," he said. "We have not yet seen him."

She went to the gate because she knew who had left in Corven's face. She did not go to comfort the king.

---

The carriage entered at dusk.

Its wheels were wrapped to spare the injured man the worst of the stones, and it had stopped twice on the climb to change the padding beneath the door that served as a litter. The Dols witnesses rode beside it. Yara walked the last stretch, carrying her warrants in one satchel and the physician's attested copies in another.

Rhyse had kept the receiving passage clear. Etta stood with the gate book open. Ysabet waited with a place for the seals. Damaris was there because the carriage had used Crown fodder and a paid team for the final ascent, not because she had been told what the king had done.

The escort captain lifted the covering only when Jalen asked him to.

The face was Corven Sareth's. The voice that came from it was Jalen's, thinner than before. Beneath the ribs the body ended in dressings that could not disguise the absence of the rest of it. A second covered board held what the Dols physicians had identified as the lower remains. There was no living pulse that the escort physician could reliably find. Jalen turned his head toward the gate book and asked that his own name be entered.

Etta looked at Rhyse. Rhyse nodded.

"Jalen Orris," she said, and wrote it.

The Dols witness gave his name, the date of the accident, the mason's order against the load, the watch captain's account and the physicians' separate observations. He described the injury he had seen, not Corven's earlier death. He then produced the reeve's dated acknowledgment of the Crown's denial. The sealed royal words had been received at Dols before the stone fell. That sequence mattered.

Luceran approached the litter.

He had ordered a man's death and made another man serve inside the corpse. He had then written that no officer of his Crown had been instructed to arrange the attack. Before him lay the result of his choice, still speaking, while foreign witnesses stood where his own servants could hear.

"Jalen," he said.

"Majesty."

"Are you in pain?"

"Some."

"Can anything be done?"

"Isera says they can keep the wound clean. She says they cannot make me whole."

Miren stood beside Ysabet, close enough to hear and far enough from Luceran that no one could mistake her presence for forgiveness. She had loved him before he had learned to make a lie sound like a royal answer. She still loved him. Neither fact supplied the missing part of the man on the litter.

The Dols witness did not accuse the king of murder. He asked for a chamber in which the sealed accounts could be entered, for the Crown physician's original notes to be produced, and for the people who had custody of Corven after the lane attack to be named. The reeve had not waived the bridge court's rights, and no other bench had delegated its judgment to him.

Ysabet said the records would be received with their seals intact. Rhyse said the witnesses would have rooms and freedom to leave. Damaris quietly asked Etta for the cost of the team, then stopped when she saw that the second board had been carried inside.

Luceran looked at the king's own seal on the acknowledgment. The sentence he had signed remained a sentence. The carriage had not made it true, nor had it yet put the original contradictory order into Dols's hands.

"Where is my discharge?" Jalen asked.

Ysabet answered him rather than the king. "We have not found the original account."

"Find it. And tell my sister I asked."

He was carried through the gate under his own name. The living guards kept their places. The foreign witnesses followed with their papers. Somewhere in the old stone, Edselsto remained ready to answer a properly framed question and obey a lawful command.

In the family nave, all seven candles burned.

The king's next answer would have to be spoken before people who could contradict it.
