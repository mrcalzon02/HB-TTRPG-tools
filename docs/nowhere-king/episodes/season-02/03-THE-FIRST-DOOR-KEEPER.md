# Season Two, Episode 3 — The First Door-Keeper

Iven Marrick had been dead for three days when his mother asked whether the castle could hear him.

Nobody answered her.

The question had been addressed to Luceran, but everyone in the old council chamber found something else to look at: Ysabet at her pages, Canon Merel at the seven names he had copied from the burial list, Rhyse at the window, Miren at Luceran.

Mara Marrick did not look away.

She had arrived from the capital before dawn with Iven's younger brother and a black traveling cloak still white at the hem from road dust. She had not cried when Mara—the guard, not the mother; an accident of names that had already confused three clerks—told her how Iven died. She had asked who had been beside him. She had asked whether he had been afraid. She had asked whether he had held the gate after it opened.

Only then had she gone to see the body.

Now she sat with both hands around a cup she had not touched.

Luceran said, "Why do you ask?"

"Because it spoke to my son."

The pale silver mask waited beside the cold hearth.

Luceran looked at it.

"Did you?"

"Yes, Your Majesty."

Mrs. Marrick's fingers tightened around the cup.

"When?"

The mask did not answer.

Luceran closed his eyes for half a breath.

The rules were three days old and already every ordinary conversation with Edselsto had become a lesson in who possessed permission.

"Answer her about Iven Marrick."

"At the gate."

Rhyse turned from the window.

"What did you say?"

"I asked whether he wished to remain."

The room changed.

Luceran stood so quickly his chair scraped stone.

Miren said, "Remain how?"

The mask faced him.

Luceran felt the answer before he heard it.

"To serve."

Canon Merel whispered something in the old liturgical tongue.

Mrs. Marrick put down her cup.

"My son is dead."

"Yes."

"Can he hear me?"

"Not now."

The words were almost worse than yes.

Luceran said, "Where is he?"

"Near."

Rhyse crossed the room.

"No riddles."

"I do not intend a riddle."

"Then speak plainly."

The mask waited for Luceran.

He hated that.

"Answer him."

"Iven Marrick gave his service at the gate. His body failed before the service did."

Nobody spoke.

Mrs. Marrick finally did.

"What does that mean?"

Edselsto said, "He may keep the gate."

---

Calienne refused to discuss resurrection.

"It is not resurrection."

"Good."

"You have not heard the rest."

"I heard enough to know what it is not."

They were in the lesser chapel because Calienne had commandeered the family nave for the dead and refused to move them merely because the king had acquired a theological emergency.

Iven lay beneath linen with his hands folded over his chest.

His mother stood beside him.

His brother, Tomas Marrick, had finally begun crying and appeared furious about it.

Merel had brought two priests from the village and one from the capital. They disagreed before anyone asked them anything.

Vess arrived with a measuring rod because the mask had said the gate mattered physically.

Damaris arrived because anything involving a permanent royal installation eventually became an expense.

Ysabet arrived because everyone had learned that if something impossible happened and nobody wrote down the exact words, they would spend the next month lying to themselves about what had been said.

Rhyse brought two guards.

Miren brought no weapon this time.

Luceran noticed.

"So," Calienne said, "tell me what it proposes without calling it resurrection."

Luceran looked toward the mask.

Edselsto said, "A place may hold a willing service."

Merel said, "A place."

"Yes."

"A soul?"

The mask tilted.

"A remainder."

One of the village priests crossed himself.

The capital priest said, "That is worse."

Mrs. Marrick said, "Let it finish."

Everyone did.

Edselsto continued.

"He died in willing defense of the sovereign and the gate. He may remain at the gate. He may know those he knew. He may speak. He may refuse passage. He may remember."

"For how long?" Calienne asked.

Luceran authorized the answer with a nod.

"As long as remains to him."

Merel frowned.

"That means nothing."

"His life was not spent."

Calienne looked at Iven's body.

"His life was very much spent."

"The body was spent."

The capital priest left.

He did not announce why.

He simply walked out.

Luceran watched him go and did not order him back.

That mattered.

Mrs. Marrick said, "How long?"

"Years."

"How many?"

"Unknown."

"Ten?"

"Possible."

"Twenty?"

"Possible."

"Fifty?"

"Less likely."

Tomas wiped his face with his sleeve.

"So he dies twice."

Nobody had an answer for that.

Edselsto did.

"Yes."

Tomas looked at the mask with naked hatred.

"Thank you."

The mask inclined its head.

He hated that too.

---

Rhyse wanted the gate closed before they tried anything.

Vess wanted it open.

They argued for eleven minutes.

"If something goes wrong," Rhyse said, "I want the thing we are experimenting on between us and the road."

"If the mechanism needs movement, closing it may make the experiment meaningless."

"It is a door."

"It is a gate."

"It closes."

"That is not the same as being a door."

Damaris pinched the bridge of her nose.

Luceran said, "I had missed ordinary problems."

Miren looked at him.

"No, you hadn't."

"No."

They compromised.

The outer gate remained open. The inner gatehouse door was cleared, braced, and placed under guard. Nobody stood in its swing.

Vess found a narrow silver socket beneath the old hinge plate.

It had been hidden under three generations of rust and a repair made with the wrong iron.

"That," he said, "is deliberate."

"Can you restore it?" Luceran asked.

"Yes."

Damaris said, "With what?"

Vess looked at her.

"Silver."

"How much?"

"Very little."

"Give me a number."

"Half a thumb."

"That is not a weight."

"It is to a mason."

"It is not to a treasury."

Mrs. Marrick laughed.

It surprised everyone, including her.

For the first time since arriving, she looked like Iven.

Luceran had seen that expression on the dead man's face when Rhyse complained about ceremonial armor.

The resemblance hurt.

Vess cut the silver bridge from a damaged length already removed from the west listening junction. No new metal was purchased. Damaris wrote that down anyway.

Merel stood beside the door.

"Before anything happens, I want the terms spoken."

Rhyse nodded.

"So do I."

Luceran looked at Mrs. Marrick.

"So do I."

The mask floated near the wall.

Luceran addressed it.

"No one is bound without consent."

"Yes."

"Not mine. Theirs."

"Yes."

"Consent given while alive."

"Yes."

Merel said, "And revocable."

The mask was silent.

Luceran felt the first coldness of the morning.

"Can he leave?"

"Yes."

"At any time?"

"Yes."

"Can he be ordered not to?"

The mask paused.

Miren's face hardened.

Luceran said, "Answer."

"By you."

"No."

The word came from Mrs. Marrick.

Not Luceran.

Everyone looked at her.

She stepped toward the king.

"If my son stays, you do not own his death because he died for you."

Luceran felt every eye in the gatehouse.

The easiest answer was reassurance.

The harder answer was law.

He looked at Ysabet.

"Write it."

She did.

"A willing dead servant may depart service at will. The Crown shall not command continuation."

Rhyse said, "Nor punishment for departure."

Ysabet added it.

Merel said, "Nor transfer."

The mask turned toward him.

Luceran understood why.

"Explain."

Merel pointed at the door.

"If he agrees to this gate, he agrees to this gate. You do not move him into a cell door because you later decide prisoners need watching."

Luceran looked at the mask.

"Can that be done?"

"Yes."

Mrs. Marrick's expression became dangerous.

Luceran raised a hand.

"It will not be."

"Write it," Miren said.

Ysabet did.

Damaris said, "And no service creates a debt against the family."

Luceran stared at her.

"Who would do that?"

She stared back.

He sighed.

"Write it."

Mrs. Marrick finally picked up the cup someone had brought from the kitchen.

It was cold.

She drank anyway.

---

They asked Iven again.

That was the part Merel insisted upon.

The mask said the dead man had already consented at the gate.

Merel said that consent given while bleeding to death beneath an assault was not enough for what they now understood the offer to mean.

Edselsto did not argue.

That frightened Luceran more than argument would have.

"Can you ask him now?" Luceran said.

"Yes."

"Then ask."

The mask turned toward the inner gatehouse door.

Nothing happened.

A minute passed.

Then another.

Tomas shifted his weight.

Vess looked at the hinge.

Rhyse watched everyone instead of the door.

Miren watched Luceran.

At last a voice said, "This is taking an embarrassing amount of time."

Mrs. Marrick dropped the cup.

It broke.

The voice had come from the door.

Not behind it.

From it.

Tomas made a sound like someone had struck him.

Mara—the guard—covered her mouth.

Rhyse went perfectly still.

Luceran knew the voice.

He had heard it shouting in the gatehouse while men died.

Iven Marrick said, "Mother?"

Mrs. Marrick walked to the door.

Merel caught her arm.

She rounded on him.

"Do not."

"The silver is not seated."

"I do not care."

"I do."

That stopped her.

Merel's face was wet.

Nobody had noticed him begin crying.

"If this is him," he said, "then I owe him the dignity of not pretending certainty because I want comfort."

Mrs. Marrick looked at him for a long time.

Then nodded.

Vess fitted the silver bridge.

The old hinge plate gave a tiny sound.

Not a chime.

A breath through metal.

The grain of the oak darkened around the iron straps.

A shape appeared in it.

Not a face.

The suggestion of one, as if knots and old cuts had happened to remember eyes.

"Iven?" Mrs. Marrick whispered.

"Yes."

"What did you steal from your father when you were twelve?"

Tomas laughed through tears.

The door said, "Which time?"

Mrs. Marrick hit the oak with both hands.

Then pressed her forehead against it.

The sound she made belonged to no court.

Everyone looked away except Calienne.

Calienne never looked away from pain.

---

They did not declare a miracle.

Ysabet wrote that sentence first.

The test lasted six hours.

Iven recognized Rhyse.

He recognized Mara.

He recognized his mother and brother.

He remembered dying, but not continuously.

He remembered the gate opening.

He remembered a man with a red scarf stepping over Jalen Orris.

He remembered being afraid.

He remembered choosing to stay.

He did not remember the name of the guard who relieved him three nights before the coup.

He remembered a song his brother hated but could not sing the second verse.

He knew when someone touched the door.

He did not know when someone stood motionless three paces away.

That made Miren laugh despite herself.

"The family resemblance extends to the house."

Luceran gave her a look.

Iven said, "Who said that?"

Miren stepped closer.

"I did."

"Lady Miren?"

"Yes."

"You still owe me four silver."

She stared at the door.

Rhyse turned very slowly.

Miren said, "That is not relevant."

"It is to me."

Mrs. Marrick laughed again.

This time Tomas joined her.

The laughter broke something open in the gatehouse.

For five minutes, Iven was not a theological crisis.

He was a dead man embarrassing someone who had known him.

That was what made the danger bearable.

It was also what made it attractive.

---

The first refusal came before supper.

A guard named Pel Ordan approached from the yard.

Iven said, "Stop."

Pel stopped.

Rhyse looked up from the test ledger.

"Why?"

"He fought inside."

Pel's face went white.

"I surrendered."

"I know."

"I was cleared for yard duty."

Rhyse said, "By whom?"

"Captain Seran."

Rhyse looked at the clerk.

The clerk checked the slate.

"He is cleared."

Luceran felt everyone waiting.

The door had done exactly what a loyal dead guard might do.

It had remembered danger.

It had also been wrong about authority.

Rhyse walked to the door.

"Iven. Pel Ordan is permitted through."

Silence.

Then: "I don't trust him."

Pel looked as if he might cry.

Rhyse said, "You don't have to."

The door remained shut.

Luceran stepped forward.

"Iven."

Mrs. Marrick looked at him sharply.

He remembered the rule.

Not ownership.

Not punishment.

"I need to understand. Are you refusing because you believe he presents an immediate threat, or because you do not trust him?"

"Because I don't trust him."

Rhyse nodded.

"Then open."

The latch lifted.

Pel did not move.

"Go," Rhyse said.

Pel crossed the threshold.

Iven said, "I still don't trust you."

Pel stopped on the other side.

"Fair."

He kept walking.

Rhyse looked at Luceran.

"There."

Luceran knew what he meant.

A perfect servant was not perfect because it never disagreed.

It was perfect because the king could always win.

The thought came easily.

Too easily.

Mrs. Marrick said, "He was stubborn alive."

The door said, "Still am."

Luceran smiled.

He was relieved.

That was dangerous too.

---

At dusk, Merel asked Iven whether he wanted to leave.

The gatehouse emptied except for family, witnesses, and the people who had signed the first rules.

"Leave the door?" Iven asked.

"Service," Merel said.

"Can I come back?"

Everyone looked at the mask.

Luceran authorized the answer.

"If the place remains prepared."

Iven was quiet.

Mrs. Marrick touched the oak.

"You do not have to stay for us."

"I know."

"You do not have to stay for him."

Luceran felt that.

Good.

He deserved to.

"I know."

Tomas said, "You could go."

"I know."

"Then why aren't you?"

The knots in the door did not move.

Nothing about the oak changed.

Iven said, "Because I died here."

His mother closed her eyes.

"And?"

"And I was angry."

"At whom?"

"Everyone."

That sounded like him too.

"I thought if I lived, I would quit."

Rhyse made a small sound.

Iven continued.

"Then the gate opened. Jalen went down. Marr—"

He stopped.

Mara said, "You can say it."

"I heard them coming through. I knew if the corner went, they would shoot into the yard. I stayed because Rhyse told us to hold."

Rhyse's jaw tightened.

"I did."

"I know."

"You could have fallen back."

"I know."

That was the second time the dead man had said it to someone living.

It carried more weight now.

"I stayed because it mattered. I don't know if this matters the same way."

Merel said, "Then you may take time."

"Do dead men have that?"

Calienne answered.

"Apparently."

Iven laughed.

The door creaked with it.

Luceran felt the sound in his teeth.

Iven said, "I'll stay until I decide."

Mrs. Marrick nodded.

"Then I will come complain about it."

"I assumed."

Tomas put his palm against the wood.

"Can you feel that?"

"Yes."

He left it there.

---

The argument began after the family went to eat.

Damaris started it.

"We cannot pay him."

Rhyse stared at her.

"That is your concern?"

"It is one of them."

"He is dead."

"Yes. Which makes the ordinary rules for wages inconvenient."

Luceran sat on the gatehouse bench.

"What did he earn?"

Damaris named the amount.

"Continue it to his mother."

"No."

Luceran looked up.

"No?"

"That turns his service into family income and makes consent filthy."

Miren leaned against the wall.

"She's right."

"His mother depended on his money."

"Then compensate his household because he died in royal service," Damaris said. "Separately. Do not make her livelihood depend on convincing her dead son to remain useful."

Luceran stared at her.

Then nodded.

"Write it."

Ysabet, who had not stopped writing all day, made an exhausted noise.

"I am beginning to hate those words."

"Write that too."

She did not smile.

She wrote the rule.

Merel added burial rights.

Calienne added that no healer would be asked to certify willingness.

Rhyse added that a bound keeper could not replace living watches.

Vess added that every prepared threshold required a physical isolation point so the silver connection could be broken if necessary.

Miren added that family access could not be treated as royal favor.

Luceran added nothing for a while.

Then he said, "No dead servant may be the sole witness against the living."

Rhyse looked at him.

"Good."

It pleased Luceran too much.

He noticed.

That was becoming a habit.

---

Night settled over Edselsto.

The gatehouse returned to work.

Living guards changed watch.

A clerk carried the new rules to the archive.

Vess covered the silver socket with a removable iron plate.

Merel left a lamp burning beside the door, then came back and extinguished it because Iven complained it shone directly into whatever passed for his eyes.

Nobody knew whether he had eyes.

That did not stop the complaint from working.

Luceran was last to leave.

Miren waited in the yard.

He knew she was waiting without looking.

Iven said, "Your Majesty."

Luceran stopped.

"Yes?"

"Thank you."

"For what?"

"Letting my mother decide things."

Luceran almost said I let you decide.

He stopped himself.

That was not what Iven had said.

"She did not seem inclined to wait for permission."

"No."

Luceran touched the door.

The wood was cold.

"Do you remember the mask asking you?"

"Some of it."

"What did it feel like?"

"Like hearing someone from the other side of a wall."

Luceran's hand remained on the oak.

"Did you know what it was?"

"No."

"Did it tell you?"

"No."

"Did you trust it?"

Iven was silent for so long Luceran wondered whether the question had exceeded whatever remained of him.

Then:

"I trusted the gate."

Luceran looked at the black iron straps.

"That is not the same thing."

"No."

The answer bothered him.

Good.

Miren called from the yard.

"Luceran."

He stepped away.

"Good night, Iven."

"Good night, Your Majesty."

Luceran crossed the threshold.

The door opened for him without being asked.

He stopped on the other side.

"Iven."

"Yes?"

"Did you open that?"

"Yes."

Luceran looked back.

"Why?"

"Because you're allowed through."

Simple.

Useful.

Obedient to rule rather than mood.

For one beautiful second, Luceran understood exactly why a kingdom might want a thousand doors like this.

Then he saw Mrs. Marrick's broken cup still waiting for a servant to sweep it away.

He understood the price was not silver.

Miren took his hand.

"Come on."

Behind them, a living guard took post beside the dead one.

Neither was permitted to replace the other.

Not yet.
