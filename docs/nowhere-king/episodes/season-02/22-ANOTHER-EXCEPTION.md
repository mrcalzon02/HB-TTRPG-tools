# Season Two, Episode 22 — Another Exception

Kett had washed the mud from his hands before he came to claim his money. He had not managed to get it out of the cracks around his nails.

His cart had carried roof timber from the lower sawpit to the receiving yard, where Pell's men held it until the wind eased. He had been promised eight pennies for the journey. He said Tomas had promised another four if the cart and mule were kept beyond the second evening bell.

The hire slip said eight.

"I would have taken my beast home," Kett told Aldren, the junior clerk. "You had her standing at the west gate while the men argued whether the beam would clear the arch."

"The slip is signed for eight," Aldren said.

"And the four were spoken after it was signed."

Aldren remembered the conversation at the receiving arch, but not its words. If he paid twelve from an eight-penny warrant, Damaris would ask for his authority. If he paid eight, Kett would not have enough to settle the mule's stall fee and the man who helped unload.

He brought the slip, the west-gate watch sheet and Tomas's yard daybook to Ysabet. The daybook showed the cart had waited past the bell; it recorded no additional price.

"Have you asked Tomas?" she said.

"He's inspecting the second roof bay."

"Then you have not finished asking the living."

Aldren reddened. "The cartman wants to leave before the road turns to mud again."

Ysabet looked at Kett's wet cloak. The clerk was not trying to spy on a lover or ruin a rival. He wanted to pay a worker without entering a false charge in the Crown's book. She ordered bread for Kett and a place to tie his mule while Tomas was fetched. Neither was charged against his hire.

---

In the kitchen Etta was being scolded for the copper pans.

The mistress had not objected to her keeping the question tally. She objected to the time it took, which was now being counted as kitchen time. Ysabet had asked Etta twice to distinguish words she heard from the castle herself from answers servants merely repeated.

"You can keep your careful papers when the plates are washed," the mistress said.

"She wanted the original too."

"Then let the keeper wash the pans."

Etta was tempted to say that Ysabet had never held a greasy pan in her life. The kitchen mistress was tired, the roof men needed broth, and the firewood was damp. If Etta became a useful copyist, someone else would have to scrub what she left behind.

Instead she said, "I can write after supper."

"And then fall asleep carrying hot water?"

The mistress studied Etta's cramped hand. Beside each question Etta had written whether she heard the answer herself. She had left spaces blank when she was elsewhere.

"You're not to fill those blanks because a page says he remembers."

"I wasn't going to."

"I know. That's why I'm letting you keep it."

The mistress put aside a candle end and a clean scrap of paper. Etta could use the table after supper, but morning pans still needed washing. The mistress would ask Damaris whether copying for Ysabet could be paid as household work.

Etta was pleased and embarrassed. A question had made her valuable, and being valuable was already giving other people work.

---

Tomas returned with a splinter under his thumb and no patience for a hearing.

"I said I would see Kett paid if the cart stood after second bell. I didn't say four pennies. I may have said the gate charge would be met."

Kett stood stiff with the effort of not shouting.

"You said four. I asked if that was in addition to eight."

"I remember the argument over the beam. I don't remember saying four."

"Then you don't remember the whole conversation."

"No," Tomas said. "I don't."

Ysabet found the admission more useful than the quarrel. The watch had entered Kett's cart before second bell and its departure after third. Pell's tally showed the timber remained aboard until the wind dropped. A laborer heard Tomas promise something extra, but not the amount. The signed slip established eight pennies. The delay was not disputed.

"I can pay eight today," Damaris said. "I can hear the claim for the other four on the living evidence. It's not impossible."

Kett looked at the sun through the arch. "How long is a claim?"

"Before the paymaster closes."

"With respect, mistress, I've heard that before."

Aldren produced his carefully written question: *What words concerning Kett's additional hire were spoken in the receiving passage after the slip was signed?*

"The fourth clause forbids a general search of private talk," Ysabet said.

"It isn't general," Aldren replied.

"No. That's what makes it harder."

Damaris could see how a quick answer might spare Kett the wait. She could also see that the same answer might be used to deny him money. She had recently protected another carter from a false accusation founded on the castle's account of a delivery.

"I won't enter a wall's answer as a wage warrant," she said.

"Nor will I," Ysabet answered. "But the king must decide whether it may be sought at all."

Tomas stared. "You're going to trouble His Majesty over four pennies?"

"Over the thing that happens next time there are four pennies."

---

Luceran heard the matter in the chamber where the six clauses had been signed. He had expected news about the river or the northern grain. Instead he received a wet cartman's testimony and three ordinary papers that disagreed about an ordinary sum.

He asked Kett whether he wanted the castle consulted.

"If it gets me my money."

"If it says you are owed nothing?"

"Then I want to hear what it says, and why."

Tomas agreed to a limited inquiry but did not like the idea of every bargaining conversation being available afterward to someone with a better pen. Aldren explained that the receiving passage was connected, the question named a particular bargain, and both speakers were present. The signed hire slip and the watch record would remain available for comparison.

"You may authorize a named inquiry," Ysabet said. "The clauses do not give the castle authority to settle contracts. But they were not written so that every forgotten conversation could be retrieved merely because retrieval was convenient."

"Can you make the question safe?" Luceran asked.

"I can make the people who use its answer accountable. That is not the same thing."

The king looked at the papers. Kett's need was real. The Crown had kept the cart. The castle might remember words that no living witness could supply. Luceran also recognized how much he wanted this small dispute to prove that he could use Edselsto wisely.

He ordered one question, asked once in the connected receiving passage, with Kett, Tomas, Aldren, Ysabet and a witness chosen by Kett present. No other speech could be sought. The answer would be copied as a report, not a finding; Damaris would decide payment from all evidence. Both parties could challenge the entry before the paymaster. The original hire slip and watch sheet would remain attached. No penalty could follow merely from a contradiction between a person and the castle.

He signed the order. Ysabet countersigned only to attest its limits and the witnesses, not the truth of any future answer.

"You have your safeguards," Luceran said.

"I have the ones you agreed to today."

He heard the difference and did not rebuke her. That restraint pleased him more than it should have.

---

The receiving passage smelled of lime and damp sawdust. Henn had hung the recovered hook where Pell could find it without opening anyone's chest.

Kett chose the west-gate watchman as his witness. Aldren read the authorized question. Ysabet checked the visible silver tile and the place where the bridge entered the arch.

"Edselsto," Luceran said, "what did you hear spoken here concerning Kett's additional hire after the slip was signed?"

The pale mask gathered in the stone.

"I heard a man say, 'Four more if you must wait beyond the second bell.' Another asked, 'Four for the wait?' The first said, 'Yes, if the beam cannot be moved.'"

Tomas rubbed his face.

"That sounds like me."

"Do you remember it now?" Ysabet asked.

"I remember saying the beam could not be moved. I cannot swear to those exact words."

Kett said, "I remember them."

Edselsto had not named the speakers. It had not declared the legal meaning of the words, and no one could establish whether the reported exchange was complete. Ysabet had Aldren read back the answer from his written copy. She did not ask Edselsto to repeat itself. A second telling from the same source would not make a second witness.

Damaris compared the reported words with the signed slip, the gate sheet, the work tally, Kett's account and Tomas's admission that he had promised something for the delay. She judged the additional four pennies payable. Tomas signed the supplementary warrant without claiming a recovered perfect memory.

Kett counted twelve pennies into two piles. Four would go to the man who helped unload; the remainder would pay the stall keeper and buy feed for the journey. He thanked Damaris and the watchman. He did not thank the wall.

The household had spent a candle, a clerk's afternoon, a royal hearing and the time of several witnesses to settle four pennies. It had also acquired a precedent that could be copied far more cheaply next time.

---

Rhyse came to the copy room carrying a request he had already refused to send.

The officer guarding Beren Kalt wanted to know whether Edselsto had heard Beren speak to his wife during a supervised visit. The officer said he was concerned that the conversation might reveal a place where the family could be threatened.

"The wife was there," Ysabet said.

"Yes."

"Has he asked her?"

"No. He thought the wall would be less embarrassing."

"Less embarrassing to whom?"

"To the officer."

Beren's wife had already objected to being made to bargain for her husband's release. She had brought stockings and the names of two children. A guarded family visit was not an ordinary hire negotiation just because an officer could stand nearby.

"Do not submit it," Ysabet said.

"I refused it," Rhyse answered. "I brought it because someone will submit the next one without asking me."

He knew that if the threat became immediate enough, he might be grateful for any warning that protected the children. He was not certain that he would always refuse. That troubled him more than the younger officer's clumsy request.

Ysabet entered a limiting note with the hearing: Kett's exception authorized no retrieval from protected visits, sleeping rooms, confessional spaces or any other conversation beyond the named work dispute. She sent copies to the watch and kitchen.

"Will it hold?" Rhyse asked.

"Until someone with authority orders otherwise."

---

Miren heard about the exception from the kitchen mistress, who had come to ask whether Etta's copying time could be paid. Miren sent the request to Damaris with a note that the girl should not be expected to do unpaid copying after a full kitchen shift. That was not an order to create an office; it was a request to pay for an hour of real work.

Then Miren asked for the exception sheet.

She read Luceran's signature, Ysabet's limiting words and the witness list. She could see why Kett had agreed. She could see why Tomas had not objected. She could see that the answer had been useful.

Luceran found her in the south-gallery room after supper. The silver break remained open in the wall.

"You have read it," he said.

"I have."

"The man was paid."

"He was owed money before you asked."

"Now we know what was said."

"We know what Edselsto reported hearing."

He was tired of being corrected. He also knew she had chosen her words carefully.

"Would you have left him unpaid?"

"No. Damaris could have heard the claim on living evidence. You chose the faster answer because it was there."

"Because it could be checked."

"Today. Tomorrow someone will ask about a person who is absent."

Luceran sat without asking her to join him. Beyond the deaf wall, carts rattled over the repaired yard stones. The second roof bay was nearly ready for the remaining sacks. There were wages to pay, grain to move and horses the Crown could no longer send to every hamlet.

"I thought I was proving I could use it carefully," he said.

"You proved that you can make an exception carefully."

He sat with that distinction. Miren did not soften it. She did not leave either. When the kitchen bell sounded, they went their separate ways.

---

The next morning Aldren found three petitions waiting at the receiving desk. One concerned a missing length of chain. One concerned a mason's claimed promise of overtime. The third asked whether Edselsto remembered who had stood outside a private door.

He knew enough to refuse the third. He was less sure about the first two.

He made a fair copy of yesterday's order and left blank spaces for a name, a room, a question, witnesses and a royal signature. No signature would be added without the king. He told himself that this made the form harmless.

Tomas saw the sheet. "Wasn't yesterday's request supposed to be exceptional?"

"It was. This is for when another one is needed."

Tomas had no ready answer. The paper was not a command. It could not make Edselsto speak. But it was already easier to fill out than to begin the argument again.

Damaris authorized a single paid hour of copying for Etta, and the kitchen mistress required the replacement work to be entered. At the west gate Kett's mule went down the road with feed in its nosebag. The watchman recorded its departure in the ordinary book.

A rider from Dols brought no permission for another grain cart. The river remained high. Six sacks were still in the far-bank store, six under Edselsto's roof, and none at the northern posts. No amount of recollection could make a carrier or a dry crossing.

Ysabet received Aldren's blank form before midday. She could tear it up. She could forbid its use without another signed order. She could also see that the household would continue producing disputes, and the king would continue wanting quick answers.

She filed the blank sheet behind the exception, not among the standing forms. At the bottom she wrote: *Not authority. No use without a new inquiry.*

It was a true warning. It was also a place where the next request could begin.

In the family nave, all seven candles burned.
