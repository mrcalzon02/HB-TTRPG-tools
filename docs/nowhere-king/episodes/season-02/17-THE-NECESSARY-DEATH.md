# Season Two, Episode 17 — The Necessary Death

Yara Fen went down to the village to inspect a room her employer might never sleep in.

The house belonged to a cooper's widow who rented two chambers above her shop. One window faced the road. The other opened onto a yard where barrels stood beneath old sailcloth. The widow showed Yara the back stair without being asked.

"My master has a bad knee," Yara said. "He can manage stairs. He dislikes being surprised by them."

"Then he will dislike the last one."

The bottom step had sunk. The widow promised to wedge it before evening. Yara insisted on seeing the timber. A promise about a stair was a promise about a body, and Corven had lately been required to trust too many of those.

She counted the keys, checked both latches and asked whether the rear lane remained passable after rain. The three sealed river-bench warrants stayed in her own case. She had not brought them from Edselsto to place them in a stranger's cupboard.

Corven's messenger arrived with a letter to Leora. Her mother's cough had improved. He hoped to come home without abandoning the work he had crossed the mountains to do. The last line had been added in haste.

Yara folded it before the widow could read it. Corven had not asked her to approve his marriage or his courage. He had asked her to get a room ready.

At the village gate she made certain the watch clerk understood that the Crown escort would end there. Corven's own attendants would remain with him, and none of them was a prisoner. The clerk entered the instruction, though he disliked it.

---

At Dols the miller's drain had become a ditch six paces long.

The carpenter had fitted the timber lip. The bank still needed inspection after the next rain, and the watch captain had not marked a load limit. Savelin watched the miller probe the edge with his own staff.

"Will the western turn hold?" he asked.

"After it dries and we test it," the carpenter said.

"How long?"

"The weather has not promised me a day."

The miller insisted that the Crown remain answerable for damage to his wall. The reeve agreed. The consent was for a drain, not a surrender of water rights or an open bridge. Savelin paid the workers for their hours and sent a messenger uphill with the measured work and the next inspection request. No cart crossed. No military countersign existed.

The ferryman offered Savelin supper.

"You always have to go back to him," he said.

"Usually."

"Then tell him the ditch is real. So is the river."

Savelin carried that distinction up the hill. He did not know that another royal message had already begun its journey by a different road.

---

Rhyse brought Luceran the escort roll before noon.

"Four Doorwardens to the village gate," he said. "Two return at once. Two remain available if the League party asks for them."

"Could you keep all four with him?"

"Not without his agreement."

"And if I commanded it?"

"I would tell him the command was yours. If you meant to confine him, we would have to call it confinement."

Luceran looked at the list of names.

"Suppose I withdrew your protection."

"Then I would ask why."

"You would not obey?"

"I would obey an order to stand down. I would also warn the man whose protection you had withdrawn, unless you forbade it before a witness."

Luceran set the roll down.

"Keep the escort."

Rhyse bowed without thanking him. A captain did not owe gratitude for being allowed to do his duty.

After he left, Luceran summoned the master of the lesser household couriers. Those men did not belong to Rhyse's Doorwardens. They carried private sealed messages, returned misdirected baggage and fetched people whose names seldom appeared in the public household book.

Luceran selected two veterans of the road service. Beren Kalt had spent the morning disputing arrears for a horse lost in the coup. Olan Sere wanted winter work nearer his wife in the lower hamlet. Both had served the Crown when its authority was less comfortable than it appeared today.

The king wrote a private instruction concerning Corven's passage beyond the village gate and the return of his remains by the west service way. It was not an arrest warrant. The men understood what he intended before he signed it.

Beren put the paper down.

"My brother died at the north gate," he said. "I have followed your orders since before the coup. I will not follow this one."

Luceran could have called the refusal treason. Instead he ordered Beren retained in the household until the evening account. It was not freedom, however politely the order was phrased.

Olan asked about the attendants.

"They are not the object of this instruction," Luceran said.

"They will try to protect him."

"I know."

The courier master named two more men from his service. One had been promised a place in the winter messenger relay. The other was owed wages. Their names went onto the private roll. Olan requested a packhorse and clean cloth. Luceran approved both.

He had imagined an act that belonged to him alone. A courier master now knew. A veteran had refused. Three men had accepted. A groom would remember the horse, and a clerk would see the wages.

Luceran signed anyway.

Corven spent another hour with Damaris and a Merehaven factor discussing the proposed first-cargo bond.

"You have twelve sacks," the factor said.

"We have twelve counted sacks," Corven corrected. "We have a roof over those twelve and not over the others. That is why I have recommended a smaller undertaking."

Damaris agreed. The smith still had a claim for the remaining nails, the next cart had not been hired, and she would not call future grain present merely because the king wanted it. Corven asked after Bram's fingers and wrote a note about the porter who was directing carts from a bench.

At last he took leave of Luceran in the outer court, where witnesses could hear.

"I will write when the benches answer," Corven said.

"I hope you will stay."

"That will depend on what you ask of me next."

Corven offered his hand. Luceran took it. He felt the stiff fingers and remembered Edselsto's assurance that a hand might continue to make signatures.

Rhyse escorted the party down the hill. At the village gate Corven thanked him and dismissed the Crown guard as agreed. He went onward with two attendants, his travelling chest and a pack animal. Rhyse watched until the party turned toward the cooper's lane.

There was an alarm from that lane before the afternoon watch ended.

The surviving attendant reached the village gate without his companion or his master. He could describe several men, a struggle, and the direction in which the pack animal had been taken. He could not name anyone. The other attendant was found injured beside the lane and taken to the widow's house for care.

Corven was gone. So was his body. His chest, broken walking stick and the letter he had meant to send Leora remained behind.

Yara heard the alarm while she was counting the room keys for a second time.

---

Ysabet discovered the private courier roll because its keeper refused to certify a blank hour.

He had been told to enter a west-road journey against a sealed royal instruction. It named a packhorse and three men but no ordinary cargo. He had also been told not to write when they returned.

"I can enter a sealed errand," he told Ysabet. "I cannot enter an hour that did not happen."

She went to the king.

Luceran was in the old mortuary beside the west service passage. The room had a stone washing table, a drain and a door through which the dead had been brought for generations without passing through the family nave. It had been kept in repair after the coup because the court had needed it.

Corven lay beneath a sheet on the table. A washer had been summoned and then turned away. That woman, too, would remember.

Ysabet stopped at the threshold.

"Who ordered this?"

"I did," Luceran said.

"Who carried it out?"

"Men of the household road service."

"Where is Yara?"

"In the village."

"Rhyse must know. And the League."

"They will."

"Before you do anything more."

Luceran told her about Beren's refusal, the courier master, and the signed instruction. He did not say Corven had threatened the Crown. He did not pretend the three benches had surrendered their warrants. He spoke instead of the northern posts, the unfinished bridge and the time the living negotiations had consumed.

"You have not learned that no other way would work," Ysabet said. "You have feared it."

"I know."

"Then do not call this necessity. Call it your choice."

He looked at the sheet. "I chose it."

Ysabet demanded the original instruction. He gave it to her. She set it with the earlier sealed refusal and the recorded question. Three separate documents described three separate decisions; the later one did not erase the earlier two.

She would not certify the death as an accident. Luceran did not ask her to.

---

Edselsto answered when Luceran called from the mortuary's connected wall.

The pale mask appeared above the stone table.

"You offered a service," Luceran said. "I have brought the body."

"Yes, Your Majesty."

"What is required?"

"A prepared place, silver, and a remainder willing to answer."

Ysabet asked whether those things had ever been proved sufficient before a living witness. Edselsto offered no such proof. It gave instructions, not evidence.

Vess came expecting a damaged drain and stopped at the doorway. He refused to prepare the silver. Luceran ordered a piece from the royal store under his own seal. A lesser smith fitted it to the old recess while Vess stood aside. The smith saw the sheet, the mask and the signature. He was paid for his work. He would not later mistake it for a hinge repair.

"Whose remainder?" Ysabet asked.

Edselsto named Jalen Orris, who had fallen at the gate during the coup.

Luceran remembered Iven Marrick speaking Jalen's name from the oak door. He remembered the rule made at Iven's mother's insistence: death in royal service did not give the Crown ownership of what remained. Jalen had never agreed while alive to become a keeper, much less to inhabit a stranger.

"Ask him," Luceran said.

For a long time nothing answered. Then a voice came from the recess.

"Is the gate lost?"

"No," Luceran said. "We retook it."

"Who speaks?"

"Your king."

The voice remembered a gate, a fallen comrade and the sound of men crossing stone. It did not remember how long it had been dead. Asked about his family, Jalen recalled a sister who had once taken his boots to be mended. He did not know whether she had married.

Ysabet said, "You did not consent to this service in life."

"No," Jalen answered.

"You may refuse."

"What service?"

Luceran explained that he wanted Jalen to occupy the body and speak to the river councils in Corven's place. He did not say this would restore Corven. He did not say the man on the table had wanted it.

"Would I know his people?" Jalen asked.

"Not as he knew them."

"Would I be a soldier?"

"No. An envoy."

"I was poor at letters."

"The castle says the hand may remember."

Jalen was silent. At length he asked whether the northern soldiers were hungry. Luceran told him that shortage threatened them and that the roads and councils had not yet provided enough.

"Then I will try," Jalen said. "I cannot promise to be him."

Ysabet refused to record the answer as lawful consent under Iven's rule. Jalen had died without knowing what was now asked of him, and Corven had never been offered a choice. The king heard the objections. He had the power to proceed despite them.

"Let him enter," Luceran said.

The silver cooled beneath the smith's hand. The man on the table drew a breath. His fingers closed around the stone edge. When he tried to stand, his left knee failed him.

He looked down in surprise.

"Your Majesty," he said.

The voice was close enough to Corven's to make Ysabet step back. The next words were not.

"Where is the gate?"

Luceran stood beside a man whose return could not restore what he had taken. The first village messenger was already climbing the hill with news of an attack and a missing body. Rhyse had been summoned. Yara still held all three warrants, and the river benches had granted nothing more than they had granted that morning.

Luceran had obtained obedience. He had not obtained Corven.

In the family nave, all seven candles continued to burn.
