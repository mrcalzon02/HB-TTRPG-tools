# Season Two, Episode 8 — Rules for Miracles

The king had been saved by a sound.

By morning the sound had acquired witnesses, a physician's account, a torn coat, two knives, a prisoner, and three different stories about how close the blade had come to Luceran's throat. One version said Edselsto had seen the murderer through stone. Another said the castle had seized the man's wrist. The third, repeated by a kitchen boy who had not been near the lower archives, said the walls had spoken before anyone drew a weapon.

None was true.

The true account was frightening enough.

---

Oren Bale could bend three fingers without help. The fourth bent only when Calienne's assistant warmed his hand in a bowl of water, and even then he had to bite the inside of his cheek.

"Do that again," she said.

"I should like to do it less."

"Then do it properly the first time."

Oren had been a Doorwarden for eleven years. He had held the western threshold when the coup was broken, carried a fevered man down a stair that later burned, and once spent six nights in the rain while a visiting duke disputed whether his own household guards could stand inside the royal gate. He had never imagined that the most dangerous part of his service might be the back of his hand.

His wife, Lysa Bale, sat beside the bed with their youngest child's shoe in her lap. She had brought it to mend while she waited. There was a split at the toe and a pebble lodged in the stitching.

"The pay is ordered," Oren said.

"Ordered is not received."

"Rhyse gave his word."

"I believe him. The baker does not take Captain Rhyse's word for bread."

The assistant looked up from the bandage.

"Damaris's clerk brought the first payment before dawn."

Lysa put the shoe down.

"Then I shall believe the clerk."

Rhyse arrived as she was tying the repaired lace. He had not slept enough, and the smell of wet wool followed him into the infirmary.

"How is the hand?"

"Attached."

"That is a beginning."

Oren held up the fingers he could move.

"I heard the other guard challenge him. I saw the cloth. I did not see the second blade until it was coming out."

"You were not meant to be fighting two blades in that passage."

"Nevertheless."

Rhyse pulled a stool close.

"I need your account again. The clerk will write it. You may correct him."

Oren studied his captain.

"Why? You know what happened."

"I know what I saw. I need what you saw."

"Because the castle said left?"

"Because by supper people will be saying it knew the man's name."

Lysa's needle stopped.

"Did it?"

"No," Rhyse said. "It heard metal. The guard had already called a halt. If we allow those facts to become a miracle, the next man who hears a wall speak will stop looking with his own eyes."

Oren flexed his good hand.

"Then write that I was late."

"I shall write that you held a man with two knives long enough for the king to get clear."

"That is kinder."

"It is also true."

Lysa looked from one man to the other.

"If the castle can warn a guard, let it warn him. But if you decide it can replace my husband, tell me before he loses the other hand."

Rhyse did not promise her anything. He asked her to come to the council's open hearing after midday.

She agreed, provided someone would mind the children.

The assistant said she would find someone.

That was how the first witness to the new royal rules was recruited: not by summons, but because she had a question the men who made the rules had not yet answered.

---

Below the east receiving yard, Hesta Vale was conducting an argument that had begun before the assassination and had not improved with the news.

The officer at the Crown-track barrier had charged her carters four pennies for their four wheels. He called it a wheel fee. Hesta called it a toll wearing a different hat.

Tomas Reed had found the order.

"It authorizes payment for emergency bridge planking," he said.

"From whom?"

"Commercial wagons not covered by Crown passage."

"My bargain covers Crown passage."

"Yes."

"Then the four pennies?"

"Must be returned."

Hesta folded her arms.

"I should like that in writing before the officer invents a fee for the horse."

Tomas had a clerk preparing the answer. He also had a mason asking for nails, a carter asking who paid for a broken axle, and a stack of timber receipts damp enough to threaten the ink. He offered Hesta a place beside the hearth until the writing was done.

She stayed in the yard instead.

Vess and Pell Falbrecht were standing before the service recess through which Marek Venn had entered. The old planks were down. A mason was measuring the opening for a proper timber barrier. Two guards watched the workers, and a third watched the road.

Hesta pointed to the black ear tile beside the receiving door.

"Is that one of the places where the castle hears?"

"Yes," Vess said.

"And the recess?"

"No verified silver through there."

"Then the mark told the man where not to be heard."

"It told everyone where they might be heard," Vess replied. "Including the men who had a right to know."

Pell rubbed lime from his sleeve.

"If we hide the marks, workers will not know whether a quarrel with a foreman is being carried to the king. If we mark every wall, we will lie about what the walls can do."

Hesta considered the unfinished barrier.

"Then perhaps the guards ought to know every hole large enough to hide a man."

"They will," Pell said. "I am making them walk the works with the foremen before each royal inspection."

"And when the works change?"

"They walk again."

Vess showed her the new survey. A line of silver passed through the receiving passage, stopped at a verified break, and did not reach the recess. Beside it he had drawn the proposed timber closure, the watch position, and the cart standing ground.

"His Majesty wants another listening branch through the yard," Vess said.

Hesta looked up.

"Does he?"

"He has not ordered it. He asked what it would cost."

"What did you tell him?"

"Silver, two skilled men, three days if the stone behaves, and the loss of a working passage while we cut it. Then I told him the wall would still not know whether a man carrying a knife intended to cut rope or a throat."

Pell gave a grim smile.

"His Majesty likes the first half of an answer better than the second."

"Most patrons do," Hesta said. "My grandfather once asked how many carts we could move in a day. He was very cross when I included the horses."

She watched the masons lift the first timber into place. A cart could not pass until they finished. The lime would have to be carried round by hand. That cost time, and time cost wages.

A listening branch was not a free precaution. Neither was a closed door.

Hesta had come to secure her family's right to a road. She now wanted to know whether a royal order could make that road, or the rooms beside it, into an invisible witness against her carters.

She asked Tomas for a second sheet of paper.

---

Ysabet had prepared six clauses.

Luceran had written objections beside four of them.

The council sat in the chamber where the walls were known to be deaf. Rhyse brought the guard accounts. Damaris brought the accounts of the guard accounts: wages, physicians, timber, stopped labor, and the cost of keeping men at doors while the work was interrupted. Vess carried the silver survey. Miren came without notes. Canon Merel carried a small book of burial prayers, though nobody had died.

Edselsto did not attend.

That was the first dispute.

"How do we make rules for a servant without asking the servant what it can do?" Luceran asked.

"We may ask it," Ysabet said. "We need not let it sit as judge of its own answers."

"It has answered truthfully when we have caught its limits."

"It has answered as it understands the question. That is not the same thing as proof."

Rhyse spread the passage drawing on the table.

"The guard saw movement. The castle heard a blade. I saw the attack. Three pieces of knowledge. If we give them all the same name, we lose the reason the king survived."

Luceran touched the mended edge of his collar.

"I will not have a rule that prevents the wall from saying left."

"Nor will I," Rhyse said. "I want a rule that says what happens after it speaks."

Ysabet read the first clause aloud.

"In connected operational passages, Edselsto may report a sound it directly hears: a drawn blade, a cry for help, a fall, breaking timber, a gate forced, a fire taking hold. It shall report what was heard and where the sound appeared to come from, not declare the intention, guilt, identity, or lawful status of any person."

Damaris lifted a hand.

"A falling barrel sounds like a falling man in some passages."

"Then the report is of a fall or impact," Vess said. "Not a broken leg."

"Good," Rhyse said. "A warning to look, not a verdict."

Luceran marked his approval.

The second clause gave urgent warnings to the nearest living watch and the responsible captain or officer. If the king stood in immediate danger, Edselsto might warn him directly, as it had done. A warning could justify stopping movement, taking cover, challenging an approaching person, or searching for the source of danger. It could not, alone, justify imprisonment beyond the immediate necessity of keeping people safe.

"And if a man runs when challenged?" Luceran asked.

"Then the guard has seen a man run," Rhyse said. "He may act upon that. The castle has not made the decision for him."

"What if the man is armed?"

"Then the guard has seen a weapon."

"What if no guard sees him?"

"Then we send one. I will not hang a man because a wall heard iron."

Luceran crossed out his second objection.

The third clause protected sleeping chambers, physicians' rooms, confessional spaces, privies, and the south-gallery withdrawing room. No new silver branch could be restored through those places without a written royal order naming the space, purpose, duration, and the living witnesses to the work. The south-gallery break would remain physically open.

Miren spoke then.

"The question is not only whether the castle hears. It is whether someone can ask it later to repeat a conversation the speakers believed private."

Luceran looked at her.

"I know."

"You know because you did it."

"Yes."

Ysabet set her pen down.

"Then the fourth clause must cover retrieval as well as alarms. No private conversation may be sought from the castle under a general warrant to investigate roads, theft, guards, or another matter. A named inquiry must say what it seeks. Anything overheard outside that purpose is not a gift to the Crown."

"Unless a life is at risk," Luceran said.

Miren did not immediately object.

Rhyse did.

"A life is always at risk somewhere in a castle full of armed men, horses, ladders and bad roofs. Make the exception that broad and the rule is decoration."

Canon Merel turned a page in the prayer book.

"Necessity is a reason a person may answer for what he has done. It is not a blessing pronounced in advance upon everything he may wish to do."

Luceran stared at the empty place beside the inkstand.

He wanted an exception large enough to have saved him yesterday if yesterday had happened differently. He also knew that no clause could have made him less frightened after the knife.

"An immediate peril witnessed or credibly reported," he said at last. "The officer may act to preserve life. The order and the reason are entered before the next change of watch. If I give the order myself, my name is entered. No one writes that the castle required it."

Ysabet nodded once.

"That can be judged afterward."

"And who judges me?"

No one hurried to answer.

Miren said, "The people who must live with what you did."

Luceran looked at her for a long moment.

"Put the answer in the record," he said.

Ysabet did not write Miren's words as a statute. She wrote that the sovereign's personal command did not erase responsibility for the command. That was as far as parchment could go.

---

The fifth clause nearly failed in the receiving passage.

Rhyse had asked the council to watch an ordinary work hour before deciding what an alarm meant. He did not ask anyone to pretend to be an assassin. The last man who had carried two knives there was still locked under guard; the captain had no taste for theatre.

A cooper came through the connected arch with a leather roll of tools under his arm. A cart horse shied at the sound of a dropped plank. The cooper stopped to help the carter free a twisted trace, drew a narrow blade from its sheath, and cut a strip of damaged harness.

Edselsto's pale mask appeared beside the marked door.

"Metal drawn from leather. East receiving arch."

The nearest Doorwarden raised a hand.

"Hold there."

The cooper froze with the knife still in his fingers.

"Have I done wrong?"

"Put the blade on the cart. Slowly."

He did.

Rhyse crossed the yard and looked at the cut trace, the frightened horse, and the cooper's trembling hands.

"What happened?"

"The strap tore. I cut it free."

The carter confirmed it. Pell showed them the damaged leather. The Doorwarden released the man and apologized for the alarm.

"I did not say danger," Edselsto observed.

"No," Rhyse said. "You said what you heard."

Luceran, standing with Ysabet at the outer gate, watched the cooper retrieve his knife.

The man was still shaking.

"He had reason to be frightened," Miren said quietly.

"Of the guard?"

"Of being named by a wall when he thought he was helping."

The Doorwarden returned to his post. He had challenged a man carrying a blade in a restricted working passage and had stopped when living evidence explained the act. That was a tolerable interruption, though not a pleasant one. A rule that had ordered the cooper seized would have made an innocent man's morning considerably worse.

Vess took them to the next turn, where a stone carrier stood behind the newly boarded recess. Edselsto had not reported him.

"Can you hear that man?" Luceran asked.

"No," the mask replied.

"Can you tell whether he carries a knife?"

"No."

"Can you remember every sound in this yard?"

"I can speak of sounds that reached me. I do not keep them as a clerk keeps a roll."

Ysabet looked sharply at Luceran. He had asked the question she wanted asked without turning the answer into an ability the castle had not demonstrated.

Rhyse dismissed the carrier.

"Then our watches remain watches."

Damaris inspected the halted carts.

"And our interruptions remain expensive. The rule should tell a captain when an alarm has proved mistaken, or every cart will be stopped until its driver dies of old age."

Ysabet added the fifth clause: the receiving officer must enter the warning, the action taken, the living evidence found, and whether the warning proved useful, mistaken, or unresolved. The subject of an alarm could ask for the entry to be corrected or for a witness to be heard. Repeated false alarms would require a review of the listening branch and the watch, not merely punishment of the people who happened to be nearby.

Hesta Vale, who had watched from beside the lime cart, asked a question nobody on the council had asked.

"Who reads the entry to a man who cannot read?"

Ysabet turned toward her.

"A clerk."

"Whose clerk?"

"The household's."

"Then let the man bring someone who can read it for him. A guild fellow, a reeve, his wife. Otherwise he has only the clerk's word that the correction was written."

Luceran nodded.

"Add it."

Hesta looked faintly surprised.

"That was not a request for favor."

"No," he said. "It was a better rule."

She bowed, but not as low as a courtier would have done. Then she returned to her carters.

Her four pennies had still not been returned.

---

The prisoner's account had not improved by afternoon.

Marek Venn repeated the name he had given at hiring and refused to identify the person who had promised he would find the king alone. He repeated that his brother was involved. He would not give a name that could be checked.

Rhyse kept the questioning brief.

Ysabet compared the strip of linen from beneath the lime cart with the wrapping taken from the attacker. The weave looked alike to the eye. The edges were worn differently. The cloth could have come from the same common bolt or from two separate rolls purchased in the same market.

"Can you say they match?" Rhyse asked the cloth merchant summoned to examine them.

"In kind."

"From the same piece?"

"No."

"Can you say they do not?"

"No."

Ysabet entered both answers.

The pay clerk's roll still proved only that Marek had been taken on as a carrier. The lodging keeper's memory still proved only that he had asked about the king's habits. The second blade still proved preparation without naming a patron. Nothing had made Deren Holt, the Red Ford riders, the earlier hunt, or the coup into one conspiracy.

Luceran read the account and frowned.

"We have so little."

"We have a living prisoner, two blades, a route, a broken watch procedure, witnesses, and a false name that may be true," Rhyse said. "That is not little. It is merely not the answer you want."

"Have you asked the castle?"

"About the unconnected yard? No."

"About the connected approach."

"It heard what it heard yesterday. We already have its words. Asking again does not make them a second witness."

Luceran shut the book.

For one dangerous moment he wanted Edselsto to be able to answer every question Rhyse could not.

He wanted the brother to exist, the sponsor to have a face, the road to lead somewhere, the prisoner to stop being silent.

He wanted uncertainty to be disobedience.

Rhyse watched him struggle with it and did not offer comfort.

"Find out who paid his lodging," Luceran said finally. "Follow the wages and the cloth. Give the guards the new passage sketch. Keep the prisoner alive."

"Yes, Your Majesty."

"Do not let him disappear between one watch and the next."

"He will not disappear for want of a living watch."

It was a rebuke.

Luceran accepted it.

---

Miren waited for him in the south-gallery withdrawing room.

The silver bridge had been removed. Vess had shown her the cut ends and the new stone covering. She trusted his workmanship more than any royal promise about what Edselsto might choose to hear.

There was a small table, two chairs, and a window looking toward the village roofs. The room was not beautiful. It had become precious because it was one place in the castle where silence was an actual property of stone and metal, not a favor granted by a listening servant.

Luceran came in without attendants.

"Ysabet has put my name in the account of the gallery," he said.

Miren stood by the window.

"I know. She showed me the wording."

"I did not ask her to soften it."

"That does not make it disappear."

"No."

He sat, then rose again because sitting while she stood felt like another small misuse of a room he had come to ask her to share.

"I keep trying to say the right thing," he said. "I think you know that."

"I do."

"And it is not enough."

"Not yet."

Outside, the workmen were moving timber into the east yard. A hammer sounded from the village, far enough away to be no one's alarm.

Miren came to the table.

"You were nearly killed. I was afraid. When I saw your blood, I wanted to hold you until the room stopped moving. I did hold you. That was true."

"I know."

"It is also true that you took something from me because you could. I had spoken to Ardel in a room where I thought the words belonged to us. You made the castle give them to you."

"I know."

"Do you understand why I cannot give you an answer about marriage while that is still between us?"

He almost said that he had not asked. He caught the defense before it became speech.

"Yes. You would have to wonder whether I would hear a refusal as a thing to be investigated."

She looked down.

"That is closer."

He took her hand only when she offered it.

"I thought if I knew what you feared, I could spare you the fear."

"You wanted to spare yourself not knowing."

He laughed once, without pleasure.

"That is also true."

Miren leaned against him. They stood that way long enough for the day to lose its sharpest edges.

She kissed the uninjured side of his neck.

He did not mistake the kiss for forgiveness, and she did not ask him to.

"I am glad you wrote the rule," she said.

"So am I."

"I am not glad we needed it."

"No."

She looked toward the door.

"Keep the room deaf."

"I will."

"And if one day you think there is a reason to open it?"

"I will tell you before anyone brings silver."

"That is a promise I can understand."

He wanted to ask whether she believed him. Instead he let the promise remain something he would have to keep.

They left the room together, still intimate, still not reconciled, and no closer to an answer about a wedding.

---

At the evening hearing, the sixth clause was the one that drew the most argument.

It concerned who might command Edselsto when the king was absent, and who might challenge a command when the king was present.

Ysabet proposed that ordinary requests pass through named household officers. Rhyse would govern urgent watch reports; Vess and the master workers would govern restoration and isolation of listening branches; the archive would keep the orders, their purposes, and their expiry. A temporary emergency order could be spoken if writing would cost a life, but the person who gave it must account for it before the next watch changed.

Canon Merel insisted that dead service remain governed by the consent and finite duties already established for Iven Marrick. No royal convenience could turn a deceased person's service into an inheritance owed by their family.

Damaris insisted that silver for new listening ways remain subject to ordinary survey and Crown expenditure, rather than a verbal wish addressed to the mask.

Pell wanted the right to stop work if a branch was being cut through a room not marked on the signed drawing.

"You can stop a roof for fear it will fall," he said. "I ought to be able to stop a wall for fear it will hear."

Several people laughed. He did not.

Rhyse wanted his guards empowered to challenge the king's movement during an active threat without losing their posts. Luceran gave the authority in writing, then asked Rhyse to make certain the guards understood that challenging him was not a license to humiliate him.

"I will instruct them to be polite while they save your life," Rhyse said.

"That will be a novelty."

Lysa Bale rose from the benches reserved for witnesses.

"My husband is in the infirmary," she said. "I was told the wall saved the king. I am grateful. But my husband was there too. If the wall is wrong and the guards act, who pays for the man they hurt?"

Damaris answered first.

"The Crown, where its officers are at fault. That obligation exists already."

"Then say it in the new order," Lysa replied. "If you are going to give the wall a place in the watch, do not let it take the blame that belongs to the living."

Ysabet added a line about injury, compensation, and the right to petition.

The last objection came from Luceran himself.

"Edselsto will still obey me if I ask it directly," he said. "I will not pretend a paper can prevent that."

The room grew quiet.

Ysabet said, "No. It cannot."

"Then what are we making?"

"A rule by which the rest of us may know what you have asked, whether it was lawful, and who must answer for the result."

Miren sat near the back, not beside the throne. Luceran could see her listening.

Rhyse put a hand on the table.

"A guard may disobey an unlawful order and stand before you to explain it. The castle may not know how. That is why these rules are for us."

Luceran looked at the signatures waiting beneath the text.

"Then we shall have to be worthy of them."

He signed.

Ysabet signed as keeper of the record. Rhyse, Damaris, Vess, and Canon Merel witnessed the provisions touching their offices. Copies went to the gates, the kitchens, the archive, the work yards, and the village reeve. The ear tiles would remain visible. The map of connected spaces would be open to workers and household staff, with the physically deaf rooms named. An accused person could seek correction through a clerk and bring a reader or witness of their choosing.

No rule made the castle less powerful.

The rules made it harder for living people to pretend that power had no owner.

Hesta received the signed order returning her four pennies before the hearing ended. She counted them, not because she doubted Tomas, but because her carters had watched the money taken.

Then she asked for a copy of the listening rules to carry to the quarry road.

"You may have one," Ysabet said.

"I expect I shall have questions."

"I expect you will."

Outside, the Doorwardens changed watches under the marked receiving arch. The cooper went home with his tools. Oren Bale's wages reached his wife. Marek Venn remained a prisoner whose sponsor was not known. Vess left the south-gallery silver break untouched.

The new order had already cost parchment, labor, silver withheld from other work, and the king a little of the pleasure of commanding.

It was an expensive thing to call a boundary.

---

The foreign letter arrived after the seals had cooled.

A courier in House Lethayne colors had ridden from the capital with correspondence concerning a delegation expected at Edselsto. The delegation represented interests whose cooperation the Crown had been seeking for months. Its appointed envoy had not yet given terms. The letter asked for safe lodging, proper precedence, and a place in which its members could confer without household servants, royal guards, or the ancestral castle itself overhearing their deliberations.

Damaris read the request twice.

"They have heard about the ears."

"Everyone has heard about the ears," Rhyse said.

"They have heard enough to ask the right question."

Luceran looked at the newly signed articles on his desk.

"Then we have an answer."

Ysabet studied the foreign seal.

"We have a rule. Whether they trust it is another matter."

Luceran had spent the day learning that a law could bind his own people only because they were willing to make him answer for breaking it. He wondered what a foreign envoy would demand before trusting a king who possessed a servant that could not refuse him.

He laid the letter beside the order.

"Prepare the deaf room for their inspection. Not for their use until they arrive. For inspection."

Rhyse nodded.

"And the delegation?"

"Receive them properly. Ask Lethayne what concessions they are likely to seek. Do not promise them anything before we know what they bring."

It was an ordinary royal instruction.

It would not remain easy.

In the family nave Canon Merel's keeper tended the seven ancestral flames. All seven burned steadily. No one had reported a change, and no one in the council had learned anything about what lay beneath them.

At the east receiving arch, the black ear tile remained where every worker could see it.

Edselsto's mask appeared to Luceran as he passed.

"Your Majesty. Shall I attend the foreign discussions?"

"Not unless you are lawfully called."

"I am at your service."

"I know."

The mask bowed.

Luceran continued toward the living voices at the end of the hall.

He had written down the difference between a warning and a judgment.

Now he would have to live with it.
