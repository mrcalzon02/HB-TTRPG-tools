# Season Two, Episode 29 — The Denial Conference

The Dols rider reached Edselsto with one boot dry and the other full of water.

He had crossed the lower ford before the morning rain, changed horses at the last road house, and walked the final half-mile after the borrowed mare began to favor a foreleg. By the time he came under the gate the day was nearly spent. He was carrying a reeve's letter, not a judgment, and he had been told not to let anyone call it one.

Etta took his name and the road house's mark in the outer receiving book. The rider was irritated by the delay until she showed him the place where an earlier messenger had been entered under the name of the man who had merely paid for his horse.

"If they ask who carried it, that will matter," she said.

"They'll ask what it says."

"They always ask that first."

She sent for Ysabet. The letter bore the Dols bridge-court seal, a date three days after Jalen's arrival there, and the names of the reeve's clerk and the living man who had carried it. Its covering leaf recorded two enclosures: a witnessed account of the hearing and a physician's written observations. Etta entered both before Ysabet broke the seal in the receiving room. The rider took his pay and a stable chit, not a place at the council table.

The account named Jalen Orris. It said that the person wearing Corven Sareth's face had disclaimed Corven's name, commission and marriage, and had described a memory of dying at a gate. Master Isera's observations described dark markings on a breathing man's back that resembled those sometimes seen after death. She did not certify death, murder or any manner of sorcery. The reeve requested the original physician's observations made at Edselsto, the persons who had custody of the body after the lane attack, and an answer about the authority under which the supposed recovery had been announced.

Ysabet read the request twice. There was no drawing in the packet: the physician's originals remained in Dols. There was no village-watch statement, and no word from Leora Sareth. Those were other roads.

Luceran was in the receiving yard when Ysabet found him. A carpenter was arguing with Tomas about the remaining guttering, and the king had been listening because the rain was running off the new roof onto the very drain they meant to protect. He read the Dols letter beside a pile of paid timber.

"Is this a charge?" he asked.

"It is a request for an account."

"Then we shall give one."

Ysabet folded the letter without handing it back. "Which account, Majesty?"

He looked toward the six sacks still waiting under the unfinished roof. Three days ago he had been able to name a delivery that no enemy could dispute. The grain remained delivered. The question before him had changed.

---

Damaris came to the open council with a roof tally, two carriage receipts and a very poor opinion of the weather.

She knew that Dols had declined to recognize the apparent envoy's authority and that a physician had found something requiring another examination. She had not been told of Luceran's private instruction or the mortuary occupation. Her objection to the first proposed reply was therefore entirely practical.

"You have written that the northern road is secured," she told Aldren. "It isn't."

"The king has secured a delivery."

"One delivery. Six sacks. There are six more here. The wall at Dols is not accepted, and the second wagon has no leave to cross."

Aldren drew his pen through the sentence. He was a competent copyist and had grown tired of being asked to make one fact look like another by moving a word.

Rhyse sat at the far end of the table. His guards had carried Jalen to Dols under orders to protect him, not to compel his testimony. He had received no fresh account from the escort and would not pretend that the Dols hearing told him what his men had seen afterward.

"What is asked of us?" he said.

Ysabet read the requests in order: original medical observations, names of body custodians, and the Crown's explanation for announcing a recovery before an independent witness could establish identity. The last was not the same as a demand to recognize Jalen as a foreign officer.

Aldren offered a narrow answer. The Crown could acknowledge that the visitor had been presented too confidently, withdraw any outstanding claim that he held Corven's commission, and allow the three river benches to examine their own warrants.

"That would cost us credit," Luceran said.

"We have already lost the second Merehaven advance," Damaris replied. "The first surety still stands because it was written against grain and a witnessed receipt. If you tell the factor something untrue about the grain, we may lose that too."

"I have no intention of lying about the grain."

She heard the emphasis and frowned. She had known him long enough to recognize when an answer was narrower than a question. But she did not know which other fact he was protecting, and she would not invent one to fill the silence.

Savelin's latest road account lay beside the map. The mason had found soft earth beneath the second course of the miller's wall. Another footing stone would be needed, and the miller would inspect it before any further daylight civilian crossing. The watch captain could still stop a wagon for unsafe water. No military countersign existed. At Teth, Calven still controlled her ferry. Nothing in the Dols physician's note changed those conditions.

"Write the roads as they are," Damaris said. "Whatever else you decide, don't make the next carter pay for a sentence you wanted to hear."

Luceran ordered the financial and road reply prepared separately. Damaris left to examine the guttering charge with Tomas. She had given sound advice about the facts she possessed. She did not know what was about to be decided behind the closed door.

---

Ysabet waited until the last clerk had gone before she placed a small sealed packet on the table.

She did not open it. Luceran knew the packet by the fold of its outer cloth. It held the original private instruction bearing his authority, preserved after Corven's death. Rhyse had read the evidence months before. Miren had heard Luceran confess the killing and occupation in the room from which the listening silver had been removed. None of them required a physician to tell them what had happened to Corven.

"We could answer the question they asked," Ysabet said. "The order, the people who carried it, the mortuary, the difference between Corven's death and Jalen's consent. Dols may condemn us. It would at least be an account."

Luceran turned the physician's description over on the table. "They have not proved he died."

"We are not Dols. We know he died."

Rhyse did not raise his voice. "My guards did not arrange the lane attack. Beren refused the errand. I will not have a road servant named in place of the person who commanded it."

"No one has suggested Beren."

"Someone will. There is always a smaller man available when a king needs the sentence to stop before it reaches him."

Miren had stood at the window while they spoke. The light fell across the table but not the packet. She had come because Luceran asked her to hear his choice, not because she had agreed to share responsibility for it.

"What would confession do to the men in the north?" he asked her.

"It might lose the next delivery."

"And to the people whose wages I have just paid?"

"It will not take the coins out of their hands."

"You know what I mean."

"Yes," she said. "You mean that a true thing may make it harder to do other true things."

Ysabet proposed a second course. The Crown could state that it could not presently substantiate the recovered envoy's authority, promise the original medical and custody accounts, and reserve any answer about the attack until named witnesses were heard. That would not be a confession. It would also not be a false denial.

"Every court from here to Merehaven will read the reservation as an admission," Luceran said.

"Some will," Ysabet replied. "Some will read it as restraint. We cannot decide what they believe by decree."

He had once thought her caution an obstacle to rule. Lately he had begun to understand that she could distinguish the king's authority from the truth of a document he signed. It made her useful and increasingly intolerable.

"Suppose I say no officer acting on my command arranged the attack," he said.

Rhyse's hands went flat against the table. "Then you would say what is false."

"It was not an order given in the public register."

"That does not make it not an order."

Luceran looked at Miren. She did not look away.

"Do you want me to tell you it would be true?" she asked.

"I want you to understand why I must keep the realm together."

"I understand the fear. I will not lend you my name for the answer."

She left without asking him to follow. The door shut with the ordinary sound of a latch. The room was not deaf; the king had chosen the council chamber, and no one had removed its listening silver. He wondered whether Edselsto had heard. He did not ask.

---

At Leora's house, the answer about the crooked peg had become more difficult to put away than the unanswered question.

Her mother had once called the peg the Archbishop too. It had held a wet cloak on the first winter evening Corven came home after a quarrel with a temple official. He had invented the title to make Leora laugh while the water dripped onto the floor. For eighteen years he had used it whenever one of them hung a cloak badly. It had not been a password. That was why the stranger's inability to remember it hurt.

Leora set the witnessed refusal beside the older letters and did not touch either for a while. Her niece was in the next room making a harness for the ribbon horse. Her mother had slept badly and would need her broth warmed again. Those duties were not inconveniences arranged around a diplomatic scandal. They were the substance of the household Corven had shared with her.

Her steward brought the charge for a representative's journey to the village watch. He had found a man willing to inspect records and make an inventory, but the journey would cost more than the steward liked because the road had been damaged by rain.

"Send him," Leora said.

"He may find nothing beyond what the clerk has copied."

"Then I shall know the copies were honestly made."

The steward asked whether the representative should demand to see the body.

"No. He is to see the watch's originals, the dates, and the people who made the entries. He is not to frighten a wounded man into remembering what he did not see."

She wrote to the representative in her own hand. He was to speak to the widow about the unused room and the costs still unpaid, to identify the two attendants separately, and to ask for the first physician's words rather than a later summary. He was not authorized to accuse the king or pronounce Corven dead. Leora did not know of Isera's examination at Dols, nor of the reeve's letters on other roads.

Her niece appeared with the ribbon horse and asked whether the Archbishop would be able to hold a harness.

Leora laughed once, painfully. "He held wet cloaks. He was never good for anything else."

The girl accepted that and went to find another peg.

Leora sealed the instructions while her housekeeper witnessed the date. The steward entered the travel coin and the original letters stayed locked in their own chest. If there was a truth to be found, it would have to survive being carried by people who had never heard the family joke.

---

At Dols, Jalen watched two men argue over the price of a room he had not asked to occupy.

The reeve had placed his lodging on the bridge court's hearing account. The innkeeper thought that entitled him to a better room than the one Jalen had chosen. The watch captain thought a better room would be easier to guard. Jalen preferred the lower chamber because he could reach it without twisting Corven's knee on the stairs.

"Am I in danger?" he asked.

"We do not know," the captain said.

"Then I will take the room I can walk to."

Yara had stayed with the warrants. She would not permit the reeve to treat her presence as an endorsement of Jalen's authority, nor permit the Crown escort to treat the hearing as a surrender of the man in its care. She had sent her own dated report toward the river benches, separately from the reeve's notices. It had not yet reached any of them.

The second physician had been delayed by a washed-out road. Isera did not ask Jalen to stay beyond the two nights he had promised without making a fresh request. She showed him the drawings again and explained what she could compare when the other physician arrived. The marks had not supplied an answer merely because they had been described twice.

"Will you tell him what you think?" Jalen asked.

"I will give him the observations. He must make his own."

"That seems a hard way to be certain."

"It is an honest way to discover that we are not."

The watch captain received the village-watch packet late that afternoon. The outer leaf named its carrier, the original clerk and the dates of the enclosed copies. The original missing-person book and broken stick remained at the village. The captain signed for the copies and did not send them straight into Isera's examination room; the physician's findings and the watch testimony were separate sources. The reeve ordered the packet kept for a witnessed comparison with the originals if Leora's representative later arrived.

Jalen asked whether the packet mentioned his sister. It did not. He knew the question was foolish before he asked, yet he could not stop wanting an answer to come in with every rider.

"Then find the original casualty account," he told the Crown escort's clerk. "I don't need anyone to say it has probably been paid."

Yara heard him. She had once regarded him chiefly as the living complication inside a dead envoy's face. He was becoming harder to think of as an instrument. He had needs that would have existed if Corven had never held a commission.

Outside, the miller's mason was still searching for footing stone. The second civilian grain wagon had not crossed. The reeve had received evidence; he had not surrendered his bridge.

---

Aldren copied the royal answer three times and kept the discarded fourth sheet beneath a weight.

The first sheet had been careful. It withdrew any claim that Jalen could exercise Corven's commission and promised that the Dols reeve would receive the Crown physician's original notes by a named courier. It admitted that the announcement of recovery had exceeded what the physician could attest. Luceran had accepted those sentences.

The second sheet had tried to say that the Crown possessed no knowledge of a royal attack. Ysabet struck out *no knowledge* because the king had heard the report of the attack and knew much more besides.

The third was worse. It named no commander, but said the attack had occurred beyond the king's direction. Rhyse refused to let it pass as a description of his watch's work.

On the fourth sheet Luceran wrote the sentence himself: *No officer of Our Crown was instructed by Us to bring about the attack upon Corven Sareth.*

He did not believe it. Ysabet did not believe it. Rhyse knew that the signed private instruction existed. The words were chosen because they sounded like the answer a foreign court had requested and because their falsity was not yet visible to that court.

"I will enter the exact words and the seal," Aldren said. "I cannot witness the event they describe."

"You are asked to copy," Luceran said.

"Then my name will attest the copying."

Ysabet ordered the discarded drafts preserved as drafts under the restricted register, without altering the original lethal instruction. She would not countersign the denial as a true account. Rhyse placed a separate guard order beside it: no witness from the lane attack, the mortuary or the escort was to be detained, privately questioned or deprived of wages for speaking to the Dols hearing. His officers would record any royal supersession by name and hour. Luceran read it and allowed it to stand. He could not make his false answer true by punishing those who might contradict it, and he was not yet willing to add that crime.

In the outer chamber Damaris received the separate, accurate road and payment reply. Six sacks had reached the northern posts. Six remained at Edselsto. The Dols wall was unaccepted, the guttering unfinished, Merehaven's second advance withheld, and the eighteen patrol mounts still unavailable. She approved the account's arithmetic and said nothing about the sealed denial, which she had not been shown.

The royal reply went into a Dols pouch with the Crown physician's observations copied under witnessed custody; the physician retained his original, and the messenger carried an attested copy with an offer to produce the source. Two narrower letters were prepared for Merehaven and Teth, withdrawing reliance on any new recommendation in Corven's name without claiming that either bench had accepted the Dols hearing. They traveled by different paid riders. The king's false sentence was addressed to Dols, where the question had been put. None of the three replies had arrived.

Aldren wrote the departure hours into the register. He had copied a king's lie with exact fidelity. That was not the same as having invented it.

---

Miren found Luceran in the gallery after the last messenger left.

He had been watching the road long after the riders disappeared below the trees. The guttering above the receiving yard rattled where it had not yet been fixed. He knew the sound now; it would have pleased him once to be able to name a problem and send for the man who could mend it.

"You did not ask me to sign," she said.

"I knew you would refuse."

"That is not the same as not wanting me to."

He turned. She was close enough that he could have reached for her hand. She had not forbidden him the gesture. She had also not offered it.

"Rhyse will keep his witnesses," Luceran said. "Ysabet will keep the order. I have allowed both."

"You have allowed the evidence to survive while telling Dols it cannot be true."

"I have bought time."

"With whose name?"

He could not say Corven's without hearing the answer. He could not say Jalen's without remembering the man asking after his sister. He thought of the roof workers, the grain, the soldiers at the northern posts and the foreign credit that might vanish before another sack could move. All of those things mattered. None of them had put the pen in his hand.

"Do you still love me?" he asked.

Miren looked tired rather than surprised. "I have not stopped. I have also not changed my answer about what you did."

He had wanted love to be a place where his decisions could rest. It had become a place where they were examined by someone who knew him too well to accept the easy version.

"I will not come to supper," she said. "And I will not be your witness if Dols asks whether I believed the letter."

She passed into the physically deaf room and closed the door. He remained in the gallery. Somewhere within the connected stone Edselsto could have answered a question about the old foundations or a measured arch. It could not tell him that Miren had consented to forgive him. He did not ask it to try.

Far away, Jalen waited by choice for another physician. Leora's representative was on the road toward the village watch. Dols held its own examination and the watch's separate copies. Merehaven still held the first surety and its withheld second advance. Teth's ferry remained Calven's. The Crown's answer was riding toward the one court that had asked for it, with a seal, a date and a sentence that could be compared to another document.

In the family nave, all seven candles burned.
