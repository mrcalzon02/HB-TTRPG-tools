## Candidate A — The Hour That Wasn't Saved
**CANDIDATE ONLY.** A labor-and-proof drama in which a true remembered detail becomes a false accounting fact.

The episode could open with a small, plausible error. The junior clerk who watched Edselsto recover Pell's wooden gauge has copied “an hour saved” into a preliminary receiving-yard estimate. At the next desk, someone has shortened it to “one paid hour saved.” No disbursement has been authorized, but a worker who is still owed roof wages sees the phrase and asks whether the Crown plans to deduct that hour from his claim. The clerk is not corrupt; he wants to keep the books tidy and has learned that the court rewards fast answers. His mistake travels farther than he does.

Pell brings the actual gauge, with its familiar knife nick, to the wet model of the drain. It did spare him the trouble of measuring an old angle again. It did not fit a new lower stone, set a course, stop the leak, or pay the men who worked beneath the roof. A fresh measurement and an old recollection are different kinds of evidence. Damaris shows the worker the signed wage roll, separating paid hours, outstanding wages, and an estimate that was never a voucher. Ysabet requires a correction that preserves the original entry rather than making the error disappear. A claimant may bring a witness to read the change.

Luceran is tempted to ask Edselsto which clerk made the second entry. Ysabet reminds him that the castle heard some words in connected rooms; it did not watch a hand write in an unconnected office, authenticate a voucher, or inspect the current gutter. A castle recollection might direct a human search, but cannot replace a paper's custody. The question matters because the king has already begun asking the ceiling before asking the people responsible. This time the delay is not simply an inconvenience. Someone's wage is on the line.

Hesta Vale has her own reason to appear. Her third block remains hers, conditionally suitable for the lower course after the seam was cut back. She is holding it without storage charge for two market days, not indefinitely, and it is not an accepted or delivered load under the existing first refusal. Other customers can use good stone. She does not threaten the king or suddenly offer charity. She asks whether the Crown will place a funded order before the hold expires or allow her to sell it. The corrected gauge record cannot answer that question.

The strongest scene could take place around a table with three things on it: the gauge, a wage roll and Hesta's quarry tally. Each is real. None authorizes the other. Luceran can direct the lawful purchasing officers to examine what can be afforded, but this candidate should end before a purchase, payment, hold extension or appropriation. The unresolved gutter and unpaid workers give the correction consequence. The king learns that an accurate sentence can become a lie when placed under the wrong heading.

A final quiet beat would show Pell's apprentice finding the gauge on its labeled hook without asking Edselsto. This modest human improvement is more durable than the royal excitement over a voice that remembers. Luceran may enter another question in his inquiry book, but the next line should require the person who knows the present state of the work. A successful procedure does not settle the fiscal crisis; it simply stops a convenient falsehood from becoming policy.

## Candidate B — The People Not on the List
**CANDIDATE ONLY.** A court-and-privacy drama in which overheard interest threatens to become counterfeit consent.

Sera Sorelle's musicians need an answer about work. The hall board lists possible dates, equal and unassigned. Her partial awning-rope assessment payment has not purchased precedence, and the remaining sponsor balance and worker claim still exist. A helpful courtier suggests that Luceran ask Edselsto who has expressed interest in a winter performance. With a list of likely guests, the musicians might plan rehearsals, the sponsors might judge costs, and the king might enjoy the appearance of a lively court again. The suggestion is plausible precisely because the castle has just found a missing gauge.

Sera knows how quickly a hopeful list turns into a false promise. She asks who will pay the musicians to keep an evening free, heat the room, mend the awning and replace work they refuse elsewhere. Senn Daro, now confined to public copying after attempting to sell a preliminary treasury inventory, knows the danger of an official-looking draft. He proposes headings: “heard,” “interested,” “invited,” “booked.” The courtier wants the third without doing the work required for the fourth. Senn's experience gives him a useful warning, not a sudden pardon or restoration of sealed access.

The proposal's public confrontation occurs at the notice board. A musician has seen a draft attendance sheet and shifted another engagement, while another refuses to reserve time without payment. Neither is bound by the draft; no event has been authorized. Sera asks Ysabet to post the correction beside the original notice so that a rumor does not outrun the truth. The scene is about actual people losing choices when someone else writes their intentions down. Edselsto can repeat some words spoken within marked connected galleries, but it cannot distinguish admiration for music from consent to attend, nor a wish from an appointment.

Miren must remain a person with her own hours, not a guest-list puzzle. She is still unreconciled with Luceran over his retrieval of private speech, and the south-gallery withdrawing room remains physically deaf because its silver junction was cut. A page may bring her an ordinary question about a possible evening. She can say that she will decide when there is an actual date and reason to attend. Her affection does not imply forgiveness, marriage consent, restored intimacy, or attendance. Luceran's real challenge is to refrain from converting the silence of someone close to him into an answer supplied by stone.

The outside world offers a useful limit without staging a new adventure. Maera's daylight village path from the cart turn to the fallen beech remains conditional, nonexclusive and unused. A plausible ending leaves the board honest and the room unbooked. Sera can tell her musicians there is still no performance to accept. Miren can continue mending a sleeve without being questioned by a listening wall. Luceran may choose not to ask Edselsto for names, but the choice is costly: he cannot claim the warmth of a full hall without paying for it and asking those who would fill it. Restraint is not a new magical limit on the castle. It is a decision he must keep making.

### The Hour That Wasn't Saved — sample scene

The word *saved* had traveled farther through the court's desks than the wooden gauge had through the measuring room.

Tomas had entered the modest saving in the receiving-yard work book after Pell checked the recovered pattern against the old turn. His entry named the work: a measuring hour spared by finding and verifying the old angle. It did not describe an hour removed from anyone's completed shift. The new lower-course stone still needed measuring, buying and setting.

Oris, the junior copyist, had carried that entry into a clean works summary. In his hand, *measuring hour spared at the old turn* became *one hour saved*. The first shortening seemed harmless. Then he prepared a third sheet for Damaris's review beside the wage chest, where works estimates and unpaid roof claims waited in separate bundles. He wrote *one paid hour saved* beneath the wages heading, reasoning that a saved hour must be an hour the Crown need not pay for.

He had copied the words himself and pinned the unsigned review sheet where the roof men could see it. He had not entered a deduction in the signed wage roll, drawn a voucher, or been told to do either. But a sheet displayed beside the pay chest did not announce its lack of authority nearly as loudly as its heading announced a saving.

Rell, who had held the lower ladder while rain ran through the gutter joint, stood before it with his cap in his hands. His thumb was bound in a strip of clean cloth.

"Is that mine?" he asked.

Oris looked up from his ruled paper. "Is what yours?"

"The paid hour. I worked the full shift. Some of that shift is still owed to me. Is the Crown saying it need not pay for one of those hours?"

"No one has taken anything," Oris said.

"Then show me the roll. If the roll says I worked it and this says you saved paying it, which paper will the chest obey?"

Oris reached toward the pin. He had meant to make the summary easier to read, not to decide a roofer's wages. But the error was no longer confined to his desk. Someone who had no authority to change a debt had made it appear changeable.

Ysabet arrived before he could take the sheet down. She read the posted words, then asked Oris to bring both the receiving-yard entry and his intermediate works copy. He produced them without being sent away. Each shortening was visible beside the next.

"Leave the notice where Rell found it until the challenge is recorded," she said. "Mark it disputed, not settled. We shall correct the public copy where the men can see the reason."

"So everyone may see the mistake?" Rell asked.

"So no one can tell you afterward that it was never there."

Rell asked for a witness of his own, not merely a court reader. Another roofer had been waiting in the doorway to learn whether his arrears were entered. He came inside, wiped his hands, and stood beside Rell. Ysabet told both men they could challenge the comparison without signing away any claim. Oris fetched the three papers while Damaris was sent for the actual signed wage roll.

Pell Falbrecht entered carrying the curved gauge, the old knife nick still visible near its thin end. He placed it by Tomas's entry, well away from the wage papers.

"This is what was found," he said. "I checked it at the old receiving-yard turn. It spared a fresh measurement there. It did not fit a new lower stone, finish the joint or work a man's shift."

Oris looked from the gauge to the first book. "Tomas wrote an hour."

"Of measuring," Pell answered. "Not an hour of Rell's labor that may be struck off. If the timber moves or the new block arrives, we measure what is there then. A pattern remembers an angle; it cannot certify the next stone."

Rell touched the bandage on his thumb. "Then the roof still leaks."

"Yes."

"And the work I did?"

Damaris arrived with the signed wage roll under her own custody. She opened it to Rell's name, set his dates and hours beside the earlier partial disbursement, and showed the unpaid balance without letting Oris's sheet lie across either column. Rell's witness followed the lines with a blunt fingernail.

"Read the entry back to me," Rell said. "The whole of it. I want to hear whether any hour has been crossed out."

Damaris read the worked hours, the amount already paid and the amount still due. No hour had been deducted. She turned the roll toward him again so that his witness could compare it with what she had said.

"That is the signed claim controlling this chest," she said. "The receiving-yard entry records work avoided on a different task. Oris's posted review sheet is neither a signed alteration to this roll nor an authorized payment voucher. Even if someone had signed his summary, a signature would not turn measuring time into wages already discharged."

Rell did not thank her for a debt still unpaid. "Then I challenge the notice, not the hours in this roll. And I want it written that I have not accepted payment."

"That is exactly what will be written," Damaris said.

Oris looked miserable. "I thought I was making the summary clearer."

"You made it shorter," Ysabet said. "Those are not always the same thing."

Ysabet dictated the correction in Oris's presence, and made him write it in his own hand. It named Tomas's checked measuring entry as the source, identified Oris's first shortening and the later *paid hour* addition as his copies, and stated that neither changed the signed wage roll. The original posted words remained legible beneath the correction. Oris dated and signed his admission as the copyist responsible. Rell's chosen witness marked that he had heard the comparison and correction read aloud; the mark was explicitly **not** a receipt for wages or consent to a deduction. Damaris kept custody of the signed roll, while the disputed sheet and correction were entered together for review.

Luceran came in as Oris finished.

The king saw the gauge first, and then the gathered men. "Has something happened to the drain?"

"To the account of it," Damaris said.

He listened while Rell described the sheet. Luceran remembered the pleasure of hearing Edselsto direct Pell to the old shelf. He also remembered how quickly he had asked what else the house could save.

"Edselsto may recall who spoke of an hour," he said.

Ysabet did not look toward the ceiling. "We have Tomas's checked work entry, Oris's two copies and a witness to the posted sheet. The copying desk is not a place where the castle has been certified to hear every word, much less see a pen move. What would its answer prove?"

Luceran looked at Oris. The young man had admitted his error before being accused of theft or deceit. The papers told the story without a supernatural witness.

"Nothing more about the wages," Luceran said. "I was going to ask the wrong question."

"Then ask the right one," Rell said, surprising himself as much as the king. "When may we be paid?"

Damaris answered before Luceran could promise anything. The outstanding sum was recorded and due; a further disbursement required money that was actually available for works, with the existing claims kept in order. She would not name a day the treasury could not yet support.

A voice at the door said, "And a new stone requires a funded order."

Hesta Vale had brought her quarry tally, folded into a dry leather cover. She had not come to offer the block again. She had come to make sure no one mistook a conditional hold for delivery.

She opened the tally beside Pell's gauge and Rell's wage roll. Three objects lay in a row, each honest in its own way.

"The third block is at my quarry," she said. "Your people inspected it after the seam was cut back. It may serve the lower course. It is still my stone. The two-market-day hold was offered without storage charge, not without an end."

Pell nodded. "I have not accepted it for an upper face, and I cannot set it here until it is ordered and carried."

Oris reached for a fresh sheet. "Should I write that the Crown has secured it?"

"No," Hesta and Damaris said together.

Hesta allowed herself a small smile. "Write that I am holding it on the stated terms. If another buyer comes after the hold, I will be entitled to speak to them. A remembered angle does not pay my quarrymen."

Luceran asked Damaris whether the east-gate figures could help. She turned the signed tally so that he could see Bessa's custody mark and the Jorvan observer's separate witness mark. The twenty-four-mark advance and twenty-seven-mark repayment cap remained attached to that net under the agreed market-day terms. A signed figure was not free money because the castle could repeat it.

"We can examine unpledged works money and the price of the actual order," Damaris said. "We cannot spend a pledge twice."

Luceran gave no purchasing order. Hesta received no extension. Rell received no new payment. But Oris's false line would no longer be copied into the next summary as if it were a decision.

When the men left, Pell's apprentice came for the gauge. He did not ask Edselsto where it belonged. He showed Oris the nick in its end and the hook he had labeled in the measuring room, so the next worker would know which pattern had actually been checked.

Oris took down the corrected summary only after the disputed words, the correction and Rell's witness mark had been copied together into the review book. He left a legible correction beside the wage chest, where the first sheet had done its harm. The words *paid hour saved* remained visible, crossed through rather than erased; anyone reading them could now see who had written them and why they carried no authority over the roll.

Luceran watched the ink dry. A clerk's signature had made the error traceable, not the wages payable. The roof still needed work, and Rell still had a claim that could be counted without being paid.

In the nave beyond the connected passage, the first ancestral wick remained dark and the other six candles burned. No one had asked the castle to explain them. Here, at least, the smaller mystery had an author who could sign his own mistake.

### The People Not on the List — sample scene

By the time Sera Sorelle reached the hall board, a musician had already given away an afternoon she might not recover.

The winter dates still stood in the steward's careful hand: three possibilities, equal and unassigned. No hall had been reserved. No player had been engaged. Beneath that notice, however, someone had pinned a handsome new sheet with two columns: *Likely guests* and *Performers expected*. Sera's name headed the second.

Mara, the young fiddler beside her, carried a folded message from the baker. He had asked her to play at his daughter's supper. She had told him to seek another player until the court decided whether it wanted her.

"Did he find one?" Sera asked.

"I don't know. His boy came back to say he could not hold the place."

"Then you have lost a possible paid evening to a possible unpaid one."

Mara looked at the notice. "It says expected."

"That is the trouble."

The courtier who had written the sheet stepped forward. He had gathered expressions of interest from conversations in the connected galleries. He wanted to show Luceran that the court was ready for winter music. He had asked no musician's price and had received no guest's acceptance.

"I intended to help," he said. "If people see their names, they may begin to plan."

"They have begun," Mara answered. "I did."

Sera did not tell her the baker would take her back. Nor could she offer to replace the lost fee from her own purse. The partial payment she had made against the awning-rope assessment had not discharged the remaining sponsor balance or the separate worker claim. Her musicians knew it; the household knew it. She had come seeking an actual date so that she could ask what work they might accept, not to spend their time twice.

"Who pays for rehearsal?" she asked the courtier. "Who pays for the journey, the strings, the hour she cannot sell elsewhere? You have written 'expected' where a payer's name should be."

The courtier touched the pin as though removing the paper would remove the consequence.

Senn Daro, carrying public copying forms from the open desk, stopped him. Senn was not permitted the sealed treasury papers; that access had been taken after his attempt to sell a preliminary inventory. He could copy public notices and recognize their dangers, but he could not make this sheet official by handling it.

"Leave it until the correction is witnessed," he said. "Otherwise the people who saw it will have nothing to show when someone says they imagined it."

The courtier looked at him with open irritation. "You are advising me about the proper use of a list?"

"About the improper use of a heading," Senn said. "I learned the distinction at a cost."

Ysabet arrived with the watch clerk. She asked the courtier to read his own columns aloud, then asked three questions. Had any invitation been sent? No. Had any guest accepted? No. Had a household officer booked the hall or offered wages? No.

"Then the sheet records neither attendance nor employment," she said. "And it has no authority to sit under the hall notice as though it does."

Mara asked whether her name could be struck out first. Sera asked that the whole error be corrected in public, not merely the one name. Other players had seen the list. One had set aside a morning for practice; another had refused to promise any time without a fee. Those choices were different, and neither became a contract because the courtier preferred one of them.

Senn drew a line down a clean page and wrote five words at its top: *heard; proposed; invited; accepted; paid*. Under each he left a blank space. The courtier protested that the first column ought to contain the names he remembered.

"No," Ysabet said. "We can say you heard expressions of interest in the connected galleries. We cannot certify every speaker, and we will not publish their names to repair an error made by publishing names."

Senn wrote the correction beneath the empty headings. The added sheet had been prepared without booking, invitation, acceptance, or authority to engage performers. Its names were not a guest roll. Its appearance had caused at least one musician to defer another offer; the amount of any lost work remained unverified. The original notice of possible dates still governed. The courtier signed as maker of the mistaken sheet, and Ysabet witnessed the correction, not the truth of his guesses. Senn signed only for the public copy he had made.

Mara read it while the ink was wet.

"Will the baker believe this?"

"He may," Sera said. "He may already have another fiddler. You are entitled to ask him, and to be angry with us if he has."

Ysabet had both papers left visible until the people who relied on the first could see the second. She did not promise compensation or order a performance to make the embarrassment disappear.

Luceran came through the connected gallery after the watch clerk had finished his account. He read the mistaken headings, then the correction. The courtier tried to explain that Edselsto could remember the people who had praised the music.

"Perhaps it could," Luceran said. "In the passages where it can hear."

"It might even remember Lady Miren saying she enjoyed the songs," the courtier added.

Sera watched the king's expression change. The south-gallery withdrawing room was physically deaf: its silver junction had been cut, the removed piece melted into stock. Miren had chosen that room after Luceran had retrieved words she had not given him. The courtier's suggestion would make a public invitation out of an alleged private feeling, even if the castle had once heard some praise elsewhere.

A page had carried the ordinary hall notice to Miren's door. He had knocked and handed it over when she answered. He had not requested an answer, and she had given none. His testimony ended at delivery.

Luceran looked at the page. "Did you ask her to attend?"

"No, Your Grace."

"Then you have no answer to report."

He did not ask Edselsto what Miren had said, or send the page back to demand a decision.

In the disconnected room, Miren set the notice beside the sleeve she was mending. She could hear the ordinary feet in the passage, but no silver way carried her answer to the king. She read the three possible dates and the absence of any invitation. She had work for the sickroom that week, and a day of her own she did not yet intend to assign. She folded the paper without writing a reply. Her silence was a choice to wait, not consent waiting to be discovered.

At the board, Luceran told the proper officers to examine whether a funded evening could be proposed. Damaris would have to count heat, watch, cleaning, the usable rooms and actual musicians' wages before any date could be offered. That instruction booked nothing.

Sera gathered her players. She would take them the correction and the truth: there was still no work to accept. Mara went to the baker's house to ask whether the lost offer could be recovered. No one at court knew what answer she would receive.

Senn pinned the corrected notice at eye level. Its empty columns did not look festive, but no one could mistake them for a paid company.

Luceran stood before the board a moment longer. He had wanted the hall full of people who were pleased to be there. He could not make it so by remembering what they had once enjoyed.

Outside the gallery, six ancestral candles burned while the first remained dark. Their light offered no opinion on the names that were no longer listed.
