## Candidate A — The Hour That Wasn't Saved
**CANDIDATE ONLY.** A labor-and-proof drama in which a true remembered detail becomes a false accounting fact.

The episode could open with a small, plausible error. The junior clerk who watched Edselsto recover Pell's wooden gauge has copied “an hour saved” into a preliminary receiving-yard estimate. At the next desk, someone has shortened it to “one paid hour saved.” No disbursement has been authorized, but a worker who is still owed roof wages sees the phrase and asks whether the Crown plans to deduct that hour from his claim. The clerk is not corrupt; he wants to keep the books tidy and has learned that the court rewards fast answers. His mistake travels farther than he does.

Pell brings the actual gauge, with its familiar knife nick, to the wet model of the drain. It did spare him the trouble of measuring an old angle again. It did not fit a new lower stone, set a course, stop the leak, or pay the men who worked beneath the roof. A fresh measurement and an old recollection are different kinds of evidence. Damaris shows the worker the signed wage roll, separating paid hours, outstanding wages, and an estimate that was never a voucher. Ysabet requires a correction that preserves the original entry rather than making the error disappear. A claimant may bring a witness to read the change.

Luceran is tempted to ask Edselsto which clerk made the second entry. Ysabet reminds him that the castle heard some words in connected rooms; it did not watch a hand write in an unconnected office, authenticate a voucher, or inspect the current gutter. A castle recollection might direct a human search, but cannot replace a paper's custody. The question matters because the king has already begun asking the ceiling before asking the people responsible. This time the delay is not simply an inconvenience. Someone's wage is on the line.

Hesta Vale has her own reason to appear. Her third block remains hers, conditionally suitable for the lower course after the seam was cut back. She is holding it without storage charge for two market days, not indefinitely, and it is not an accepted or delivered load under the existing first refusal. Other customers can use good stone. She does not threaten the king or suddenly offer charity. She asks whether the Crown will place a funded order before the hold expires or allow her to sell it. The corrected gauge record cannot answer that question.

The strongest scene could take place around a table with three things on it: the gauge, a wage roll and Hesta's quarry tally. Each is real. None authorizes the other. Luceran can direct the lawful purchasing officers to examine what can be afforded, but this candidate should end before a purchase, payment, hold extension or appropriation. The unresolved gutter and unpaid workers give the correction consequence. The king learns that an accurate sentence can become a lie when placed under the wrong heading.

A final quiet beat would show Pell's apprentice finding the gauge on its labeled hook without asking Edselsto. This modest human improvement is more durable than the royal excitement over a voice that remembers. Luceran may enter another question in his inquiry book, but the next line should require the person who knows the present state of the work. A successful procedure does not settle the fiscal crisis; it simply stops a convenient falsehood from becoming policy.

## Candidate B — The People Not on the List
**CANDIDATE ONLY.** A court-and-privacy drama in which overheard interest threatens to become counterfeit consent.

Sera Sorelle's musicians need an answer about work. The hall board lists possible dates, equal and unassigned. Her partial awning-rope assessment payment has not purchased precedence, and the remaining sponsor balance and worker claim still exist. A helpful courtier suggests that Luceran ask Edselsto who has expressed interest in a winter performance. With a list of likely guests, the musicians might plan rehearsals, the sponsors might judge costs, and the king might enjoy the appearance of a lively court again. The suggestion is plausible precisely because the castle has just found a missing gauge.

Sera knows how quickly a hopeful list turns into a false promise. She asks who will pay the musicians to keep an evening free, heat the room, mend the awning and replace work they refuse elsewhere. Senn Daro, now confined to public copying after attempting to sell a preliminary treasury inventory, knows the danger of an official-looking draft. He proposes headings: “heard,” “interested,” “invited,” “booked.” The courtier wants the third without doing the work required for the fourth. Senn's experience gives him a useful warning, not a sudden pardon or restoration of sealed access.

The proposal's public confrontation occurs at the notice board. A musician has seen a draft attendance sheet and shifted another engagement, while another refuses to reserve time without payment. Neither is bound by the draft; no event has been authorized. Sera asks Ysabet to post the correction beside the original notice so that a rumor does not outrun the truth. The scene is about actual people losing choices when someone else writes their intentions down. Edselsto can repeat some words spoken within marked connected galleries, but it cannot distinguish admiration for music from consent to attend, nor a wish from an appointment.

Miren must remain a person with her own hours, not a guest-list puzzle. She is still unreconciled with Luceran over his retrieval of private speech, and the south-gallery withdrawing room remains physically deaf because its silver junction was cut. A page may bring her an ordinary question about a possible evening. She can say that she will decide when there is an actual date and reason to attend. Her affection does not imply forgiveness, marriage consent, restored intimacy, or attendance. Luceran's real challenge is to refrain from converting the silence of someone close to him into an answer supplied by stone.

The outside world offers a useful limit without staging a new adventure. Maera's daylight village path from the cart turn to the fallen beech remains conditional, nonexclusive and unused. The woodcutters' mule and weather still matter. No horse, escort or hunt has been authorized; eighteen patrol mounts remain withdrawn. A messenger can bring ordinary weather news, not a secret report of Miren's intention or a castle-heard account from beyond its conduits. The king is forced to acknowledge that the people outside his walls have no duty to become his audience.

A plausible ending leaves the board honest and the room unbooked. Sera can tell her musicians there is still no performance to accept. Miren can continue mending a sleeve without being questioned by a listening wall. Luceran may choose not to ask Edselsto for names, but the choice is costly: he cannot claim the warmth of a full hall without paying for it and asking those who would fill it. Restraint is not a new magical limit on the castle. It is a decision he must keep making.

### The Hour That Wasn't Saved — sample scene

The word *saved* had traveled farther through Edselsto than the wooden gauge.

By morning it stood in three different hands on three different papers. Tomas's receiving-yard estimate said *one measuring hour possibly spared, subject to checking*. On a clean summary beside the wage chest, the junior copyist Oris had written *one hour saved*. A third sheet, prepared for Damaris's review, had acquired the words *one paid hour saved*.

No one had signed the third sheet. It had nevertheless been pinned where the roof men could read it.

Rell, who had held the lower ladder while rain ran through the gutter joint, stood before it with his cap in his hands. His thumb was bound in a strip of clean cloth.

"Is that mine?" he asked.

Oris looked up from his ruled paper. "Is what yours?"

"The hour you have saved. I worked the full shift. If the king has found a way to take an hour out of it, I would like to know before the chest opens."

Oris turned red. "No one has taken anything."

"Then why is it under wages?"

It was a reasonable question. Oris had put the line there because the wage roll and the estimate lay together on his desk. He had not wanted to lose the pleasing little saving that everyone at court was discussing. Now the two headings made a claim neither paper could support.

Ysabet arrived before he could take the sheet down. She read it, then read Tomas's original.

"Leave it where it is for a moment," she said.

Rell's expression hardened. "So everyone may see it?"

"So you may see exactly what we correct. If I remove it first, you have only my word for what it said."

She asked whether he wanted a reader or witness present. Rell nodded toward the doorway, where another roofer had been waiting to learn whether his own arrears would be entered. The man came inside, wiped his hands and stood beside him. Neither was required to agree with the clerk.

Pell Falbrecht entered carrying the curved gauge, the old knife nick still visible near its thin end. He set it on the table without allowing it to touch the wage roll.

"This is what was found," he said. "It saved me from taking the first angle again. It did not straighten the lower course, and it certainly did not work Rell's shift."

"How much time did it save?" Oris asked.

"Less than I hoped, after I checked it against the stone. I cannot make a paid hour out of that, either way. The new joint still needs measuring when the lower block is here."

Rell examined the little piece of wood. "Then the roof still leaks."

"Yes."

"And my hours?"

Damaris, arriving with the signed roll, laid it open before him. She showed the dates of work, the partial payment already made, and the remaining amount owed. There was no deduction against Rell's line. There was also no money in her hand to settle the balance now.

"This is your claim," she said. "The estimate is a separate record of possible labor saved on work not yet finished. Neither record may silently alter the other."

Rell read slowly. His witness followed each line with a blunt fingernail.

"I will accept that these are the hours written," Rell said. "I have not accepted that they are paid."

"Nor have I written that they are," Damaris answered.

Oris looked miserable. "I thought I was making the summary clearer."

"You made it shorter," Ysabet said. "Those are not always the same thing."

She had him write a correction beneath the offending entry, giving its source, the exact mistaken phrase, and the signed wage roll that controlled the claim. The original words remained legible. Rell's witness marked that he had heard the correction read aloud, not that the Crown's debt was discharged. Oris signed his own name as the maker of the mistaken copy.

Luceran came in as Oris finished.

The king saw the gauge first, and then the gathered men. "Has something happened to the drain?"

"To the account of it," Damaris said.

He listened while Rell described the sheet. Luceran remembered the pleasure of hearing Edselsto direct Pell to the old shelf. He also remembered how quickly he had asked what else the house could save.

"Edselsto may recall who spoke of an hour," he said.

Ysabet did not look toward the ceiling. "We have Tomas's estimate, Oris's copy and a witness to the posted sheet. The copying desk is not a place where the castle has been certified to hear every word, much less see a pen move. What would its answer prove?"

Luceran looked at Oris. The young man had admitted his error before being accused of theft or deceit. The papers told the story without a supernatural witness.

"Nothing more about the wages," Luceran said. "I was going to ask the wrong question."

"Then ask the right one," Rell said, surprising himself as much as the king. "When may we be paid?"

Damaris answered before Luceran could promise anything. The outstanding sum was recorded and due; a further disbursement required money that was actually available for works, with the existing claims kept in order. She would not name a day the treasury could not yet support.

A voice at the door said, "And a new stone requires a funded order."

Hesta Vale had brought her quarry tally, folded into a dry leather cover. She had not come to offer the block again. She had come to make sure no one mistook a conditional hold for delivery.

She opened the tally beside Pell's gauge and Rell's wage roll. Three objects lay in a row, each honest in its own way.

"The third block is at my quarry," she said. "Your people inspected it after the seam was cut back. It may serve the lower course. It is still my stone. The two-market-day hold was offered without storage charge, not without an end."

Pell nodded. "I have not accepted it for an upper face, and I cannot set it here until it is ordered and carried."

Oris reached for a fresh sheet. "Should I write that the Crown has secured it?"

"No," Hesta and Damaris said together.

Hesta allowed herself a small smile. "Write that I am holding it on the stated terms. If another buyer comes after the hold, I will be entitled to speak to them. A remembered angle does not pay my quarrymen."

Luceran asked Damaris whether the east-gate figures could help. She turned the signed tally so that he could see Bessa's custody mark and the Jorvan observer's separate witness mark. The twenty-four-mark advance and twenty-seven-mark repayment cap remained attached to that net under the agreed market-day terms. A signed figure was not free money because the castle could repeat it.

"We can examine unpledged works money and the price of the actual order," Damaris said. "We cannot spend a pledge twice."

Luceran gave no purchasing order. Hesta received no extension. Rell received no new payment. But Oris's false line would no longer be copied into the next summary as if it were a decision.

When the men left, Pell's apprentice came for the gauge. He did not ask Edselsto where it belonged. He showed Oris the nick in its end and the hook he had labeled in the measuring room, so the next worker would know which pattern had actually been checked.

Oris took down the corrected summary only after making the required copy for the review book. The words *paid hour saved* remained visible there, crossed through but not erased.

Luceran watched the ink dry.

In the nave beyond the connected passage, the first ancestral wick remained dark and the other six candles burned. No one had asked the castle to explain them. Here, at least, the smaller mystery had an author who could sign his own mistake.

### The People Not on the List — sample scene

By the time Sera Sorelle reached the hall notice board, her musicians had already begun arguing over a performance that did not exist.

The board had always been plain about its uncertainty. Three winter evenings were marked *possible*, with no room reserved, no company engaged and no preference given to any sponsor. Beneath it hung a second paper, written in a different hand. This one carried a column headed *Likely attendance* and another headed *Performers expected*.

Sera found her own name in the second column.

She read it twice, then looked at the fiddler beside her. The girl had her instrument case under one arm and a baker's note in the other.

"I told the baker I might be occupied," the fiddler said. "He wanted music for his daughter's supper. I said I would answer after the court decided."

"And has the court decided?"

The girl looked at the paper. "I thought it had."

It had not. The second sheet bore neither a date of issue nor a paying officer's signature. A courtier in a velvet collar admitted he had assembled it from remarks heard in the galleries. Several people had admired Sera's musicians after the festival. Others had said the hall would be warmer with songs in it. The courtier had wanted to show the king that the court was ready.

"Ready to do what?" Sera asked.

"To gather. To restore some cheer."

"Then gather people who have agreed to come. My players cannot spend a compliment on supper."

The courtier indicated the names. "There is good reason to believe these people would attend. The castle itself might remember who spoke of it."

Sera held up the fiddler's baker's note. "And if she refuses that job to wait for yours, who pays her for the evening you have not booked?"

He had no answer that included money.

Senn Daro came out of the public copying room carrying a stack of blank forms. He recognized the sheet immediately, although he had not made it. Once he had tried to sell a preliminary treasury inventory as if an unfinished list were worth more than its authority. The punishment had left him with public copying and no access to sealed accounts. He knew the difference between a neat page and a lawful one.

"Who authorized the headings?" he asked.

"I wrote them," the courtier said. "No harm was intended."

Senn set his forms on the sill. "The headings are the harm. 'Likely' will be copied as 'invited.' 'Expected' will be copied as 'engaged.' By tomorrow a man may come to collect a wage for an evening that was never ordered, and a musician may have lost work she could actually have taken."

"You are hardly in a position to lecture me on lists."

"No," Senn said. "I am in a position to know how much a list can cost."

Ysabet arrived when the voices drew the watch clerk from his table. She examined the original board, the added sheet and the fiddler's note. She asked the courtier to say, before the people named, whether he had received any acceptances. He had received none. He had sent no invitations. No household officer had approved a hall date.

"Then this is not a guest list," Ysabet said.

"It is a forecast."

"Then it should not have been posted beneath a public notice without its source and limitations."

The courtier reached to remove it. Ysabet stopped him, not to punish him before the crowd but because the fiddler had already relied upon what she saw. A correction made privately would leave her with no public evidence to show the baker.

Sera asked for one more thing. "Let her speak before you write the correction."

The fiddler's cheeks burned. "I was pleased to be named. I thought I had been chosen. I have not agreed to play, and no one has offered my fee. I would like to know whether I can still take the other work."

"Ask the baker," Sera said. "He is a living man who can give you an answer."

Senn ruled a new sheet with five narrow headings: *heard, proposed, invited, accepted, paid*. He left every name column empty. He wrote beneath them that the added paper was an unauthorized expression of interest, not a hall booking or attendance roll; no musician, guest or sponsor was bound by it. The courtier signed acknowledgment of having made the first sheet. Ysabet witnessed the correction. Sera and the fiddler could read both without being asked to endorse either.

Luceran arrived as Senn pinned the correction beside the original.

"Could Edselsto settle which people expressed interest?" he asked, and heard how easily the question came.

Ysabet answered carefully. "In the marked connected galleries, it may remember some words spoken where its listening ways actually reach. It cannot certify who intended to accept an invitation that was never sent. It cannot turn praise into consent."

The courtier looked relieved at the prospect of another source. "Lady Miren praised the songs, I am told."

Luceran's gaze went toward the south-gallery turn.

There the withdrawing room remained physically deaf. Its silver junction had been cut and the removed piece melted into stock. The room was not a loophole in an otherwise complete guest register. Miren had gone there because she was entitled to speak, or not speak, without a king retrieving her words afterward.

A page had already carried the ordinary hall notice to her door. He had knocked, offered the paper, and departed without asking for a decision. She had not returned an answer. The page could testify to delivering a notice; he could not testify to an intention she had not expressed.

"She has said nothing to me about attending," Luceran said.

"Then her name does not belong on that paper," Sera replied. "Neither does mine."

Luceran did not call on Edselsto. He did not ask a page to seek a private answer from Miren. He told the courtier to leave the correction visible and directed the proper household officers to determine whether a real, funded proposal could be made at all. That was an instruction to examine costs, not an authorization to reserve the hall.

The fiddler folded the baker's note and put it in her case. She would ask him whether the supper work remained open. Sera would not promise that it did. She would not ask the girl to keep a winter evening empty on the strength of an unsigned column.

Senn stood back from the board. Two papers remained: one mistaken, one corrected. The empty columns on the new sheet were more useful than the full ones on the old.

"Will the hall be quiet, then?" Luceran asked.

"Until someone offers a date, a fee and a choice," Sera said. "Yes."

He looked once toward the south gallery and did not send for Miren. The silence there was not evidence against her, nor a promise waiting to be collected.
