## The Letter Without a Seal

Daysha recognized the handwriting before she unfolded the note. Every downstroke looked as if its writer had been gripping a tent peg rather than a pen.

*Are you eating? The old kettle has a new handle. You would say we fixed it badly, and you would be right. We do not know whether we are still allowed to write to you like this.*

She read the last sentence twice.

The dragonborn mercenaries who had raised her had never asked permission to worry about her supper. They had shoved a bowl across the campfire, complained about the state of her boots, and argued over whose turn it was to mend a strap. Now somebody had written *allowed* as carefully as if it were a word in a treaty.

She pulled a fresh sheet toward her.

*You must come here,* she began.

That was easy. She could have a carriage prepared, a guest room cleared, escorts assigned. Her household would interpret the sentence as an instruction; theirs might too. Even if the visitors hated every minute, who would tell a queen they would rather stay home?

Daysha crossed out the sentence so hard the nib tore the paper.

Her next attempt was worse. *I will arrange everything.* It sounded generous until she imagined them packing in a hurry, worrying what offense a refusal might cause.

She pushed both spoiled pages away. The question in their letter was not how to travel to court. It was whether the people who had once shouted for her to wash her hands could still speak to her without kneeling.

This time she wrote more slowly.

*Yes, I am eating. You would still complain that I eat too quickly.*

She smiled and kept going.

*You do not need permission to tell me the kettle leaks, or that I am being foolish. I miss the noise of your table. I would like to sit there again someday, if you want me there. You owe me no visit, and you need not invent an excuse if you do not.*

She paused before the last line.

*Please tell me who made the new handle. I want to know whom to blame.*

At the bottom she wrote only *Daysha*. No crown, office, or instruction to reply.

A court courier could carry a sealed summons quickly. An ordinary personal answer would take more care to send without making it everybody's business. She set aside the prepared official dispatch slip and left her folded reply in her private drawer; she had not yet chosen a safe, private route.

Nothing was dispatched that evening. She did not know when they would read her words, or whether they would believe the invitation could truly be refused.

For once, she had chosen not to decide that for them.
