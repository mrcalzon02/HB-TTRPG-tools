## The Unfinished Column

The first minister to notice that the king and queen had barely slept was wise enough not to mention it.

The second was not so wise. He stared at Rome's untouched tea, then at the dark circles beneath Daysha's violet eyes, and asked whether the royal household had suffered some emergency during the night.

“No,” said Rome, a fraction too quickly.

“Not an emergency,” Daysha agreed. Her long sapphire-blue hair had been dressed immaculately beneath her crown. Nothing could be done about the rather less immaculate state of her concentration.

The minister looked from one to the other. “Then I am relieved.”

He did not look relieved.

Before them lay the morning budget papers. One column proposed replacing the decorative lantern fittings in the western reception passage; another recorded a troublesome leak above a household storeroom. Both were recommendations, neither an authorized expenditure. The accompanying estimate treated the lanterns as ordinary improvements and the roof as an inconvenient addition.

Rome tried to follow a line of figures. He reached the end, blinked, and began again.

Daysha placed a finger on the roof entry. “This estimate says the work can be done without interrupting the household.”

“That was the assumption, Your Majesty,” said the minister.

“Whose assumption?”

He examined his notes. “Mine, I am afraid. I expected the stores attendants could move the shelves after their usual duties.”

“Then the cost has been moved out of your column,” Daysha said, “and into someone else's evening.”

The minister's ears reddened. He could have defended his calculation, or quietly amended it after the meeting. Instead he took the sheet back and drew a line beneath the unfinished total.

“I have counted the stone and timber,” he admitted. “Not the hands or the hours. The comparison is not fit for a decision.”

Rome looked up, grateful for a question he could answer without performing arithmetic. “Can the leak be made safe while you obtain a proper estimate?”

“The household steward must determine that, Sire. I haven't inspected it.”

“Then ask. And bring us a version that accounts for the work, rather than pretending it happens for free.”

The minister wrote the instruction in his own hand. The clerk beside him struck the apparent savings from the summary and marked the two proposals **for revised comparison**. The ornamental lantern recommendation remained undecided; the roof had gained no fictitious allocation. A misleading choice, at least, would no longer arrive disguised as a tidy number.

There was a brief silence.

“May I ask,” the minister began, “whether either of Your Majesties requires the physician?”

Daysha's smile was exquisitely unhelpful. “Only if physicians have begun prescribing quieter council chambers.”

Rome covered a yawn with the budget folder.

The minister, who could make sense of a doubtful total but not of this particular mystery, resolved to ask no more questions until the revised figures were ready.
