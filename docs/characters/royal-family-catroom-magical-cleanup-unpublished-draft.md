The catroom was the only chamber in the palace where King Rome had learned to knock before entering.

The first time he had opened its door without warning, a long-furred cat had shot past his boots and hidden beneath a table that required three attendants and a good deal of royal embarrassment to move. The second time, he had disturbed a sleeping animal on the window seat and received a look so offended that he had apologized aloud. By the third, he understood the custom. A king might summon ministers, open councils, and ask for the keys to his own treasury. He could not reasonably expect two Maine Coons to rise because he had arrived.

On the afternoon in question, he knocked and waited for the little answering scratch that sometimes came from the other side.

There was no scratch.

There was a shout of delight from Estelle, followed by a noise like a cushion striking the floor.

“Come in, Father! They have found it!”

Rome paused with his hand on the latch. “Found what?”

“The evidence!”

He considered turning around. The last time his daughter had spoken about evidence, a council clerk had spent an inconvenient portion of his day rescuing documents from water. But the door opened before he could retreat, and Estelle appeared beneath it with a smile that made retreat impossible.

She was carrying a wooden brush in one hand and a folded cloth in the other. Behind her, the room looked as though a small, determined storm had passed through it. Cushions lay in a heap by the low hearth. A woven ball had been deposited in the drinking bowl. One cat occupied the window seat; the other sat beside a basket, examining a bright thread with grave suspicion.

The evidence, apparently, was Rome himself.

Estelle looked up at his silver-white hair and began to laugh.

“Oh,” she said. “It's worse in the sunlight.”

Rome closed his eyes. He had known there were still flecks of glitter in his hair. He had not known there were enough to be compared with the sun.

“Your mother,” he said carefully, “assured me she could remove it.”

“She can. She said you should come here first.”

“Why here?”

“Because the cats are already covered in it.”

That was not an answer he found comforting.

The window-seat cat rose and stretched. This was Lexi, the celestial Maine Coon whose dark-blue coat carried tiny luminous points like stars caught in velvet. When she turned her head, the lights seemed to travel across her fur without ever falling away. She had been given to Daysha as an early birthday gift, and the family had long since stopped trying to decide whether the stars were more astonishing when Lexi moved or when she slept.

The second Maine Coon was a different animal entirely: a broad, soft-footed presence with an impressive tail and a talent for making every basket appear to have been built specifically for its inspection. No one in the room was inclined to mistake it for Lexi, even from behind. Lexi watched before acting. The other cat had already put one paw into the basket.

Rome looked between them. “How many are covered?”

Estelle lowered her voice as if discussing a difficult medical condition. “Perhaps all of us.”

“Perhaps?”

She held out the cloth. Three glittering specks winked from its fold.

Rome took the cloth between two fingers. “That is not all of us.”

“You haven't looked at your shoulders.”

He looked. The front of his coat was mostly respectable. The back, which he could not see without turning himself inside out, had apparently become a minor attraction.

“Ah.”

The cat beside the basket sneezed. A thread drifted to the floor. Estelle went to retrieve it, and Rome caught her wrist gently before she could crawl beneath the table.

“Wait. Is there anything sharp down there?”

“No.”

“Have you looked?”

She stopped, thought, and shook her head.

“Then we look before we reach.”

He crouched to peer beneath the furniture. There was no broken glass, only a lost ribbon, two wooden balls, a cloth mouse, and a small amount of glitter that had collected in the grooves of the floorboards. Estelle knelt beside him.

“It's like a little silver river,” she said.

“It's like a job for someone with a brush.”

“I have a brush.”

“So I see.”

He passed her the cloth mouse. She offered it to Lexi, who inspected it once and declined. The other Maine Coon seized it immediately and carried it away with the determined stride of a guard escorting a prisoner.

Rome, still crouched, discovered a bright fleck on the toe of his boot. He rubbed it with his thumb. It moved to his thumb.

“Remarkable,” he murmured. “It has learned to travel.”

“That is why Mother said not to rub it.”

Rome stood. “Then your mother has arrived at the problem before the king.”

“She often does.”

“Your Majesty?”

Daysha's voice came from the doorway. Estelle turned so quickly that her brush nearly caught the edge of a cushion. Rome looked up, and the expression on his wife's face changed from concern to unmistakable amusement.

Daysha had come without attendants. Her familiar blue floral gown moved softly about her ankles, and her very long iridescent blue-violet hair fell over one shoulder. The afternoon light picked out the violet of her eyes and the elegant, proportionate points of her high-elf ears. She had brought a shallow porcelain basin, a roll of plain cloth, and a small lidded box.

Rome studied the objects.

“Are you going to wash me?”

“Not unless you insist.”

“Then why the basin?”

“To give the glitter somewhere to go.”

“And the box?”

“To give it somewhere to stay.”

Estelle pointed at her father's hair. “There is a great deal.”

“I can see that,” Daysha said. “Or rather, I can see nearly nothing else.”

Rome put one hand over his face. A little glitter fell from his sleeve.

Daysha laughed, then set down the basin before she laughed hard enough to spill it. She approached him, took his hand away from his face, and kissed his cheek.

“You were very patient with her,” she said quietly.

“I was attempting to be patient with everyone.”

“I know.”

He looked toward Estelle, who was already showing the brush to the other cat. “I am less certain I was successful.”

“Patience is not the same as pretending nothing happened.”

She said it without making the room solemn. Rome knew that tone: the one she used when a question deserved an answer but not an audience. He gave her hand a small squeeze. Then he looked down at the basin.

“Will this be unpleasant?”

“Only if you keep making that face.”

“I am a king. I am entitled to inquire about a spell.”

“You are entitled to sit on that chair and stop trying to remove the glitter with your fingers.”

He obeyed. Estelle looked delighted to see that her mother could give orders in the catroom too.

Daysha did not begin with magic. She asked Estelle to put the brush down and showed her how to gather the loose cloths into a separate basket. She moved the cats' drinking bowl out of the way. She checked the low hearth to be certain no flame was burning, and she asked Rome to remove the brooch at his collar so the spell would not catch on its delicate setting.

“Does magic catch on jewels?” Estelle asked.

“Not in the way a thread catches on a thorn,” Daysha said. “But a good spell should know what it is being asked to move. I want the loose glitter, not the brooch, not the embroidery, and certainly not your father's hair.”

Rome touched the ribbon at the back of his head. “That last distinction is important to me.”

“It is important to me too.”

Estelle examined the brooch when he placed it in her hand. She set it safely on the high shelf where neither cat could reach it.

Daysha drew a slow circle over the basin. The air above the porcelain trembled, as though a window had been opened onto a distant summer afternoon. There was no thunder, no blinding light, and no sudden gust to frighten the animals. A handful of silver motes lifted from Rome's shoulder and hovered for a moment.

Estelle gasped.

The motes drifted toward the basin. One hesitated near the edge of his collar, then followed the others. They settled with the faintest sound, like dry sand falling on a plate.

Rome looked at his sleeve. It was clean.

“That seems promising.”

“It is only the easy part,” Daysha said. “Stay still.”

She moved behind him. A thin shimmer followed the line of his long silver hair, separating the glitter from the strands without pulling them. More motes rose, catching the light as they turned. Estelle stood very quietly, watching her father's hair emerge from beneath the false stars. When Daysha reached the ribbon, she loosened it and worked carefully through the hair beneath.

The other Maine Coon chose that moment to leap onto the chair.

Rome's hands went up. Daysha stopped the spell at once. The floating motes remained suspended for a breath and then settled gently back onto his coat.

“Off,” Rome told the cat.

The cat regarded him with perfect indifference and put a paw on his knee.

Estelle began to giggle.

“Not helpful,” Rome said.

“Perhaps it wants to supervise,” Daysha suggested.

“It has chosen a very inconvenient position.”

Daysha lifted the animal, supporting its body with both hands. It accepted this with the tolerant expression of a creature permitting an interruption to a much more important appointment. She set it beside the basket, where it promptly began to investigate the lid of her box.

“Now,” she said, “we shall need a second rule.”

Estelle straightened. “No cats on Father?”

“No moving magic while a cat is in its path.”

Rome glanced over his shoulder. “I rather like that rule.”

Daysha resumed, drawing the glitter in short, careful streams instead of one broad cloud. The motes moved from his hair to the basin and settled there. The blue floral embroidery on her sleeve remained untouched. The carved wood of the chair retained its polish. His silver hair fell cleanly over his shoulders.

Estelle stepped closer to examine him.

“May I touch it?”

“When the spell is finished,” Daysha said.

“May I touch the glitter?”

“No. The glitter is precisely what we are putting away.”

The last motes lifted from Rome's collar. Daysha closed her hand, and the air became still. She inspected the seam at his shoulder and the ribbon at his neck, then walked around to face him.

“There,” she said. “You may return to the council without illuminating the entire table.”

Rome ran his fingers through his hair. Nothing glittered on his hand.

“I am grateful.”

“I know.”

“I am also concerned about the cats.”

“So am I.”

Lexi had watched the spell from the window seat, her celestial fur shining in the slanting light. Estelle climbed onto the low stool beside her and peered at the stars in the dark-blue coat.

“Mother, if you take the glitter off Lexi, will her stars go away?”

Daysha set the lid on the porcelain basin but did not close it yet.

“No,” she said. “Those are hers.”

“How do you know which ones are hers?”

“Because the stars are part of Lexi. The glitter is something that has landed on her. They may look alike from across the room, but they are not the same thing.”

Estelle frowned with the seriousness she reserved for questions that sounded simple until an adult answered them.

“So if I put glitter on myself, it isn't my stars?”

“Not even if you wear it for a very long time.”

Rome considered this. “An excellent distinction for the court to learn.”

Daysha gave him a look. “Do not start a speech.”

“I had only reached the opening sentence.”

“Then you may save the rest for later.”

Lexi rose from the cushion and approached the edge of the stool. Daysha held out a hand, letting the cat decide whether to come nearer. Lexi sniffed her fingers, then stepped onto the folded cloth Daysha had placed beside the basin.

The queen did not cast the same broad spell she had used on Rome. She checked the long fur with her fingers first, separating the strands near the shoulder and tail to find the ordinary specks that had lodged there. She removed a loose thread by hand. Then she made a smaller, slower motion above the coat.

A few grains of glitter rose from Lexi's fur.

Her luminous constellations did not move.

Estelle held her breath. The stars shone steadily in the deep-blue coat, as familiar and mysterious as ever. When the last stray speck had drifted into the basin, Lexi gave a single offended twitch of her tail and stepped away to wash one paw.

“She kept them,” Estelle whispered.

“She did.”

“Could you do that to the sky?”

Daysha looked toward the window. Beyond it, the courtyard was bright and cloudless.

“I should hope the sky needs less cleaning than this room.”

“That isn't an answer.”

“It is the answer I have at present.”

Rome leaned back in his chair, smiling. “Your mother is wise enough not to promise to polish the heavens.”

Estelle accepted that for the moment. She looked toward the second Maine Coon, which had succeeded in pulling the box lid halfway off.

“What about this one?”

“This one,” Daysha said, crossing the room, “is determined to make our work more interesting.”

The cat had found the narrow gap beneath the lid and was using one paw to pry at it. Daysha lifted the box away. The cat sat back, apparently disappointed that its progress had not been recognized.

Rome rose to help. “I can hold the basin.”

“You can hold the lid.”

“I have been promoted.”

“Do not make me reconsider.”

Daysha inspected the second cat's coat with the same care she had given Lexi's. She found a scattering of ordinary glitter along one flank, but nothing that resembled Lexi's celestial stars. Estelle wanted to know whether the cat would enjoy the spell. Daysha said that the cat's comfort mattered more than whether the family enjoyed watching it.

They waited until the animal settled on a cushion. Daysha worked gently, pausing when the tail flicked and stopping entirely when the cat stood up. The glitter gathered in the basin, a small silver drift beside the larger heap from Rome's hair. No one held the animal still for the sake of finishing quickly.

“This takes longer,” Estelle observed.

“Yes.”

“Because the cat is difficult?”

“Because the cat cannot tell us in words what it likes,” Daysha replied. “We have to pay attention to what it does tell us.”

Rome watched the cat settle again. “That is another lesson the council might profit from.”

Daysha looked at him.

“I shall not make a speech,” he promised.

When the second cat was clean, Estelle fetched the brush and approached the patch beneath the table. She had watched her mother lift glitter into the air and believed she understood the trick. She held out her free hand and whispered an imitation of the word Daysha had used.

Nothing happened.

She whispered it again, a little louder.

The brush slipped from her fingers and struck the floor. The other cat looked up, offended by the noise.

“Mother?”

Daysha came to her side. “What were you trying to do?”

“Clean the floor.”

“With my spell?”

Estelle nodded.

“Do you know where the glitter would go?”

“To the basin.”

“Do you know how to keep the dust out of the cats' eyes? Or how to leave the wood polish where it belongs?”

Estelle looked at the narrow grooves between the boards. “No.”

“Then we will use the brush.”

“But you can do it in a moment.”

“I can do it because I have practiced. You can learn, but learning begins with knowing what you have not yet learned.”

Estelle considered this, then took the brush back. She swept carefully along the grain of the boards. The glitter moved into a little line. She swept it onto a folded piece of paper and carried it to the basin. It took several trips. Rome fetched a second brush and worked beside her, which made the job feel less like a punishment and more like something the room had asked of them both.

“Does a king sweep floors?” Estelle asked.

“A king should know how much work he creates when he does not.”

“You didn't make the glitter.”

“No,” Rome said. “But I have been walking around the palace wearing it.”

Estelle thought about the glitter in the council chamber and the glitter on her father's shoulders. “I suppose that makes some of it yours.”

“I suppose it does.”

Daysha checked the cushions and the cloth basket, lifting a few stray motes with the smallest possible movement of her fingers. When Estelle finished her section, Daysha examined it and thanked her. Rome gathered the last sweepings from beside the chair.

Only then did Daysha close the porcelain basin and fasten its lid. She transferred the glitter into the small lidded box, which she sealed with a quiet touch. The magic was not a punishment for the glitter; it was simply a way of keeping the fine particles from scattering through the room again.

Estelle looked at the box with renewed interest.

“Can we keep it?”

“For a craft table, perhaps, if someone supervises it,” Daysha said. “Not for another surprise over a council meeting.”

Rome raised an eyebrow. “A condition I endorse.”

“I wasn't going to do that again,” Estelle protested.

“I am glad,” said Rome.

“I was thinking of making a picture of the cats.”

“That,” Daysha said, “sounds much safer.”

Estelle looked at Lexi, who was now resting beside the other Maine Coon. The pair had settled with the easy closeness of animals that had decided the humans were no longer worth supervising.

“Lexi's stars would be difficult to draw.”

“Then draw the stars you can see,” Daysha said. “You need not copy every one to show who she is.”

Estelle nodded, but did not ask for the box again. She went to the window and stood with her hands behind her back, considering the two animals and the silver light on the courtyard stones.

Rome approached Daysha and took her hand.

“You could have done all of it yourself,” he said.

“Yes.”

“Why didn't you?”

“Because the room belonged to all of us while we were in it.”

He looked toward Estelle, who was telling Lexi that the drawing would require a great deal of blue.

“And because she needed to see what happens after the laughter,” he said.

“That too.”

Rome lowered his voice. “I think I needed to see it as well.”

Daysha rested her head briefly against his shoulder. “You spend your days making decisions that other people must carry out. It is good to carry a brush now and then.”

He smiled. “I shall ask the council to record my new qualification.”

“Do not.”

“Very well.”

At the window, Estelle turned. “Are we going outside?”

Daysha looked at the floor. It was clean. The basin was covered. The cats were comfortable. No servant had been left to discover the mess after the royal family departed.

“Yes,” she said. “We can go toward the courtyard.”

“Will the cats come?”

“If they wish.”

Estelle opened the inner door only a little, so the animals could choose without being startled. Lexi approached first, paused at the threshold, and turned back as though to inspect the room she was leaving. The second Maine Coon followed at a more deliberate pace, stopping to sniff the empty basket before deciding that the corridor might contain something better.

Rome offered Daysha his arm. She accepted it with a smile, and Estelle walked between them, reaching for her father's free hand.

They passed through a sunlit gallery where the air smelled faintly of stone warmed by afternoon light. Beyond the arches, the courtyard lay open, with a low fountain, flowering borders, and the ordinary sounds of palace life: footsteps, a distant conversation, a gardener setting down tools. None of it waited for a royal announcement. People were carrying on with the work that allowed a household to be a home.

Estelle looked up at Rome's clean silver hair.

“You look like yourself again.”

“Was I someone else before?”

“You were a very sparkly king.”

“And now?”

“A regular king.”

Rome gave a small, exaggerated sigh. “How quickly greatness passes.”

Daysha laughed. Her violet eyes caught the sunlight, and for a moment Rome looked at her with the same helpless admiration that always threatened to make him forget whatever sensible remark he had intended to offer.

“Not entirely regular,” she said, straightening the collar she had cleaned. “You did help sweep the catroom.”

“Then I shall treasure the distinction.”

Estelle squeezed both their hands. “Can we make the cat picture after we walk?”

“We can,” Daysha said. “We will use the craft table, and we will ask before opening the glitter.”

“And I will draw two cats,” Estelle declared. “Not two Lexis.”

“Very wise,” Rome said.

Lexi crossed the threshold behind them, her own dark-blue constellations undisturbed by any spell. The other Maine Coon followed with its tail held high, interested not in the family's conversation but in a bright patch of warmth on the courtyard stones.

They moved into the afternoon without hurry. Rome's hair no longer glittered. The silver motes had been put away. Yet Estelle kept glancing back at the two animals, as though she had learned something worth remembering: that a borrowed sparkle could be cleaned away, and a true constellation would remain.
