## Scene

The forty minutes the captain could offer had passed without a tool being lifted. At the Gilded Spindle, Garrick laid two sheets on the counter: the glazier's proposed stages and a blank authority for a possible inspection.

The youngest apprentice put a scrap of cloth over the word APPROVED.

“That is what people will see first,” she said. “They will think the shutter may come down.”

“It says conditional beneath it,” Garrick replied.

“Beneath it, in smaller letters.”

The silver-haired seamstress drew the sheet closer. Wednesday's proposal had priced the exposed joint separately; Thursday had proved the promised watch too short. Neither fact had changed. She crossed out APPROVED and wrote REQUESTED in its place.

Garrick winced. “A merchant cannot live forever among requests.”

“A worker cannot escape through a blocked passage because a merchant would prefer a different verb.”

Across the counter, the red-haired artisan measured the narrow service route with the handle of an unlit lantern. No trestle could stand there. The youngest apprentice copied the route onto the reverse of the page and left space for the staff to mark it clear on the actual day.

“What are we authorizing, then?” Garrick asked.

“An appointment to decide whether an inspection can begin,” said the seamstress. “Not permission to expose the window tonight. Not permission to replace a joint nobody has seen.”

He divided the money column into three ruled spaces. The first was reserved for the glazier's firm price for a protected inspection; the second, for a separate upper limit for replacement work that would require a fresh decision; the third he left empty.

“Glass?” asked the apprentice.

“Not here,” Garrick said. “A lattice maker acknowledging an order is not a pane in our hands.”

Nor would an empty number be charged to the wages ledger. He showed the apprentices Thursday's recorded preparation time and the unpaid acknowledgment spaces, making certain each could see that the hours existed without pretending the coins had been handed over.

Queen Daysha, waiting near the closed fitting barrier, watched Lexi investigate the blank third column with great interest.

“Does a royal signature make the watch longer?” she asked.

Garrick almost smiled. “Not even yours, Majesty.”

“Then do not ask for one.”

The youngest apprentice read the proposed conditions aloud: seventy uninterrupted minutes confirmed by the captain; the exterior route protected; the receiving room shut to visitors while work was underway; the staff's evacuation route checked again; the glazier free to decline unsafe conditions. Any newly exposed damage would be described in writing before a replacement cost could be authorized.

“And if any of those are missing?” asked Daysha.

“We cancel,” the apprentice said. “Before the case is opened.”

Garrick signed only the request to seek a suitable appointment. The seamstress countersigned that she had reviewed its conditions, not that they had been met. The repair ledger still showed no exposed work authorized.

Beyond the shutter, evening light dwindled. Inside, the dais remained closed. The shop possessed a clearer way to ask for safety, but it did not yet possess safety itself.
