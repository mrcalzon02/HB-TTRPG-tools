## The Names Left Open

The embassy clerk had left two copies of the same account on the table. One carried the refugees’ names in the margin. The other had empty spaces where those names ought to be.

Daysha read them both and laid a fingertip over the blanks. Her blue hair fell forward as she bent to the candlelight. “If this is sent without names, someone will call it a rumor.”

Rome stood opposite her. “If it is sent with names, someone may decide those people are easier to answer than their accusations.”

Neither possibility made the account less true. Three families had described the same abandoned crossing, but their journeys differed in details: who had seen the carts, which road had been closed, how long the children had waited. The clerk had arranged their words into a single polished paragraph. It was easier to read. It also made them sound as though they had spoken together.

“Did they approve this?” Daysha asked.

“The clerk asked whether they would testify. He didn't ask whether they wanted their stories joined.”

“Then this cannot be their statement.”

Rome picked up the cleaner copy. “It can be our summary of what we heard. That matters. If we pretend every line is a direct quotation, the first contradiction will discredit all three families.”

“And if we remove the details that differ?”

“We give whoever reads it an account that cannot be challenged because it says almost nothing.”

Daysha pushed the polished sheet aside. “I know what an embassy wants from a witness. A useful sentence. A name beneath it. Someone else bears the consequences of making the sentence useful.”

Rome looked at the three separate notes. “I want the crossing examined. I also want the record to survive an unfriendly reader. We cannot do either by borrowing their voices.”

For a while they worked without speaking. Daysha marked each observation that belonged to one family alone. Rome circled the points repeated independently, then struck through a sentence that claimed all three had seen the same cart. Only one had.

When they finished, the official draft contained no accusation disguised as an established finding. It recorded that three accounts had been heard, that they differed in particulars, and that no witness had yet approved how their words or names might be shared.

Rome held the page up to the light. “It may be too cautious to move anyone.”

“It may,” Daysha said. Her violet eyes were tired. “The other version might move them against the wrong people.”

He placed both drafts beside the original notes. “Then we do not file either as their testimony tonight.”

“We ask them tomorrow what they are willing to have carried forward,” she said. “Separately. Without promising that silence will protect them or that speaking will change anything.”

Rome uncapped the ink. At the foot of the draft he wrote, in his own hand: *Witness review pending.*

Daysha watched him leave enough room beneath those words for an answer neither of them could supply.
