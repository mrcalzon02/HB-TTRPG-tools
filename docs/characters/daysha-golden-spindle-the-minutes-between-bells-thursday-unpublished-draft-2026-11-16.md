## The Minutes Between Bells

On Thursday, the youngest apprentice came to the Golden Spindle with the closure notice folded in her pocket. Nobody had posted it. The front bell still rang for customers admitted only to the receiving room.

Master Garrick Vellmont had cleared a table for the glazier. The red-haired artisan had carried three lengths of cloth into secure storage to keep the passage clear. Their morning was already costing them something.

The captain arrived with one guard and a measured promise.

“From the quarter bell until the change of watch,” he said. “Forty minutes without interruption. After that I can promise you nothing.”

The glazier opened his case without taking out a tool. “Seventy minutes. That is the shortest interval in which I can lift the strained fitting, inspect what lies behind it and secure the shutter again. I won't begin a job whose ending you cannot guard.”

“We could at least start,” Garrick said, more from disappointment than conviction.

The apprentice took the folded notice from her pocket. “And if the watch leaves while the opening is exposed, do we stay to hold it shut?”

Nobody answered.

“I cannot stay after closing,” she continued. “And I will not volunteer the others by standing here quietly.”

The silver-haired master seamstress laid the route diagram beside the schedule. “Then the work does not start. We agreed yesterday. Today is when agreement costs us something.”

Garrick looked at the empty table. “We have disrupted a morning for nothing.”

“Not nothing,” the red-haired artisan replied. “Record the time we spent clearing this room. Don't turn it into leave because the glass is still outside.”

He opened the wage ledger. The corrected amounts remained due, receipt spaces blank. He added their preparation time to the work record without marking payment. In his separate repair ledger he wrote: *Watch interval insufficient; shutter untouched; no stage authorized.*

Queen Daysha arrived as the glazier closed his case. Lexi, her star-flecked dark-blue Maine Coon, inspected the deserted table and settled upon it.

“Who stopped the work?” Daysha asked.

“I did,” answered the apprentice, before Garrick could take credit.

“Was there any objection?”

“There was disappointment,” said the seamstress. “That is not the same thing.”

Daysha regarded the folded notice. “Then do not post a promise you cannot keep.”

The apprentice pocketed it again. The receiving-room bell rang; a customer could still be served there, but no fitting was offered beyond the barrier.

Garrick sent the captain away with one exact requirement: seventy protected minutes, confirmed before anyone moved another worktable. He requested an answer, not another assurance.

At closing, the shutter stood untouched, the dais barred and wages unpaid. The apprentice's preparation time, however, existed in ink. No one had mistaken her refusal for an absence of work.
