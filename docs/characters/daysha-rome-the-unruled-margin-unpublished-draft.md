## The Unruled Margin

Rome had chosen the smallest ledger on the table. It was a private account of supplies they had used on the road, not a document anyone required him to sign. He wanted to finish copying the last three entries before putting it away.

The book would not stay open.

He held the left page with his left palm, picked up the pen, and discovered that he needed that same hand to move the paper beneath it. The quill slipped. A comma of ink landed across the margin.

“Excellent,” he muttered. “A complete inventory of one blot.”

Daysha looked up from the window seat. Her blue hair spilled over a shoulder; in the afternoon light her violet eyes followed the pen, not the empty right sleeve. “Would you like another sheet?”

“No. I would like to be able to turn this one.”

He reached toward the page with an arm that was no longer there. The movement stopped at his shoulder. For an instant he seemed more angry at the surprise than at the loss itself.

Daysha stood, then waited.

“You can turn it,” he said finally. “Just the page. I want to write the figures.”

She turned the leaf and set it flat. Rome caught the next page beneath the base of the inkpot, tested whether the arrangement would hold, and began again. His letters were cramped and uncertain. He crossed out one number, checked the original slip, and wrote it correctly on the line below.

Daysha watched the correction. “That's the same amount on two slips.”

“Two different purchases.”

“The handwriting is identical.”

“Mine,” he said. “I copied the first one twice.”

He made a mark against the duplicate rather than tearing it away. He could have asked someone to keep the accounts for him. He could have had the pen taken from his hand, the error neatly repaired, the book returned without anyone seeing how much work three lines had cost.

Instead he put a finger on the remaining slip. “Read the date to me, please. The ink has rubbed off at the edge.”

Daysha leaned close enough to see, careful not to crowd the table. “The last digit is gone. I can't tell you what isn't there.”

“Good. Leave it uncertain.”

He wrote *date unclear* beside the entry, then rested the pen. His shoulder ached from holding the page steady. The empty sleeve had caught under the desk's edge, and he drew it free with his left hand.

“I keep thinking the hard part ought to be something grand,” he said. “An enemy. A spell. A king's command. Not a stupid notebook.”

Daysha set the slips in a stack without altering their order. “Would it be easier if I finished the copying?”

“Much.”

“Do you want me to?”

He studied the uneven lines and the two mistakes he had managed to catch. “Not yet. Could you move the inkpot to the far corner? I need room to brace the book.”

She moved it. He settled the ledger against his body, where one arm and the table could hold it steady between them. The next line took less time.

Daysha did not call it a triumph. Rome did not pretend it was easy. When he finished, he closed the book himself, with the uncertain date still plainly marked inside.
