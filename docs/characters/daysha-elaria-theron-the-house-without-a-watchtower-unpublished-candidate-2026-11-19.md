## Scene

Elaria laid a folded handkerchief on the sitting-room table to keep the damp field sketch from staining the wood. The three of them had returned from No. 62 Applewoud Lane, and Theron was still drawing the turn between the gate and the former house site from memory.

“You've made a very straight path,” Elaria told him.

“I've made a line. The ground may object.”

Daysha set her tea beside the paper. Her long sapphire-blue hair had caught the day's mist, and she was still finding leaves in its ends. “The ground has already raised several objections.”

“The garden has not,” Elaria said. “I could almost see it. A quiet place beyond the orchard, where someone may walk without being counted.”

Theron placed one fingertip by the hedge. “And someone could approach unseen from here. I used to make a living noticing such things.”

“You also once had guards outside every door,” Elaria answered. “I do not want to leave one formal household only to live inside another.”

“I wasn't proposing a tower.”

“Not yet.”

Daysha smiled at their old familiarity, but neither of them looked away from the sketch. Her mother's wish was not an argument against her safety. Her father's caution was not permission to turn somebody else's land into a royal outpost.

“Suppose a watch could see the gate,” Daysha said, “without seeing every garden chair. Is that possible?”

Theron studied the lines. “Possibly. This isn't a survey. I don't know the true height of the hedge, what lies behind the orchard in winter, or where a watcher could stand without blocking the lane.”

“Nor do we know where rain will make a footpath soft,” Elaria added. “If I have to carry a basket from the gate, I would rather not need someone's arm each time the weather turns.”

“The approach should be comfortable for you both,” Daysha said, “and for someone arriving with a walking stick or a cart. But a path through the lowest ground may be the worst place to put one.”

Theron turned the paper around. His pencilled straight line now looked less certain. He made three small question marks instead of more lines.

“Access,” he said. “Drainage. What can actually be seen from the road.”

“And what needn't be seen,” Elaria said. “Our breakfast table. My herbs. A place to quarrel about the weather without an audience.”

Daysha found herself imagining a guard politely pretending not to hear her parents dispute a peach tree. She knew how easily such arrangements became permanent once people began calling them precautions.

“No instructions to the guard captain,” she said. “No posts, screens, walls or paid work. We'll ask qualified surveyors what the land permits, and only when there is a proper right to commission them. Security needs must be considered separately from Lillian's price and your household's wishes.”

Elaria put her hand over Daysha's. “I would rather have a small home we may close the door on than a splendid one that cannot be ours for an hour.”

Theron looked at the three question marks. “Then the first thing we build is a better list of questions.”

“On paper,” Daysha said.

“On paper,” he agreed, and finally set down the pencil.
