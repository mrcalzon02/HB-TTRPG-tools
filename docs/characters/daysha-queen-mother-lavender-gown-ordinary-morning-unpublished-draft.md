Daysha had expected the lavender-and-pink gown to appear beneath chandeliers, with half the court turning to admire it. Instead, she found it beside a teapot.

Her mother-in-law had chosen a quiet sitting room. Silver-white hair rose in its careful coils beneath a jeweled crown; the new ballgown's gold accents caught the daylight whenever she moved. A chair had been drawn up to the window, and the Queen Mother was studying its arms with the concentration of a general considering a narrow bridge.

“Is something wrong with it?” Daysha asked.

“Only the chair. It was made for someone with much less skirt.”

Daysha hurried forward, but the older queen raised a hand.

“I can negotiate with furniture, dear.”

She gathered the lavender folds, turned sideways, and sat without so much as disturbing a gemstone. Her triumphant expression was so familiar that Daysha laughed before she could help herself.

“There,” said the Queen Mother. “An excellent gown. A thoroughly defeated chair.”

Daysha touched the back of the other seat. She was dressed for an ordinary visit, with her long blue hair loose across her shoulders. “I thought you might save it for something important.”

“I am wearing it for something important.”

“There is no audience.”

“Precisely.”

Daysha looked again at the gown she had chosen as a gift. She remembered how certain she had been that its jewels and color would please the woman who had welcomed her into the family. She had not considered whether wearing it might become another obligation.

“Perhaps I should have asked what you wanted to do in it,” she said.

Her mother-in-law poured a second cup. “You asked what I liked. You remembered. Those are not small things.”

“But perhaps it is rather grand for tea.”

“It is,” the older queen said, utterly satisfied. “And if I only wore grand things when somebody was counting who could see them, I would be letting the audience own my wardrobe.”

Daysha settled into the opposite chair. Her pointed ears warmed a little. “I suppose I have been trying to make certain you approve.”

“I do. But you must not spend your whole life trying to earn the same answer from me.”

The Queen Mother moved the sugar bowl between them. “Tell me something you disliked about the dress.”

Daysha blinked. “I gave it to you.”

“And I am keeping it. That makes us both safe to be candid.”

Daysha considered. “The gold is lovely, but I worried the embroidery around the cuff might catch whenever you reached for a book.”

The older queen reached for the teapot, deliberately showing that the cuff cleared the handle. “A sensible worry. Better than pretending I never read.”

They spent a while testing ordinary movements: lifting a cup, leaning toward the window, turning a page of the book on the table. Nothing caught. Daysha began to look less like someone awaiting judgment.

At last the Queen Mother raised her cup.

“To a gift I can actually live in.”

Daysha lifted hers. “And to chairs that learn their place.”

The Queen Mother's laughter filled the small room. No herald announced them. No one came to admire the jewels. The dress remained beautiful, but Daysha was relieved to discover that it did not need to be the most important thing there.
