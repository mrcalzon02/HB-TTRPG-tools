# The Ribbon on Each Box

**Status:** UNPUBLISHED / NONCANONICAL companion vignette for creator review. Not a publication, approved history, new illustration, or replacement for the canonical baking scene.

**Authority:** [Royal Palace Patisserie — Canonical Baking Staff](daysha-royal-patisserie-staff-canon.md), including the already-finished *last pastry* scene. This companion begins **after** Daysha has allocated the individual sweets and given her **own final pastry** its second generous layer of cream; it does not retell that moment.

**Chronology:** An undated domestic kitchen continuation; no fixed placement in the royal history, regnal year, Estelle age, or calendar. “Mom,” “Dad,” and “that person” remain unspecified recipients. Delivery arrangements and incidental dialogue are proposed fiction only.

---

The trays were ready. The labels were not.

Mirelle Rosethorn held up a square of folded paper between two flour-dusted fingers. “Your Majesty, I can pack a cream puff to survive a journey down three staircases. But I cannot address a parcel to ‘that person.’”

Daysha looked up, blue hair spilling over the pink-and-white ruffles at her shoulder. Her violet eyes went wide with recognition.

“Oh! That was only to remind myself.”

“Then I shall leave the reminding to you,” Mirelle said brightly. She set the paper down without peeking beneath its fold.

Master Caelion Valebrook inspected the cooling rack. His ash-blond hair had escaped its tie, and his burgundy neckerchief had acquired a stripe of flour. “No lids until the pastry is cool. Nothing ruins a good crust faster than a boxful of its own steam.”

Tavren Duskwick, dark hair standing up from an enthusiastic morning at the whisk, leaned over the cream puffs. “And nothing ruins my filling faster than leaving it beside the ovens while everyone argues about ribbon.”

“Nobody is arguing,” Mirelle said.

“We have not selected the ribbon.”

“That is not an argument.”

“It is the beginning of one.”

Selienne Moonmere intervened with the quiet authority of someone who had once rescued a sugar flower from an open window. She arranged the parcels beneath the cool end of the worktable, her silver-blond hair falling in soft waves beneath her bonnet. “Different ribbons,” she suggested, “but the same plain boxes. Then no one will be able to tell which gift was considered grandest.”

Daysha considered that seriously. “I wouldn't want them compared.”

“Then they won't be,” Selienne said. “The decoration belongs to the person opening it, not to anyone watching it leave.”

The queen wrote separate instructions on four small slips, folded each one shut, and passed them to the bakers herself. One was for him; the others kept their private descriptions of Mom, Dad, and that person. No one asked for names. Caelion checked the parcels for safe carrying; Mirelle tucked each pastry into its own little cradle; Selienne laid a tiny sugar blossom beside the filling where it could not be crushed; Tavren moved the boxes away from the warm oven wall.

When the lids were tied, the four elves carried the gifts to the inner service door. Daysha pointed out the separate handoffs, and each baker entrusted a box to the appropriate household messenger with its folded instruction intact. They returned with empty hands and the satisfied expressions of people whose work had arrived at the right beginning.

Only one pastry remained on the table.

Tavren inspected the extraordinary second crown of cream already rising above it. “And this one needs a larger box.”

“No box,” Daysha said, grinning.

Caelion passed her a plate instead. Mirelle fetched four small spoons for the bakers' own leftover custard, and Selienne set the last unbroken sugar flower in the middle of their shared dish.

Daysha took up her plate. The gifts had gone out by different doors. For a moment, everyone in the kitchen had something sweet of their own.
