## The Measure of Mint

Rain had left the market stones shining. Daysha lifted the hem of her light-blue day dress clear of a puddle and discovered that her mother had already stopped at the first herb stall.

“Your Majesty,” the stallholder began.

“Please charge us the same as everyone else,” Daysha said, then glanced at Elaria. “Was that terribly queenly?”

“It was terribly early,” Elaria replied. “Let her tell us the price.”

The woman set two bunches of mint on the counter. “Three coppers for yesterday’s cutting. Five for this morning’s. The fresh one will keep longer, if you have somewhere cool to hang it.”

Daysha bent close. Both looked green enough. Elaria touched a leaf on each bunch and held her fingers out.

“The older leaves have gone soft,” she said. “They’ll still flavor a pot of tea. They simply won’t survive a week in a kitchen.”

“So three coppers is not a bargain if I need them next week,” Daysha said.

“And five is unnecessary if you mean to brew them tonight.”

The stallholder’s shoulders eased. She poured a little hot water over a broken leaf and passed them a shared cup to smell. Daysha inhaled, then sneezed hard enough to make the woman laugh.

“That,” Daysha informed her mother, “is a very persuasive mint.”

At the cloth stall, a breeze worried the corners of a rack of linen. Daysha caught one runaway sample before it landed in a muddy gutter.

“Six silvers a length for the close weave,” the merchant said, retrieving it with thanks. “Four for the lighter. They look alike when folded, but the cheaper one twists after washing.”

Daysha examined the blue thread running along the edge of the sample. It nearly matched her dress. She had been about to praise it when she noticed a faint blue mark on her thumb.

“The dye comes away,” she said.

The merchant looked embarrassed, then brought out a second strip. “That roll hasn’t been rinsed properly. I should have marked it. This one has.”

Elaria let Daysha test the new strip against a damp corner of clean cloth. No color transferred.

“You knew about the mint,” Daysha whispered as they stepped aside. “Did you know about the dye?”

“No. I was watching whether you would ask.”

“That sounds suspiciously like a lesson.”

“Only because you insist on learning things.”

Daysha folded the merchant’s little sample neatly and returned it. Neither woman had ordered a length or bought a bundle of herbs. They had prices, a few useful answers, and a promise to come back once they knew what the kitchens actually needed.

Before leaving, Daysha asked both stallholders their names and thanked each of them, rather than pretending she remembered.

“Next time,” she told Elaria, “we’ll bring a list.”

“Next time,” Elaria said, accepting her daughter’s arm, “we’ll remember to look at it.”
