The last of the guests departed beneath the crescent-moon banners, and the ballroom grew so quiet that Daysha could hear the faint creak of a ribbon in the open window.

Lady Seraphine's laughter had been the final sound to leave the hall. Vaelira had offered a discreet farewell, Ilyndra a distracted smile, Caelith a warm embrace, and Nyssara a look that suggested she had noticed the queen trying not to yawn. Five new friends, five different ways of making a crowded room feel less like a court.

Now there was only the moonlight and the aftermath of the music.

Daysha stood beside a pillar, the pale swirls of her lavender-violet gown catching what remained of the candlelight. Its great side bow had survived a full evening of dancing. Her long sapphire-blue hair spilled over the fabric, blue even where the silver moon struck its ends. She tipped her crowned head and found Rome near the musicians' empty chairs, his silver-white hair loosened at the shoulders.

He was holding a narrow folder.

“Is that tomorrow?” she asked.

“Only the notes I meant to look at before morning.”

“Only,” Daysha repeated. She had spent the earlier part of the day at the temple among priests and clerics. The words of that visit still lingered, but she had been hearing everyone's words since sunrise, each one asking something of her.

Rome looked down at the folder, then toward the empty dance floor. “I brought this because I thought there would be a moment between dances.”

“There was.”

“I spent it watching you.”

“An excellent use of your time.”

He smiled, but did not open the folder. On the small side table stood Daysha's own folded list of matters to remember. She had set it there before the first guests arrived, certain that she would need it. She crossed to the table and placed his notes beside hers.

“Neither of us has forgotten our work,” she said. “It will still be there when we come back to it.”

“And if I begin remembering it now?”

“Then I shall have to distract you.”

His blue eyes brightened. “How?”

She offered him her hand.

Rome glanced once at the vacant musicians' chairs. “We have no orchestra.”

“We have danced through worse difficulties.”

“I don't recall any.”

“You were too busy being dignified.”

“I have been accused of that.”

He bowed with exquisite solemnity, took her hand, and led her onto the polished floor. At first their steps were uncertain. Their slippers made a soft uneven rhythm; Rome tried to hum the last waltz and lost his place almost immediately. Daysha laughed, not to rescue the tune but because he kept attempting it with such kingly determination.

Then they found a rhythm that needed no music.

Without an audience, there was no reason to turn at the proper place, no one to impress with a perfect figure, no moment in which to release one another for the next courtly obligation. Rome's fingers rested lightly at her waist. Daysha could see the blue of his eyes as plainly as the moon through the high windows. Her pointed ears warmed when he bent to whisper, “I had forgotten how much I like dancing badly with you.”

“We are not dancing badly.”

“We are certainly not dancing correctly.”

“Then we have improved it.”

They made one slow turn beneath the banners. At its end Daysha looked back toward the table, where two sets of notes lay side by side, unopened. For once neither of them reached for them. This was not a promise to abandon tomorrow, only a choice about the few remaining moments of tonight.

Rome touched his forehead to hers.

“Another dance?” he asked.

Daysha tightened her hand around his.

“Until the moon gets tired of watching.”

And because there was no one left to announce the next event, they began again.
