## The Weight of a Flower

The next morning, the second gown stood beneath the Gilded Spindle's tall windows, dark as a garden just after sunset.

Daysha had expected the blue flowers to catch her eye first. They did. Brilliant cobalt petals climbed across the overskirt, over midnight folds deep enough to swallow the daylight. But it was the fine black lace across her shoulders that made her lift her chin while the elven attendants eased the bodice into place.

“Oh,” she breathed. “You have somehow made shadows look delicate.”

Master Garrick Vellmont smiled over his closed ledger. “That is the sort of compliment which makes our workshop forget to sleep.”

“Then please do not let it,” Daysha said. “I should like the people who made it to enjoy their own evening.”

The elemental seamstress crouched beside the sweeping train. “Your Majesty, I must ask you to walk.”

Daysha took three measured steps. The sapphire sash drew taut. One embroidered flower near her hip turned inward, disappearing beneath a fold.

“Something is wrong,” Daysha said.

“Not with your stride,” the seamstress answered. “With my attempt to make the flowers follow it. I bound the thread to hold its position when the fabric moves. That binding also resists the fabric when you turn.”

Garrick adjusted his spectacles. “Can we loosen the working?”

“We can,” the seamstress said, “but then the petals may lose their arrangement.”

Daysha looked from the bright blue pattern to the woman who had made it. “What would you choose if I were not standing here wearing a crown?”

“Move the weight into the stitching beneath the sash. Keep most of the flowers where an ordinary needle put them. Give the enchantment less to carry.”

“And the train?”

“Let it flow instead of commanding it.”

Daysha turned again, more slowly this time. The long dark skirt followed beautifully; the trapped flower did not. She caught the seamstress's worried glance.

“There,” she said, pointing. “That little one. I would rather it move honestly than shine perfectly.”

The artisan rose with a new expression: not relief, precisely, but the pleasure of being allowed to solve the right problem.

Garrick opened his ledger at last. “An alteration, then. And proper workshop time for it.”

“Yes,” Daysha said. “I came to admire a finished dress. Instead I have learned why it isn't finished yet.”

The seamstress lifted her needle. “Shall we try the turn again when it can keep up with you?”

Daysha smiled, her iridescent blue hair spilling over the black lace, violet eyes bright. “As often as it takes. But I insist you take a break before the next attempt.”

No one wrote down a sale. The seamstress marked the stubborn flower with a temporary thread, and the second fitting ended where good workmanship began: with something still left to do.
