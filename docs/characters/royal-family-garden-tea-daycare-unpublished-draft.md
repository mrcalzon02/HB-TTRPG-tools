The first problem with the garden tea was that the smallest pastries had been made for the wrong people.

They stood in four immaculate rows on a silver tray in the palace kitchen, each no larger than a coin, each crowned with a petal of candied fruit. The pastry cook had made them small because the queen had asked for something that could be eaten between conversations without scattering crumbs over the garden table. The kitchen steward had made the portions smaller still because the royal council had unexpectedly requested the adjoining courtyard for a visiting delegation.

Now the cook regarded the tray as though it had personally insulted him.

“There are enough for the royal table,” he said. “There are not enough for the people who will have to stand around it.”

The steward checked the slate beside the ovens. “The delegation's servants can be fed from the lower kitchen.”

“After they have spent an hour watching everyone else eat?”

“That is the arrangement.”

“It is a poor arrangement.”

A young kitchen runner, who had been counting the pastries without being asked, offered a solution. The cook listened, considered the supply of fruit and pastry left from the morning bake, and sent the runner to fetch a second tray. Nothing about the new tray would be as decorative. There would, however, be enough to share.

The runner had just returned when Queen Daysha appeared at the kitchen door.

She had dressed for the garden in a familiar blue floral ensemble, its soft folds gathered neatly at her waist. Her very long blue-violet hair was braided loosely back from her face, and her pointed ears were visible beneath it. She looked from the silver tray to the plain one, and from the plain one to the cook's expression.

“Have I arrived during a dispute?”

“Only a matter of portions, Your Majesty,” said the steward.

“Portions are very serious matters,” Daysha replied. “Especially when they are this small.”

The cook showed her the second tray. “We are preparing enough for the attendants as well.”

“Good. I would rather have a table where everyone can eat than one where everyone can admire the food.”

The steward opened his mouth, perhaps to explain the protocols for serving a queen, but she had already taken a tiny pastry and was examining its delicate fruit petal.

“Did you make these yourself?”

“With my assistants, Your Majesty.”

“Then please tell them they are beautiful.”

He bowed, but Daysha remained long enough to ask how much time the extra preparation would require. The runner answered before the cook could invent a reassuring figure.

“Long enough to delay our own meal, Your Majesty.”

Daysha put the pastry down again. “Then I shall ask the household to move our tea, not your meal.”

She did not order the cook to abandon his other duties or pretend that royal gratitude could be eaten in place of lunch. She left the kitchen with the tray still unfinished, which was the first useful decision anyone had made about the tea that morning.

***

King Rome was waiting beside the garden doors with two letters in his hand.

One bore the seal of the council. The other, less impressive, was a note from the royal daycare's senior attendant. Both concerned the same strip of garden, though neither writer appeared to know that the other existed.

“The council wishes to receive its visitors beneath the pear tree,” Rome said. “The daycare has reserved the same lawn for the children's outdoor hour.”

“Which request arrived first?” Daysha asked.

“The daycare's. The council says its business is urgent.”

“Is it?”

Rome turned over the letter. “They wish to discuss where they will meet next week.”

Daysha stared at him.

“I know,” he said.

Through the open doors they could see the garden. The pear tree cast a broad patch of shade across the grass. Beyond it, a low gate divided the formal walks from a smaller, enclosed lawn furnished with child-sized benches, watering cans, and a painted wooden horse that had plainly survived more adventures than its maker intended.

Estelle was standing by the gate, speaking to the senior daycare attendant with the determined politeness of a person who had already been refused something once.

The attendant held a bundle of folded cloths beneath one arm. “Your Highness, the little garden is for the children in our care during the outdoor hour. Your parents are welcome to visit, but the other children must be able to play as usual.”

“I am a child,” Estelle pointed out.

“You are.”

“And I am going to daycare.”

“That is what we are discussing.”

Estelle looked toward her parents. “Father, may I go through the gate?”

Rome approached with Daysha beside him. “I think that is a question for the person responsible for the gate.”

The attendant gave him a grateful glance, so brief that only Daysha noticed it.

“Your Majesties,” she said, “we have a full group today. Some of the children have never played with the princess. If she joins us, she must follow the same rules about tools, games, and leaving the grounds.”

“Of course,” Daysha said.

Estelle considered this. “Even if I have to go somewhere important?”

“Especially then,” the attendant replied. “You tell me first, so I know where you are.”

“Very well.”

“And no sending the guards to fetch the best watering can.”

Estelle's eyes widened. “I wasn't going to.”

The attendant raised one eyebrow. Estelle looked away.

Rome coughed into his hand. Daysha found something fascinating about a nearby rosebush.

The attendant was not finished. “The children were promised the pear-tree lawn. We cannot simply put them indoors because someone else wants the shade.”

“You will not have to,” Rome said.

He called for a clerk, wrote a short instruction on the back of the council letter, and sent it to the steward responsible for receiving visitors. The council could use the eastern terrace, where chairs and water would be arranged. The children's reservation would stand.

The clerk read the instruction back. Rome corrected one line before the note left his hand: the terrace staff were to be consulted about preparation time, not told that they must have it ready immediately.

Daysha watched him do it. “A king who can move a meeting without moving the people who were there first.”

“I shall put it among my accomplishments,” he said.

“Under 'diplomacy.'”

“Under 'survival,' perhaps.”

Estelle had already turned back to the attendant. “May I come in now?”

“When your parents have finished saying goodbye.”

Estelle kissed Daysha's cheek, embraced Rome with the unceremonious force of a child who had better things to do, and went through the gate without waiting to see whether anyone watched her.

Rome watched anyway.

***

The garden tea began later than planned, which made it better.

The pear tree remained the daycare's. The royal table was carried to a sunny corner of the courtyard where the roses were just beginning to climb the stone. The kitchen sent both trays, and the cook's assistants were told that they could return to their meal before the last cup was poured.

Daysha noticed the second tray placed on a sideboard, well away from the guests.

“Is that one being kept for the staff?”

“Yes, Your Majesty,” the steward said.

“Then please make sure the staff are actually free to eat it.”

He hesitated. “During service?”

“Not all at once. I should dislike having to pour my own tea.”

Rome gave her a sideways look.

“I could do it,” she added. “But I am told I am dangerously generous with the hot water.”

A footman laughed before he could stop himself. The steward looked scandalized for a heartbeat, then relented enough to arrange a rotation with the attendants. The first footman took his break; another replaced him; the trays began to empty for the people they had been intended to feed.

Across the lawn, Estelle was learning that a royal title did not make a watering can any easier to share.

A boy had taken the green one. A girl had taken the red one. Estelle stood before the remaining yellow can with a look of private disappointment.

The attendant crouched beside her. “Is something wrong?”

“The green one pours better.”

“It does.”

“I wanted it.”

“So did he.”

Estelle watched the boy carefully watering a patch of soil where nothing had been planted. “He is wasting it.”

“He is practicing.”

“That is a great deal of practice.”

“You may ask for a turn when he is finished. Until then, there are seedlings that would be grateful for the yellow one.”

Estelle took the yellow can. She poured too quickly, made a small muddy crater, and looked appalled by the result. The attendant handed her a cloth and showed her how to steady the spout with both hands.

From the courtyard, Rome leaned toward Daysha. “Should we help?”

“Would we help,” she asked, “or would we be rescuing her from a watering can?”

He considered the distinction.

“She has survived worse,” Daysha said. “I believe you once allowed her to assist with a council seating chart.”

“That was an error of judgment.”

“An educational one.”

The boy with the green can eventually approached Estelle. He had seen her carefully refill the yellow one from a barrel without spilling more than a few drops.

“Would you like this?” he asked.

Estelle glanced at the attendant. The attendant did not answer for her.

“Yes, please,” Estelle said. “But you can use the yellow one if you want. It pours gently when you hold it like this.”

The two children exchanged cans. A minute later they were watering the same patch together, arguing amiably about whether a weed deserved as much water as a flower.

Daysha watched them with an expression Rome recognized. It was the look she wore in an atelier when an ordinary piece of cloth revealed workmanship that no gemstone could improve.

“What?” he asked.

“She is learning something we cannot teach by telling her.”

“That she may borrow a watering can?”

“That other people do not cease to have wishes because she has entered the room.”

Rome followed her gaze. “A lesson some ministers could use.”

Daysha smiled. “We shall invite them to daycare.”

***

The council's visitors arrived at the eastern terrace with their papers and their complaints.

One of them, seeing the king at tea, sent a messenger to ask whether the business could be concluded immediately. Rome read the note and asked for the petition itself rather than a summary. The messenger returned with three pages concerning a proposed ceremonial route through the market district.

Rome's smile disappeared as he read.

“Is something wrong?” Daysha asked.

“The route would close two shop streets during the busiest trading hours.”

“Did anyone ask the shopkeepers?”

“The petition says the disruption will be minor.”

“That is not the same thing.”

He looked toward the terrace. “I ought to go.”

“Then go. We can keep your cup warm.”

“I promised Estelle I would see her garden.”

“You already have. You are allowed to keep a promise without standing beside her every moment.”

Rome touched her hand. “And you?”

“I have a meeting with a pastry.”

He laughed, but he left the table. Daysha watched him stop to speak with the garden steward on his way to the terrace. The steward knew which paths carried deliveries and which gates the children used. Rome listened before continuing.

At the tea table, Daysha was left with the quiet and the little pastries. She might have enjoyed both, had the senior daycare attendant not approached with a concern she plainly disliked raising.

“Your Majesty, may I speak about the princess?”

Daysha set down her cup. “Please.”

“The other children are beginning to treat every ordinary disagreement as a matter requiring a royal ruling. When Estelle chooses a game, they think it is an order. When she loses, they are frightened they have offended her.”

Daysha looked through the gate. Estelle was now sitting on a bench, surrounded by three children and a collection of wooden animals. One child was offering her every animal at once.

“That will not do,” Daysha said.

“I agree. But if I correct them in front of the princess, some parents may complain that I have embarrassed their children before the royal family.”

“Then the complaint should come to me, not to you.”

“I would prefer that no complaint be necessary.”

“So would I.” Daysha thought for a moment. “What would you do if Estelle were not the princess?”

“I would ask each child to choose one animal and tell the story together. I would remind them that no one child is the owner of the game.”

“Then do that.”

The attendant did not move. “Your Majesty, with respect, I need to know that I will still have your support when the children repeat it at home.”

“You will,” Daysha said. “And I will say so to their parents myself, if it becomes necessary. But I should rather they hear it from you first, as they hear the rest of your rules.”

The attendant bowed once. It was a working bow, not a performance. She returned to the bench and knelt beside the children.

Daysha stayed where she was. She could have crossed the lawn and told Estelle to be kind. She could have made a fine speech about fairness, and every child would have nodded because a queen was speaking. Instead she allowed the person entrusted with their care to do the work.

The attendant distributed the wooden animals. The girl who had offered all of hers to Estelle was given first choice of a story. Estelle protested when the girl chose a fox instead of a horse.

“Then perhaps,” the attendant said, “the fox may meet a horse later.”

Estelle considered the possibility. “And a dragon?”

“If the story needs one.”

“It does,” Estelle declared.

The other children laughed. Soon everyone was contributing something impossible, and no one seemed especially concerned about who wore a crown.

***

Rome returned to the courtyard with ink on his finger and a different expression from the one he had worn when he left.

“Has the market been closed?” Daysha asked.

“No. The proposed route is going back for consultation. The merchants will be heard before any date is set.”

“Then the kingdom has survived another pastry.”

“I have missed two.”

She offered him the last one from her plate.

He looked at it, then at the empty place beside the tea service. “Where is the kitchen's second tray?”

“Where it was meant to go.”

He glanced toward a footman finishing a break beneath the arch and understood.

The steward arrived to ask whether the royal table should be cleared. Daysha was about to answer when she noticed Estelle at the gate, looking toward the pastries with an unmistakable longing.

“Perhaps a little longer,” Daysha said. “But please ask the daycare attendant whether the children are due a snack. We must not change their routine simply because ours has been delayed.”

The answer came back that the children had their own prepared food, served at a regular time, and that any special treats would need the kitchen's ingredient list checked first. One child could not eat the fruit topping on the royal pastries. Another had been promised something different by a parent. The attendant would not accept a tray merely because it was offered by the queen.

Daysha was delighted.

“At last,” she said, “someone in the palace who is not afraid to tell me no.”

Rome raised his cup. “I know several.”

“They all work in the wardrobe accounts.”

The daycare's snack arrived on its own cart: bread, sliced fruit, and small cups of water. Estelle helped carry the cups from the gate to the benches, under the attendant's eye. She did not carry the hot tea. She did not command the children to sit. She asked where each cup should go and waited for an answer.

Afterward, the attendant allowed the children to come to the garden fence to wave to the royal table before their indoor hour.

Estelle stood at the front with the boy who had shared the green watering can. “Mother! We made a story about a fox and a dragon and a horse who could speak!”

“Who won?” Rome called.

“Nobody,” Estelle said. “They found a place to live together.”

“That is an excellent ending,” Daysha replied.

“No,” Estelle said. “It is the beginning. We have not decided what they will eat.”

Rome laughed. “That is indeed where most governments begin.”

The attendant called the children inside. Estelle lingered for a heartbeat, one hand on the gate.

“May I come again?” she asked her parents.

Daysha glanced at the attendant. “If there is room, and if you are invited.”

Estelle turned at once. “May I?”

“You may,” said the attendant, “provided you remember the rules.”

“I do.”

“Tell me one.”

Estelle looked at the green watering can, now hanging beside the yellow one.

“Everyone gets a turn.”

“And another?”

“If I leave, I tell you.”

“And one more?”

Estelle thought hard. “The fox does not always have to be a horse.”

The attendant smiled. “Close enough for today.”

***

When the courtyard was quiet again, the gardener came to collect the watering cans. He discovered the muddy crater Estelle had made beside the seedlings and knelt to smooth the soil.

Rome saw him from the table. “Has the princess damaged your garden?”

“Nothing that cannot be put right, Your Majesty. A young plant was washed loose, but she helped me set it back.”

“Should we have someone repair the bed?”

The gardener shook his head. “I would rather she see it next time. She should know whether it lived.”

Rome nodded. “Then we shall leave it to your care.”

The gardener returned to his work. Daysha watched him press the soil gently around the roots.

“That,” she said, “may be the finest lesson of the afternoon.”

Rome sat beside her. “The watering can?”

“The plant. You can make a mistake without being forbidden to try again. But you must return and see what your mistake cost.”

He looked toward the daycare gate. “I hope she will remember.”

“She may remember the dragon instead.”

“That would be like her.”

Daysha leaned against his shoulder. The blue floral folds of her dress settled over the edge of the bench. She had not acquired a new gown, or a jewel, or anything that would require Garrick's ledger. Rome had not won a debate by speaking more loudly than the council. Estelle had not been crowned queen of the nursery, and the nursery had been better for it.

The kitchen staff had eaten their lunch. The visitors had found another terrace. The market streets remained open while the merchants were consulted. A caretaker had kept the authority to care for the children in her charge.

From inside the daycare came the faint sound of several voices arguing over what a dragon might eat.

Rome reached for the last pastry and discovered that Daysha had already divided it in two.

“For you,” she said.

“And the other half?”

“For me. I am learning to share.”

He accepted his portion with great ceremony. “A lesson from the royal daycare?”

“A lesson from everyone who kept this afternoon running while we were busy enjoying it.”

They ate beneath the roses as the pear tree cast its shade across an empty patch of grass. Tomorrow, or on some other undetermined day, there would be more letters, more errands, more occasions when a king or queen could mistake the needs of the household for an inconvenience. For the moment, the garden belonged to the people who cared for it, and the family had been welcome there because they had learned to ask.
