## The Place at the Table

The lady-in-waiting had spent the morning telling people she wanted no fuss. By late afternoon, the laundry girls had hung a garland of paper blossoms above the little dining table, and someone had borrowed three mismatched candlesticks.

She recognized the ribbon around the cake knife. It was the one she used to mark the Queen's parcels when the household was busy. It had been washed, pressed, and knotted into a bow.

"Who stole that?" she asked.

"Borrowed," said the laundress. "You can inspect our receipts tomorrow."

Beyond the pantry door, two kitchen hands hovered with a lopsided cake. One was new enough to carry it as though a breath might crack the icing.

"Bring it in," she said.

"They said we should put it down and go back to washing up."

"Who said?"

He looked alarmed. "Nobody in particular."

"Then nobody will miss you for ten minutes."

The table had only eight places. She counted nine people, excluding the two with the cake. The old reflex arrived: measure the room, assign chairs, apologize to the last person. For once, she decided not to apologize.

She carried a bench from the passage herself. The junior boy put down his end of the cake board and helped; when the bench snagged on the sill, she had him lift while she steered, rather than tugging at it until the wood split.

"That's how you move furniture," she told him.

"Is that a birthday lesson?"

"It's the only one I'm qualified to give."

When Queen Daysha arrived in a simple day dress, blue hair loose over her shoulders, the lady-in-waiting bowed by habit.

"No," said Daysha, catching her sleeve lightly. "I'm a guest. Where shall I sit?"

"Wherever there's space. We made some."

Daysha took the end of the bench beside the youngest kitchen hand and asked him which part of the cake had survived the oven. He answered with such solemnity that the room began to laugh.

The laundress unveiled a needle case wrapped in scrap linen. Inside lay two small needles and a thimble, polished but not new.

"You've repaired all our hems," she said. "We thought you might want tools that belong to you."

The lady-in-waiting turned the case over in her hands. One crooked blue stitch held the flap closed. Someone had made it with care, not perfection.

"Then I shall use it for something besides mending other people's sleeves."

She cut the cake into uneven portions, set the largest before the boy who had carried it, and took the next for herself. When a crumb landed on her cuff, she left it there until the singing was done.
