# The King Who Sparkled

The first thing Estelle learned about the council chamber was that it had two kinds of doors. There were the enormous carved doors through which her father entered when he was king, and there was a narrow door above the western stair through which the people who kept the chamber working carried lamps, clean water, fresh paper, and all the other things that made a king look as though he had never needed help in his life. Estelle had been allowed to watch from the upper gallery on several occasions. It was an excellent place for learning which ministers fell asleep with their eyes open. It was also an excellent place for discovering that her father could promise to look at something after council and then remain in council until the thing had lost its entire afternoon.

On this particular day the thing was a little painted wooden boat. Estelle had built it from a block of soft wood, a crooked mast and a piece of blue cloth that had once been a ribbon. She had tested it in a basin and discovered that it would sail beautifully provided nobody expected it to sail in a straight line. Rome had promised to see it before the lamps were lit. The promise had been sincere. So had the apology sent from the council chamber when a discussion of the palace service passage turned into a discussion of the market delivery route, and the delivery route became a question of who was supposed to pay for the stones worn smooth by wagons.

“After the meeting,” the messenger said, with the careful expression of someone who had delivered this sentence too often.

“That is what he said before the meeting.”

“Yes, Your Highness.”

“Is the meeting different now?”

“The subjects have changed.”

“Then perhaps they should stop changing them.”

The messenger did not know how to answer that. Estelle let him escape, though she was quite sure he had not solved anything. She set her boat on the nursery table, looked at its crooked blue sail, and considered the injustice of a room full of grown people who could keep her father all day simply by finding another question to ask. She was not angry with Rome exactly. She knew the difference between a promise forgotten and a promise postponed. What she wanted was for the men and women in that room to remember that there was a person waiting on the other side of their very important doors.

The second thing Estelle had learned about the council chamber was that nobody there ever laughed until the king did. Even when a minister said something absurd, the others would sit very straight, as though laughter were a tax they could not afford. Her mother laughed more freely, although she had the inconvenient habit of seeing a joke before Estelle had finished arranging it. Estelle had once asked Daysha why the ministers looked so grave, and Daysha had replied that many of them were frightened of being wrong in public. Estelle thought this a terrible reason to be solemn. She was wrong in public all the time and generally survived it.

The idea began in the small household workroom where decorations from a recent family amusement had been sorted into baskets. There were ribbons that could be used again, paper flowers that had survived being worn as crowns, and a stoppered jar of magical glitter kept with the supervised festival decorations. Estelle had helped make some of the flowers. She remembered the glitter catching the lamplight in their petals, each silver grain bright enough to make a plain scrap of paper look important. It was the sort of magic used for celebration, not a spell that cleaned up after a celebration. The jar belonged among the things she could use with an adult's help. Estelle remembered the first part of that rule and found the second inconvenient.

Beside the workroom window lay the remains of a game of water balloons, including one unfilled balloon that looked far too small to cause serious trouble. Estelle looked at the jar, then at the balloon, then at the distant council windows. She did not need to enchant anything herself. The glitter was already magical; the water in the basin was only water, and the balloon was only a balloon. That distinction seemed reassuring to her. It ought to have reminded her that a burst balloon would still make everything wet.

A proper surprise, she reasoned, needed three qualities. It must be unexpected, it must be funny, and it must leave everybody able to forgive it. She had no intention of hurting her father. She knew his hair was very long and very silver, and she knew the magical flecks would show beautifully against it. She had seen him endure an entire banquet with a flower tucked behind one pointed ear because Daysha had put it there and he could not bear to remove it. A little water and a scattering of shining grains would be nothing compared with that. Estelle neglected to consider the papers, the polished floor, the people who cleaned it, or the possibility that a minister might laugh for a reason that was not kind. She imagined the sparkle, not the work that would follow it.

She filled the balloon from the basin, added a careful pinch from the jar, and tied the neck with the concentration of a jeweler closing a clasp. It was heavier than she had expected. The magical grains did not rise into a helpful little cloud or arrange themselves for her amusement. They settled into a shining crescent at the bottom, like a tiny captured moon. She turned the balloon in her hands and watched the crescent slide with the water. Then she wrapped it in a folded cloth and placed it in the small basket she ordinarily used to carry her boat and its spare ribbon. The boat stayed behind. This was the first decision of the afternoon that Estelle would later wish to explain differently.

The household attendant assigned to accompany her was waiting at the passage. Estelle asked to watch council from the upper gallery. She had been permitted to do so before, and the attendant knew that the gallery had a stout railing and a guard at the stair. The request itself was ordinary. The basket was not. When the attendant asked what was inside, Estelle answered, “Something for Father.” This was true, which made the answer feel almost virtuous. The attendant asked whether it was another boat. Estelle said, “Not exactly.” She did not open the cloth.

At the gallery stair the guard checked that the chamber was admitting visitors to the upper viewing space. The attendant remained with Estelle. Below them, the council table stretched across a floor polished so thoroughly that the windowlight appeared to lie beneath it. There were papers in ordered piles, a map weighted at its corners, a jug of drinking water, and a narrow aisle left clear for messengers. Rome sat at the head of the table, his silver-white hair caught at the nape and falling down the back of his chair. His pointed ears were visible above the collar of his formal coat. Daysha sat to one side in a familiar blue floral ensemble, her long iridescent blue-violet hair bright even in the chamber's restrained light. Neither of them looked up.

The discussion concerned a passage used by workers carrying supplies between the palace kitchens and the outer court. One minister wished to close it while repairs were made. Another warned that closing it would send heavy carts through a narrower route used by the people bringing food to the market. A third wanted the work postponed until the next accounts were approved. Rome was asking which route the workers themselves had been asked to use. It was a reasonable question, and the answer was taking an unreasonable length of time.

Estelle listened for a while. She liked the part where her father asked questions. He was good at making people explain themselves without raising his voice. But the ministers were beginning to repeat things, and he had not once looked toward the gallery. She put the basket on the bench, moved the folded cloth, and felt the balloon shift beneath her fingers. For one moment she imagined taking it home and showing him the boat instead. That would have been the sensible choice. She was aware of this, which was why she was particularly proud of choosing the other one.

“Your Highness,” the attendant whispered, seeing the balloon at last.

Estelle had already stood. She did not climb the railing or venture onto an exposed beam; the enclosed gallery ran beneath the chamber's decorative rafters, and there was room to throw from behind its wooden balustrade. That made the place safe for a child to stand. It did not make it a safe place from which to throw things at a council table.

“Father!” she called.

Rome looked up immediately. The expression that crossed his face was the one Estelle had been waiting for all afternoon: surprised, affectionate, entirely hers. It lasted just long enough for her to see it before she threw.

The little balloon described a crooked arc. It passed well clear of the nearest lamp, missed the edge of the council map, and struck Rome high on the side of the head. It burst at once. Water scattered across his shoulder and the back of his chair. The magical glitter went with it: silver flecks flashed as they spun through the chamber light, then caught in his long white hair, on his collar, and across one startled cheek. For an instant the king looked as though somebody had shaken a handful of stars over him. Then the wet grains began to settle on wood, cloth, and stone, where the people below would have to deal with them. By the time the first drops reached the table, Estelle's throwing hand was empty. There was no second balloon waiting in it, no trick to repeat. The entire magnificent ambush had taken less time than it took the council clerk to stand.

For a heartbeat nobody moved.

Rome blinked. A bright fleck slid down the bridge of his nose. He lifted one hand to his hair, looked at the glitter shining on his fingers, and then looked up at the gallery. Estelle was grinning so hard that her cheeks hurt. She expected him to laugh. She expected everybody to laugh. The room seemed to be holding its breath while it decided whether it was allowed.

A minister at the far end made a noise like a chair being dragged across stone. It was not a chair. It was the beginning of a laugh, smothered behind a hand. The woman beside him tried to remain stern, failed, and bent over her notes. Someone else coughed into a sleeve and produced a sound no cough had ever made. The chamber erupted. A minister who had spent the morning defending a difficult budget was laughing with his forehead against the table. Another wiped his eyes and attempted to say “Your Majesty” three times without succeeding. Even the clerk, who had managed to rescue the nearest stack of papers from the splash, was smiling in helpless disbelief.

Rome looked as though he could not decide whether he had been attacked or decorated.

Daysha's laughter was the last to begin and the most dangerous to Estelle's confidence. Her mother pressed her lips together, glanced at the wet papers, glanced at Rome's sparkling head, and finally covered her mouth. Her violet eyes were bright. Rome turned toward her.

“You knew about this?”

“No,” Daysha said. “I should like that entered into the record.”

The clerk, still clutching his rescued papers, said, “I can enter it, Your Majesty.”

“Please don't,” Rome replied.

That made the ministers laugh harder. Estelle took a bow from the gallery. It was a very good bow, learned from watching formal guests, and it would have been the perfect ending if the chamber had contained only her family and an audience. But a drop of water struck the corner of a document. The clerk moved it out of danger. A servant hurried to lay cloth over the wet stone. Someone reached for the council map before its ink could run. The laughter changed its shape as people noticed that the room still had work to do.

“Hold,” Rome said, not loudly.

The council obeyed. He was still glittering, which made the command difficult to take seriously, but his voice was steady. He asked the clerk whether any records had been damaged. The clerk inspected the pages nearest the splash and said that two had wet corners but the writing was intact. A runner was sent for clean drying sheets. The map had escaped. A minister moved his chair back from the damp patch and nearly put his heel into it.

“Do not step there,” the servant said before remembering whom she was addressing.

The minister stopped. “Quite right. Thank you.”

Rome looked up again. Estelle had expected a scolding by now. What she saw instead was a look that made her wish she had brought the boat. He was not furious. He was disappointed in a way that did not need a raised voice.

“Estelle,” he said, “come down, please. With your attendant. Slowly.”

The stairs seemed much longer on the way down. The guard opened the gallery door and kept the passage clear. The attendant carried the empty basket. Estelle walked beside her, no longer quite sure where to put her hands. When she reached the chamber, the ministers stood aside. Some were still smiling, and that was almost worse. She had wanted them all to see what she could do. Now she could not avoid being seen.

Rome had risen from his chair. Water darkened one shoulder of his coat, and glitter still caught the light whenever he moved his head. He looked absurd. He also looked unmistakably like her father.

“Were you trying to hurt me?” he asked.

“No.”

“I believe you. Were you trying to make me laugh?”

“Yes.”

“Did you think about the people sitting beneath you?”

Estelle looked at the clerk with the papers, the servant with the cloth, and the minister who had nearly slipped. “Not very much.”

“That is the part we must talk about.”

“I only used a little.”

“You used enough to make other people stop their work.”

She swallowed. “But they laughed.”

“They did. I may laugh myself when I have recovered the dignity of my hair. That does not mean the floor is dry or the clerk's work has become less important.”

Daysha came to stand beside them. She did not rescue Estelle from the conversation. That was another inconvenience of having a mother who understood a joke. She took Estelle's hand, gave it one reassuring squeeze, and said, “Tell your father what you wanted.”

Estelle looked from one parent to the other. The answer had been obvious in the workroom, where the balloon was beautiful and the plan was clever. Here, among the damp papers and the people who had not been invited to play, it felt smaller.

“I wanted him to look at me.”

Rome's expression softened at once. He knelt so that they were level, despite the water on his coat.

“I would always rather look at you than at a dispute about cart wheels,” he said. “But when people bring their problems to this room, they are asking me not to forget them either.”

“You said you would see my boat.”

“I did. I meant it.”

“You kept saying after.”

“I did that too.” He considered this without making an excuse. “I should have sent you a better answer than another after. You deserved to know when I could come.”

Estelle's eyes moved toward the council table. “Could you come now?”

“Not quite yet. The people whose work was interrupted deserve to finish it.”

Estelle heard the scrape of a chair being lifted rather than pushed. The servant who had warned the minister was kneeling at the edge of the wet patch, one hand holding a cloth in place. The clerk had rescued the writing, but he could not put it away until he knew which sheets were damp. A runner stood in the doorway waiting for a clear route through the chairs. Estelle had thought magical glitter might make a splendid mess and then somehow cease to be a mess. But the magic had not dried the papers or lifted a single grain from the floor. Water ran toward the seams between the tiles; silver flecks followed it and stayed there, bright enough to be seen and small enough to be carried farther by every careless footstep. Whatever made the glitter sparkle, it had not made the people responsible for cleaning it disappear.

“Can I help?” she asked.

The servant looked up. She had been about to answer when the attendant from the gallery spoke first. “I should have looked in the basket.”

Estelle felt the remark like a hand closing around her wrist. The attendant had asked. Estelle remembered the question, and remembered enjoying the cleverness of an answer that was true without being useful.

“You did look,” Estelle said. “At me. I told you it was for Father.”

“I should have asked to see it.”

“I knew you might say no.” She glanced toward Rome. “That was why I didn't show you.”

For the first time since the balloon burst, Estelle saw the attendant's face without the gallery's shadow across it. She looked neither angry nor forgiving. She looked worried. Estelle had thought the danger of her prank ended when her father proved unhurt. She had not considered that the person entrusted with bringing her safely to the gallery would have to explain how a hidden balloon had reached the council chamber.

Rome did not offer a quick absolution to either of them. “I will hear what happened,” he said. “I won't decide it from a room full of laughter.”

“The gallery was open for visitors,” the attendant said. “I stayed beside her. When I saw the balloon, I spoke, but she was already standing.”

“And you did ask about the basket,” Estelle put in. “I didn't lie exactly. I made you think it was the boat.”

Daysha tilted her head. Her long blue-violet hair shifted over her shoulder, and her violet eyes remained on Estelle. “You used the answer you knew would let you pass,” she said. “Call it what it was, darling.”

Estelle stared at the empty basket. “I tricked her.”

“That is a better beginning,” Daysha said. She held Estelle's hand just as firmly as before.

The servant had moved the basin away from the nearest chair. She stood, wiping her hands on a cloth that had acquired more glitter than water. “There is a dry rail the princess could wipe,” she said, “but not this floor. I need people to stop walking through it, and I need the second basin before we can finish.” She glanced at the runner. “Someone must bring it from the passage.”

The runner went, taking care not to step through the damp patch. Estelle noticed that while he fetched a basin, he was not carrying messages. The servant was not doing whatever she had been doing before the balloon struck. The attendant was standing in a council chamber answering questions instead of taking Estelle home. Each consequence had a person attached to it.

“May I do the rail?” Estelle asked.

“If you follow her instructions,” Rome said, indicating the servant. “This is her work. She knows what is safe.”

The servant gave Estelle a clean rag and showed her a dry section of the lower chair rail. The attendant stayed beside her; neither was sent into the wet area. Estelle wiped once. The glitter spread into a bright, stubborn smear. The grains still sparkled, though the smear was no prettier for it. She looked at the rag, then at the rail, and tried again with less water.

“Does it always do that?”

“Find somewhere to stay?” the servant asked. “Always.”

“Even when it's magical?”

“The shine doesn't make the cloth move itself, Your Highness.” The servant pointed to the rag in Estelle's hand. “Turn it to a clean side. Otherwise you only put the same grains back.”

Estelle obeyed. A few flecks came away on the cloth; others remained lodged in the carved edge, and she waited while the servant showed her how to reach them without scratching the wood. She looked at her father's shining hair. Rome gave her a rueful little nod. It was almost enough to make her smile, but she was beginning to understand that making a joke last longer did not necessarily make it better.

The second basin arrived. The servant set it down and asked the runner to carry the first away. Estelle offered to take it, then stopped when she saw the water sloshing close to the rim. She waited to be given something she could actually manage. She collected the folded cloth that had concealed the balloon, returned it to the empty basket, and finished the rail under the servant's eye. The small task did not shorten the adults' work by much.

When the clerk had secured the drying sheets, Estelle went to him. “I am sorry I splashed your papers.”

He showed her the two damp corners. “These can be saved. Some older pages would not have been so fortunate. Please don't make me find out which.”

She promised she would not. To the servant she said, “I'm sorry you had to stop your work to clean mine.” The servant accepted that, then returned to the floor without pretending the apology had dried it.

Estelle turned to the attendant. This was harder. “I am sorry I made you carry the basket without knowing what was in it. If Father asks again, please tell him I hid it from you.”

“I will tell him what happened,” the attendant replied. “And I will ask to see your basket next time.”

“I'll show you,” Estelle said. “Even when I don't want to.”

The guard at the gallery stair had come down to keep the passage clear. Estelle apologized to him as well for throwing something from a place he had allowed her to visit. He answered that the gallery was for watching, not throwing, and she could hardly disagree.

Rome had listened without interrupting. “You may be sorry and still be responsible,” he told her. “And the people who helped you today may need to explain their part. You cannot spare them that by speaking for them, but you can tell the truth about yours.”

Estelle nodded. Nobody could undo the balloon, and nobody had to pretend that being the king's daughter made her explanation the only one worth hearing.

Daysha brushed a stray silver fleck from Estelle's sleeve. “You wanted your father to notice you,” she said quietly. “He does. Now notice the people who made it possible for you to stand up there at all.”

Estelle looked toward the narrow service door above the stair, then back at the servant wringing another cloth. She had known that door existed. She had never thought much about where its people went when a princess turned their afternoon upside down.

One of the ministers, a broad-shouldered man who had laughed so hard he had lost his place in the accounts, approached Rome with an expression of solemn concern.

“Your Majesty,” he said, “may I recommend that we defer discussion of the silver reserves until you have removed the silver from your person?”

Rome regarded him. Daysha turned away, shoulders shaking.

“That,” Rome said, “is a remarkably poor joke.”

“Yes, Sire.”

“I'm afraid it is also the best one we have heard all day.”

The laughter that followed was quieter, and this time it did not prevent anyone from working. Rome asked the clerk to note which papers had been checked, then asked the council to resume the interrupted question of the service passage. A messenger took the drying sheets to a safe table. The wet floor remained cordoned off until the servants declared it sound. The meeting did not end because a princess had made an entrance. Neither did it continue as though the people who cleaned up after her were invisible.

Estelle waited beside Daysha at the side of the room while the ministers finished their point. She discovered that the question of cart wheels was not as foolish as she had thought. The palace passage was narrow because it had been built for people carrying baskets, not for wagons. Closing it would send kitchen loads along a route used by other workers, and those workers had their own deliveries to make. Nobody at the table could make the problem disappear with a joke. Her father listened to the explanation, asked for the people who actually used the passage to be consulted, and left the final arrangement open until their account could be heard. Estelle was surprised to find herself pleased that he had not simply told everyone to do as he wished.

When a natural break finally came, Rome walked over to her. The damp patch on his coat had begun to dry. The glitter had not.

“Now,” he said, “tell me about the boat.”

“You haven't seen it.”

“I know. I would like to.”

“It sails sideways.”

“Does it?”

“Only when it wants to. I made the mast crooked by accident, but I think it is a better boat that way.”

“Then we shall have to test whether it agrees.”

Estelle looked toward the doors, where the attendant was waiting with the empty basket. “Will you really come?”

Rome glanced at the council table. He did not promise an hour he could not control. Instead he asked the clerk how long the next scheduled item required, listened to the answer, and arranged to take a short family interval before the remaining business. It was a modest promise, made where everyone could hear it and where the people who depended on his time could plan around it.

“I will come when this item is finished,” he said. “If it takes longer than expected, I will send word myself.”

Estelle nodded. “And I will not throw anything else.”

“That would be appreciated.”

“Even if it is very funny.”

“Especially then.”

Daysha bent to kiss the top of her daughter's head. She had made no effort to hide her amusement, but she had not allowed it to cancel the apologies either. Estelle leaned into her for a moment, then went with the attendant to fetch the boat. She had been given a job, a consequence, and a promise. None was as spectacular as a bursting glitter balloon. All three seemed, unexpectedly, worth keeping.

The ministers returned to their papers. Rome returned to his chair. The clerk placed the rescued pages in a fresh stack, the servants lifted the last damp cloth, and the council resumed its argument about a passage that would still need repairing tomorrow. The room had become itself again, with one exception. Whenever Rome inclined his head toward a speaker, a little constellation of silver light moved through his hair.

He did not ask Daysha to remove it. Not yet. Whatever might eventually become of the glitter in his hair, the chamber floor had required real hands, real cloths, and the time of people who had never agreed to be part of the joke.

Across the table, the broad-shouldered minister began to smile. Rome raised one glittering eyebrow, and the minister straightened immediately. For the rest of the sitting, no one could accuse the council of having forgotten that the king was also a father.


### The little boat

The boat was still on the nursery table when Estelle returned. Its blue sail had folded against the crooked mast, and the basin in which she had tested it had been put away. She set the empty basket beside the table and reached for the boat, but the attendant remained in the doorway rather than following her inside.

“Will you be asked about the gallery?” Estelle said.

“I expect so, Your Highness.”

“Will you tell them I tricked you?”

“I will tell them what I saw and what you told me. That is different from asking you to tell my story for me.”

Estelle turned the little boat in her hands. “I thought if I said it first, nobody would blame you.”

“That was kind to think of afterward. It would have been kinder to let me know what was in the basket beforehand.”

The attendant spoke without sharpness, which made the answer harder to escape. Estelle had imagined that a princess could fix the trouble by saying the right thing loudly enough. The attendant had her own account to give, and the people responsible for hearing it would have to listen.

“May I bring the boat?” Estelle asked.

“Yes. That is what the basket was meant for.”

This time Estelle lifted the cloth so the attendant could see exactly what she was carrying. The attendant looked at the crooked sail and smiled a little.

“I hope it floats.”

“It does,” Estelle said. “It only has difficulty deciding where to go.”

They went to the small family sitting room near the council passage. Estelle wanted to set a basin on the table and fill it at once, but the attendant pointed out that the table held books and letters. They chose a low stand near the window instead, with a cloth beneath the basin. Estelle fetched the water herself in two small trips, carrying no more than she could manage without spilling it. It was much less exciting than preparing a balloon. It was also rather satisfying to arrive with the water still inside the jug.

Rome did not appear at the first sound of footsteps in the corridor. Estelle counted the windowpanes, then the blue stitches along the edge of the cloth. She wondered whether the council had found a third route for the carts or another reason to argue about the first two. She was beginning to understand that the people who used those routes had not yet been heard. Even if her father came to see the boat, the passage would still need a decision made with their knowledge.

A messenger arrived before Rome did.

“His Majesty says the present item is taking longer than expected,” he told her. “He has sent word as he promised. He will come at the next break he can keep.”

Estelle was disappointed. She was also surprised to discover that the message made waiting easier. Her father had not forgotten her, and he had not pretended the council was finished. She thanked the messenger and left the boat dry on the cloth.

When Rome finally entered, he still carried a few glittering flecks in his silver hair. He had changed neither his coat nor his expression of tired amusement.

“Have I missed the voyage?” he asked.

“No. I waited.”

“Then I owe you a demonstration.”

“You owe me a look,” Estelle corrected him. “The demonstration is mine.”

She set the boat in the basin. It drifted forward, turned with great dignity, and made for the side as though the window were a harbor. Rome crouched beside her and watched it strike the basin with a quiet tap.

“A determined captain,” he said.

“A crooked mast,” Estelle replied. “I can straighten it, but I wanted you to see what I made before I changed it.”

Rome looked at the sail, the little hull, and finally at her. “I am glad you did.”

She considered the boat for a moment. “I could have brought this to the gallery.”

“You could have asked.”

“You might have said no.”

“I might. But then we would have known what we were deciding.”

Estelle touched the edge of the basin and watched the ripples turn her boat toward open water again. Her father had kept his promise, though later than either of them had wanted. The attendant had not been required to accept Estelle's account in place of her own. And somewhere beyond the sitting-room door, the people who carried the palace's food and supplies still had a passage to explain to the council. None of those things had been settled by the glitter.

“Tomorrow,” she said, “I shall make a straighter mast.”

Rome smiled. “And if the boat still sails sideways?”

“Then I shall ask someone who knows boats.”

“That,” said the king, “sounds like a very good beginning.”
