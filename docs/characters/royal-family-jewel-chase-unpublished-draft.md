# The Jewel That Wasn't Missing

The trouble began with a box small enough to hide beneath a folded napkin.

Daysha noticed it because the palace servant carrying it had chosen the longest possible route around the morning gallery, walking with the grave concentration of a person transporting something that could not afford to be dropped. He wore the ordinary blue-and-silver household livery, but his hands were clasped around a little velvet case, and whenever another servant passed he turned his shoulder protectively toward the wall.

A queen who had spent enough time in the company of dressmakers knew that posture.

She had seen it when Garrick Vellmont's apprentices carried a newly cut stone across a crowded atelier. She had seen it when an embroiderer brought out a length of enchanted silk that cost more than its own weight in ordinary cloth. She had, on one unforgettable occasion, seen Rome adopt the very same expression while carrying a birthday surprise up the stairs.

“Interesting,” she said.

King Rome, who was trying to read a council memorandum while walking beside her, made the mistake of answering without looking up.

“No.”

“I haven't asked anything.”

“That's why I answered early.”

Daysha smiled. Her iridescent blue-violet hair slipped over one shoulder as she turned to watch the servant disappear around a column. “You don't even know what I was going to say.”

“I know the voice you use when you have discovered an object of considerable expense.”

“I was going to say that someone appears to be taking great care with a jewel case.”

Rome lowered the memorandum. His silver-white hair, usually arranged with such kingly composure, had escaped its ribbon in the morning breeze. “That is not better.”

Their daughter had been following them at a decorous distance of three steps, which was the precise distance at which she could hear every interesting adult conversation while pretending to study the colored tiles. Estelle's lighter, silvery-blue hair caught the light when she looked up.

“A jewel case?” she asked.

“A possible jewel case,” Rome corrected.

“Which is an entirely different kind of case,” Daysha said solemnly. “One must investigate before making accusations.”

Estelle considered this and nodded with the seriousness of a magistrate. “We should investigate.”

“We should allow the household to do its work,” said Rome.

The servant reached the far door, glanced at the little case, and hurried through.

Daysha gathered the edge of her blue floral skirts in one hand. “He seems to be in a hurry.”

Rome saw the expression on her face change from curiosity to purpose. “Daysha.”

“I'm merely going to ask.”

“You are moving much faster than asking usually requires.”

Estelle was already running.

For a heartbeat the king remained still, holding a memorandum that suddenly seemed to concern a very distant kingdom. Then he folded it and followed, because the alternative was to explain to the household why his queen and daughter had vanished in pursuit of a box.

***

The first corridor was easy. The second was full of inconvenient people.

A footman stepped aside with a tray of polished cups. Two gardeners, carrying baskets of cuttings, pressed themselves against a wall. The servant with the case heard footsteps behind him and looked back, startled to find the queen approaching at a speed not normally associated with state occasions.

“Your Majesty!”

“Good morning!” Daysha called. “A word about your delivery?”

He looked toward the door ahead, then down at the case. “I beg your pardon, Your Majesty, but I have instructions to take this directly—”

“Directly where?”

“I—well—”

Estelle darted past a marble pedestal, stopped just short of colliding with a chambermaid, and offered an impeccable little bow.

“Sorry,” she said, and continued.

The chambermaid's expression followed her down the passage. “I suppose,” she remarked to nobody in particular, “that is one way to practice court etiquette.”

Daysha caught the comment and laughed, but her laughter faded when she saw the servant's distress. He was not a conspirator enjoying a game. He was a young employee trying to obey an order in a palace where the royal family had suddenly become an obstacle.

“Wait,” she said, slowing. “You are not in trouble.”

That should have settled matters. Instead, the servant mistook her concern for a new urgency and quickened his pace.

Rome arrived in time to hear the hurried retreat of footsteps.

“Did you tell him he was not in trouble?”

“Yes.”

“Did you tell him to stop?”

“Not in those exact words.”

Rome shut his eyes briefly. “Ah.”

Estelle, farther ahead, turned at the junction and called back, “He went toward Grandmother's rooms!”

Daysha looked at Rome. Rome looked at Daysha.

There was a pause in which both of them considered the same possibility and neither wished to admit it first.

“Your mother,” Daysha said at last, “has excellent taste in jewels.”

“My mother,” Rome replied, “also has an excellent sense of privacy.”

“Then we had better catch up before we disturb it.”

“That reasoning,” he said, beginning to walk again, “has several missing steps.”

***

At the turning stair, the chase ceased to be amusing for anyone who worked there.

A messenger carrying sealed household orders flattened himself against the rail. A pair of laundresses paused with a basket between them. Estelle reached the landing, saw the servant stop at a locked side door, and stopped too. She had enough breath left to be delighted and not enough to conceal it.

“We found you!”

The servant looked as though he might drop the case from sheer relief.

“I wasn't hiding, Your Highness.”

“Then why were you running?”

“Because you were chasing me.”

Estelle blinked. The logic was inconveniently complete.

Daysha came up the last steps more slowly. Her gown brushed the stone; its familiar blue floral folds were a poor choice for pursuit, but she had not intended to be pursuing anyone when she put it on. Rome arrived beside her, breathing evenly and looking tired in a way that had nothing to do with stairs.

“Everyone,” he said, “is going to stand still for a moment.”

It was a remarkably effective royal command. Even the laundresses, who had been planning to retreat, remained where they were.

Rome turned to the servant. “What is your name?”

The young elf gave it, along with the name of the steward who had assigned him the errand. Rome repeated both carefully, so that the answer was heard and not merely extracted.

“You have done nothing wrong,” the king said. “You were given a delivery. We interrupted it. You may tell the steward precisely that.”

The servant's shoulders dropped.

Daysha stepped forward. “I owe you an apology. I saw the case and let curiosity turn into a chase. I did not think about how it would feel to be the one carrying it.”

“Your Majesty, you needn't—”

“I do,” she said. “If a queen can set half a corridor running after an innocent servant, she can say she was mistaken.”

Estelle studied the servant's face. “I made you run faster.”

“You did, Your Highness.”

“I'm sorry.”

He smiled then, not because the incident had become harmless, but because someone had finally allowed it to end.

Rome gestured toward the locked door. “And the destination?”

The servant hesitated. “The Queen Mother's private receiving room. It is an item she requested be brought up before her morning visitors.”

Daysha's eyes widened. “Oh.”

“Ah,” said Rome, with the weary satisfaction of a man whose prediction had finally been confirmed.

The door opened from within.

Rome's mother stood on the threshold in blue-and-gold, her silver-white hair swept into a careful arrangement beneath a jeweled crown. Her blue-violet eyes moved from the servant to Estelle, from Estelle to Daysha, and finally to her son.

“I was expecting a delivery,” she said. “I was not expecting a procession.”

“Mother,” Rome began.

“Have you been racing through the palace?”

“Not I.”

“Your daughter?”

“She has inherited a lively interest in household affairs.”

“And my daughter-in-law?”

Daysha folded her hands with great dignity. “A regrettably lively interest in velvet cases.”

The Queen Mother looked at the case. Then she laughed, one short, bright sound that echoed down the stairwell.

“Come inside, all of you, before someone concludes that the treasury has been robbed.”

***

The receiving room smelled of polished wood and fresh flowers. A narrow table stood beside the window, laid with tea that had been waiting long enough for the surface to cool.

The servant set the case down before the Queen Mother. He did not open it until she nodded. Inside lay a carefully arranged collection of stones and settings, glittering against dark velvet. They were beautiful, but they were not Daysha's, and the simple fact of their destination changed the entire adventure.

The Queen Mother leaned closer, examining the work without touching it.

“An alteration,” she explained. “A piece I asked to have brought for inspection. Nothing has been purchased this morning, and nothing is being given away. I wished to see whether the setting was secure.”

Rome glanced toward Daysha. She had the grace to look embarrassed.

Estelle, however, was fascinated. “How do you tell?”

“By asking someone who knows how it was made, and by not shaking it to find out,” her grandmother said.

Estelle drew her hands behind her back at once.

The Queen Mother noticed. “An excellent beginning.”

The servant, now standing near the door, appeared uncertain whether he was free to leave. Daysha turned toward him.

“Please finish your delivery properly. And ask your steward to note that the delay was mine, not yours.”

Rome added, “I'll send the message myself.”

This was not a grand act of royal generosity. It was a small repair to a small harm, which mattered because the servant would otherwise have carried the consequences of their entertainment into the rest of his working day.

The Queen Mother inspected the clasp and the stone seats, asked two practical questions, and directed that the case remain on her table until the appropriate craftsperson could examine a loosened fastening. No one declared a sale, and no one put a price on the contents.

Daysha watched her with genuine admiration.

“You noticed the fastening before the jewels.”

“My dear,” the Queen Mother said, “the jewels are only as safe as the hands that set them.”

Rome gave a faint smile. “I wish that principle applied to all of our household purchases.”

“It does,” Daysha said. “It is why I admire the seamstresses.”

“And why Garrick keeps such meticulous ledgers,” Rome replied.

His mother looked between them. “I see the dressmaker's accounts are still a topic of family conversation.”

“Only when your son starts the conversation,” Daysha said.

Estelle had been listening intently, piecing together the parts of the incident that adults had considered self-evident. She came to the Queen Mother's chair and rested a hand on the arm.

“Grandmother, if the jewels are yours, why did we think they were Mother's?”

The Queen Mother looked to Daysha rather than answering for her.

Daysha knelt so their eyes were level. “Because I wanted to know what was in the box, and I decided I already knew. I was wrong.”

Estelle thought about that.

“Then we should ask first.”

“Yes.”

“And run only if someone says it's a race.”

“An excellent rule,” Rome said.

Estelle looked at him with sudden interest. “Would you race?”

“No.”

Daysha raised an eyebrow. “Not even if we gave you a head start?”

“No.”

“Not even,” his mother asked, “if the prize were the return of your unread memorandum?”

Rome considered this with a seriousness that made the women laugh.

“That,” he said, “would depend on how many corridors were involved.”

***

The Queen Mother kept the family for tea.

Estelle was given the important duty of choosing which small pastry each person should receive. She distributed them with elaborate fairness: one for Grandmother, one for Mother, one for Father, and one for herself. Then she noticed the servant, who had remained to confirm the delivery had been accepted.

“There should be one for him.”

The Queen Mother signaled the attendant to bring another plate. The young elf accepted it with a blush and a bow, and was finally dismissed with the thanks he should have received at the beginning.

Rome watched the door close.

“You have accomplished something remarkable this morning,” he told Daysha.

“I've discovered the importance of secure jewelry settings?”

“You have turned a private delivery into a lesson in palace procedure.”

“That's two lessons,” Estelle corrected. “Ask first. And don't chase servants.”

“Three,” said the Queen Mother. “Never let a king pretend he is too dignified to run.”

Rome put down his cup.

“Mother.”

“Son.”

Daysha was laughing too hard to speak. She reached for Rome's hand beneath the edge of the table, and he took it, his thumb resting against hers. The moment was small enough that no steward would record it and no painter would be summoned to preserve it.

Estelle saw it anyway.

She slipped her own hand between theirs, joining the two, and announced, “Next time, we all walk together.”

Rome leaned down to kiss the top of her head.

“Agreed.”

Daysha squeezed both their hands. Beyond the window the palace gardens stirred in a light breeze, and somewhere below them the interrupted work of the household resumed. The servant would finish his round. The steward would receive the king's explanation. A craftsperson would inspect the Queen Mother's fastening. The queen would have to learn to leave a velvet case unopened.

And for the rest of the morning, at least, the palace could enjoy the rarest of royal luxuries: nothing more expensive than a pastry, nothing more urgent than a child's question, and no reason whatsoever to run.
