# Matron Lagas Atrovald

## Continuation VI — Two Chains of Custody

The escort commander's office answered Atrovald's request with an apology.

It was written in excellent calligraphy, which made the apology no more useful.

The officer's adjutant regretted that the duty roster was under temporary seal while the custody balance was being reconciled. He offered to send a certified numerical abstract in its place.

Arthus Pell read the letter twice.

"Fifty-three at dispatch," he said. "Fifty-two at return. Shall I write back and tell him we can already count?"

"Ask whether his commander is available for interview."

"He's on the southern transfer circuit."

"Then ask when he left, who signed his travel order, and who holds his authority in his absence."

Pell began a fresh sheet.

"Three questions?"

"Three answers, if fortune has become generous."

A second reply had arrived from the reconciliation office. The officer who submitted the adjustment from fifty-three to fifty-two could not be named because the surviving acceptance record carried a departmental seal. The office offered to certify the seal's validity.

"They will certify the seal until the man beneath it is forgotten," Pell said.

"That would be the efficient outcome."

Atrovald set the two letters side by side. Each office was entitled to withhold something from the other. The escort commander had operational authority over the men he dispatched. Reconciliation had authority over the count that entered the custody balance. Neither could create the other office's missing signature.

"Do not combine these requests," she told Pell. "The commander may have sent fifty-three positions without knowing how the return was reduced. The reconciler may have accepted fifty-two without knowing who occupied any of them."

"And if they conspired?"

"Then we shall need evidence of a conspiracy. Two unsatisfactory letters do not constitute one."

The quartermaster's missed-shift allowance lay beneath the replies, still awaiting Personnel's calculation. Atrovald signed a separate demand for a payment date. The woman had returned to issuing gloves. That did not restore the money she had lost.

---

The escort office surrendered its duty assignment on paper after Pell located the regulation that distinguished a roster of personnel from a roster of authorized positions.

The distinction mattered. The document bore fifty-three numbered places, but the names had been entered on a detachable attendance sheet. That sheet was missing from the copy the adjutant had supplied.

Atrovald had Pell bring the adjutant to the record room. She did not summon him as a prisoner. He arrived with his own clerk and an evident determination not to become one.

"Who prepared the numbered roster?" she asked.

"The escort commander's staff."

"Who signed the dispatch?"

"The commander."

"Who attached the names?"

"His duty clerk, under delegated authority."

"Where is that sheet?"

The adjutant folded his hands.

"The sealed original is in the commander's traveling chest."

"Has the chest left the spire?"

"I don't know."

"Then that is what we will establish."

He offered to send a message to the commander. Atrovald accepted. She also requested the packing register, the chest's seal impression, and the name of the custodian who had released it. The adjutant seemed disappointed that she had not accused him of hiding a man.

Pell examined the surviving roster.

The first fifty-two positions were grouped into watches, each with a duty designation. The fifty-third was marked **RELIEF AUXILIAR — CUSTODY TRANSFER**, with no name on the face of the form.

"Could it have been left vacant?" he asked.

"Yes," said the adjutant. "We often authorize a spare position."

"Then why issue a numbered chit for it?"

"Because the chit book is prepared from the authorization, not the attendance."

Atrovald looked at him.

"Was it the commander's practice to issue unused chits?"

"Sometimes. They are supposed to be returned unused."

"Would an unused chit bear a return exception stamp?"

"It might, if someone discovered it outside the unused bundle."

"Then we have an ordinary explanation to test."

The adjutant's expression eased. He had expected the Matron to demand a confession about a ghost.

"An ordinary explanation," she repeated, "not an established one."

She asked him to identify the clerk who had actually detached the fifty-third chit. The adjutant could name the office, not the hand.

Pell wrote down the distinction. It was becoming a habit of his own.

---

The reconciliation office had a different difficulty.

Its chief would allow Atrovald to inspect the departmental seal, but not the personal authorization slips. Those were protected because a change in custody strength could affect discipline, ration entitlements, and an officer's liability for a lost prisoner.

Atrovald did not dispute the rule. She asked for the rule's exception procedure.

The chief produced it reluctantly.

A disputed count could be reviewed by two custodians, one from each affected jurisdiction, without opening unrelated personnel cases. Both had to record what they inspected and why. The office could withhold the identities of witnesses from the other office until the review was complete, but not from the two appointed reviewers.

"Why was that not offered in your reply?" Atrovald asked.

"Because no one requested it."

"Your reply was to a request for the authorizing officer."

"Which is not the same request."

"No," she said. "It is not."

Pell, who had been preparing an indignant answer, put his pen down.

The chief selected a custody auditor who had not served on the disputed watch. Atrovald appointed Pell, whose recollection of a fifty-third guard made him an interested witness rather than an independent observer. She said so in the appointment. The chief objected.

"Then he cannot review it."

"He can inspect and record what he sees. Your auditor will separately certify whether the evidence supports his account. If they disagree, the disagreement survives. I will not disguise his interest by assigning a stranger who cannot recognize the documents."

They worked at two desks facing one another. Neither could see the other's notes until both signed.

The original adjustment request had been made on a reusable office form. Its lower strip was missing, torn away at a perforation. The upper half bore the departmental seal and the figure fifty-two. Beside the seal was a short code for a night-watch reconciliation desk.

Pell recognized the code from the inquiry closure, but he did not announce that as a discovery. The code identified a desk. Several officers had worked there.

The independent auditor examined the impression and found the same result.

"Do you have the lower strips?" Pell asked.

"In the watch packets."

"All of them?"

"Those retained under the ordinary schedule."

The chief checked the retention book. That night's packet had been sent for destruction after the standard interval, then held by a routine litigation notice concerning a different transfer. It was still in sealed storage.

Atrovald resisted the urge to call that providence. The archive contained millions of pieces of paper preserved for reasons no longer remembered. One of them had happened to survive.

She ordered the packet brought under dual custody. She did not order it opened.

---

They opened the packet at the next watch, in front of both reviewers and a clerk who recorded each broken seal.

The missing lower strip was there.

Its torn edge fitted the upper form. The paper stock matched. The figure fifty-two had been copied onto it by the same pen that had made the adjustment.

The personal authorization box contained a mark.

Pell leaned forward.

It was not a name.

It was the small hooked stroke used by the night-watch duty clerk when he acknowledged receipt of a request that still required an officer's approval.

The auditor compared it with three ordinary samples. All four marks were consistent. The duty clerk had received the adjustment. The strip did not show who ordered it.

"The request was accepted without a completed personal authorization," Pell said.

"Yes," the auditor replied.

"Then the count should not have changed."

"Under the current rule, no."

"Under the rule then in force?"

The auditor turned to the dated instruction.

Atrovald watched him read. She could see him deciding whether to defend his office.

"Under the rule then in force," he said, "the department seal was sufficient for a routine balance correction. The personal sign was required only for an exceptional custody loss."

"And who classified this as routine?"

"That is not recorded."

There it was: a perfectly functioning procedure that could close a missing-person inquiry without a person ever declaring the loss exceptional.

Atrovald asked the clerk to read the finding back before anyone signed. The clerk's voice was thin.

The auditor signed. Pell signed his own account, which explicitly identified his prior memory of an additional guard. They agreed about the paper. They did not agree about whether a man had disappeared.

"Can we now suspend the night-watch clerk?" the chief asked.

"Why?"

"He received an incomplete form."

"He received a form accepted by the rules you gave him. Interview him. Preserve his statement. Do not punish him for a policy your office maintained."

Pell looked up from the strip.

"Will we change the rule?"

"We have already ordered human review for missing-person and physical-custody disputes. Now we have a case demonstrating why. Let the two offices sign the amendment before the next watch. The closure machine need not be destroyed to prevent it from swallowing another complaint."

The chief took the order. He did not look pleased. He had a real backlog, and every exception required someone to read a file.

Atrovald did not pretend that reading was free.

---

The night-watch duty clerk was named Halden. He had been on leave for two days and returned expecting an accusation.

Atrovald gave him a chair.

He sat on its edge.

"I received the count correction," he said after seeing the strip. "I can't tell you who brought it."

"Why not?"

"Because I received twelve that watch. The seal was valid. I copied the number and put the request in the balance queue."

"Did anyone mention a missing guard?"

"Not to me."

"Did you hear the name Hesk Venn?"

"No."

"Did you see fifty-three men?"

"I never saw the escort. I work the count desk."

Atrovald asked whether he remembered the temperature in the transit lift. He did not. His desk was two floors away. She did not lead him toward a memory he had no reason to possess.

Halden watched her pen.

"Am I being disciplined?"

"Not for doing what the instruction required."

"But you are changing the instruction."

"Yes."

"Then someone will say I should have known better."

"Someone may. The record will say what you were authorized to do."

He stared at her for a moment.

"My mother works in the outer ration office," he said. "They corrected a household count last year. A woman lost her widow's allowance for two months because her husband was recorded alive in another district. The figures matched. They said there was no discrepancy."

Atrovald did not ask him for the woman's name. The case had its own office and its own appeal. She asked only whether the ration office had corrected the error.

"Eventually."

"Did they repay her?"

"I don't know."

"Then we do not write that they did."

He seemed to expect a grander response. She had none.

When he left, Pell remained by the desk.

"He wasn't the man."

"We have not established who the man was."

"He wasn't the officer who ordered the reduction."

"Correct."

Pell stared at the two pieces of the form. His own memory of a scarred man complaining about cold had not grown any clearer. The new evidence made the administrative failure more intelligible. It did not make the missing place less disturbing.

"Do you still believe you saw Venn?" Atrovald asked.

"I believe I saw a guard. I remember a scar. I remember the cold."

"And the name?"

"I learned that from the personnel sheet afterward."

"Keep those apart."

"I am trying."

For once, she did not tell him he was improving. He knew.

---

Sorn arrived after evening watch with the Mortuary-Seventeen concordance.

The grey ceramic container remained on its original rack, unopened. Its lead seal had not been disturbed. The shelf report that had returned after vanishing was still filed beside the physical concordance. Sorn had not added the hidden inscription to the ordinary index.

"The Logos observer asks why the container has not been transferred," she said.

"Because no competent authority has ordered a transfer."

"He says the consultation agreement gives him inspection rights."

"It gives him consultation rights. We will answer in writing."

Sorn waited.

"Is that all?" Atrovald asked.

"I wanted to know whether you found the guard."

"No."

"Then I will not let the shelf report be used to imply we did."

Atrovald studied her. Sorn had never met Hesk Venn, and the box in Mortuary-Seventeen was not evidence that he existed. Yet both inquiries had begun to attract people who preferred a satisfying account to an accurate one.

"Good," she said.

Sorn left with the written reply.

Atrovald made one visit to Null-Seven before retiring. The custody clerk recorded the grille opening. Predulum sat on the edge of his bed. Witness Four remained on the desk under its seal.

"Matron," he said. "Have you found the man I failed to see?"

"No."

"Have you decided he was never there?"

"No."

He seemed disappointed by the symmetry.

"Then why visit?"

"To confirm you are being fed and that no one has altered the custody order."

"How tender."

"You may complain in writing."

"I have."

"I know."

He leaned closer.

"Did you find anything about tomorrow?"

Atrovald thought of the unsigned return instruction, still sealed with the two clock readings. She did not tell him about the dispatch counterfoil or the split adjustment form. His recollections were not to be repaired with facts he had never witnessed.

"Nothing I can authenticate as an event that has occurred," she said.

Predulum was quiet.

"Will you erase me again?"

"I have issued no such order."

"You said that before."

"Perhaps I did."

He looked at her sharply. She had not conceded that his memories were accurate. She had conceded that her own records of their encounters were incomplete.

The grille closed. The clerk wrote down the time.

---

The escort commander's office finally sent the packing register.

The traveling chest had been sealed and released to a messenger on the previous watch. The messenger had carried it only as far as the southern transit junction, where a replacement courier signed for it. The commander was still away. No attendance sheet had been produced.

Pell brought the register to Atrovald as she was signing the quartermaster's back-pay order. Personnel had agreed to restore the missed shift allowance, but the payment would not clear until the next accounting cycle. The quartermaster remained at work and had received the written decision. She had not received the money.

"Two couriers," Pell said. "One chest. The seal at the junction is entered, but the second courier's name is written over a correction."

"Then preserve the uncorrected line."

"The junction clerk says the first name was copied from the wrong shift."

"That may be true."

"Shall I bring the clerk?"

"Bring the original book first. Then ask the courier who handed over the chest, not the courier who supposedly received it. They may have seen different things."

Pell hesitated.

"Should we send wardens to seize the chest?"

"From a commander on official transfer duty, on the strength of a corrected name?"

"No."

"Then we agree."

He looked at the papers in her hands: the counterfoil recording fifty-three positions, the matched strips showing how the return count became fifty-two, and the commander's roster whose names had been removed to a chest no one had yet inspected.

"Someone made the missing-person report disappear," he said.

"An established procedure made the report close. Someone submitted the count. We do not know whether those are the same person, or whether either knew a man was missing."

"All this work, and still no Hesk Venn."

Atrovald laid the pay order on top of the packet.

"The quartermaster has her work back. Halden will not be punished for obeying an instruction. The next missing-person inquiry cannot be closed by arithmetic alone. Those are not substitutes for finding Venn."

"No."

"They are what we have done while looking."

Outside, the old ventilation shook the paper in the window frame. The archive continued to keep its contradictory records: fifty-three at dispatch, fifty-two at return, a sealed future instruction, a container not opened, a prisoner who claimed to remember tomorrow.

Atrovald wrote the next two requests on separate sheets.

One followed the chest.

The other followed the officer who had authority to sign the count.

Neither required the archive to invent a man in order to search for him.
