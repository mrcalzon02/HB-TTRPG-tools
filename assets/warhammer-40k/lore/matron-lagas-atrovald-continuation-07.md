# Matron Lagas Atrovald

## Continuation VII — The Name Beneath the Correction

The southern transit junction had corrected its courier's name twice.

The first correction was made in black ink. The second was made by attaching a narrow strip of paper over the first, then stamping the strip with the junction's official seal. The clerk who brought the register to Atrovald appeared to consider this an admirable improvement.

"It is legible now, Matron."

"That is not the question I asked."

"No, Matron."

"Can you show me what was written before it became legible?"

The clerk placed the book on a felt rest. Arthus Pell leaned over it with the expression of a man who had spent a great deal of his life being reassured by people who had misplaced the thing they were reassuring him about.

The original line was still visible at the edges of the strip. It recorded a traveling chest under the escort commander's seal, received from a first courier at the southern junction. The space for the next custodian held an abbreviated name. Someone had later crossed it through, then covered the crossing with the strip.

"Who made the first entry?" Atrovald asked.

"Day watch."

"And the correction?"

"Evening watch."

"Who applied the strip?"

"The same evening clerk, on instruction from the route office."

"Was the instruction written?"

"It should be in the watch packet."

Atrovald had heard that phrase before. It could mean the packet existed. It could mean a packet had once existed. It could mean that someone had discovered a rule requiring a packet and hoped the rule would be sufficient.

"Bring the packet," she said. "Do not detach the strip."

The clerk looked down at the book.

"Are you alleging a false handoff?"

"I am alleging that the handoff has been edited. I would like to know by whom and why."

The clerk nodded, relieved to have been accused of something that could be answered with paper.

---

The junction occupied three levels of the spire, connected by two freight lifts and a stair whose lower flights had been closed for repairs longer than Pell had worked at the Interlocutum.

The first courier was found on the middle level, waiting for a delayed return assignment. His name was Reth Carrow. He was an ordinary transport messenger, with a broken thumbnail and a grievance concerning the price of hot food.

He remembered the chest.

"It was heavy enough," he said. "Not the heaviest I've carried."

"Was it opened in your presence?" Atrovald asked.

"No. I was told to preserve the commander's seal."

"Did you?"

"I checked it when I collected it. Checked it again when I gave it over."

"To whom?"

"A route receiver. At the south desk."

"Name?"

"I don't know his name."

Pell indicated the amended entry. Carrow looked at the covered line, then at the clerk.

"I signed for the thing I handed over. I didn't write the name beside it."

"Would you recognize the receiver?" Atrovald asked.

"Possibly. I saw his face. Not for long."

"Describe what you remember before you look at anyone."

Carrow thought. The receiver had been older than he was, wearing the route office's grey sleeve band. His left hand was wrapped at the knuckles, as if from an old injury. He had complained that the commander's chest was being sent through a transfer desk reserved for documents rather than personal baggage.

"Did he say where he was taking it?"

"He said the lower dispatch cage. I told him the cage was closed. He said he had the key."

"Did you see the key?"

"No."

"Did you see him enter the cage?"

"No. I had a return load."

Atrovald asked Carrow to sign the account. She did not ask him to choose a face from the watch staff. There would be time for that if the route office could first establish who had actually been on duty.

Pell waited until Carrow had gone.

"A wrapped hand."

"A wrapped hand," she agreed.

"Not a scar on the chin."

"No."

He had not suggested they were the same man. Atrovald was grateful that he no longer needed to be reminded.

---

The evening watch packet was present.

It contained a routine correction notice, signed by a route supervisor who had noticed that the receiving column used a day-watch designation even though the chest was transferred during the evening overlap. The supervisor had instructed the clerk to replace the designation with the receiving desk's proper duty title.

No one had instructed the clerk to identify a particular human being.

"Then the correction is not a name," Pell said.

"It is a duty position," Atrovald replied.

"The first line looks like a name."

"The first line is an abbreviation. The clerk assumed it was a name. The supervisor assumed it was a shift code. Neither assumption identifies the receiver."

The junction chief, summoned from the upper level, examined the packet and looked ill.

"Is the transfer invalid?"

"The transfer is documented," Atrovald said. "Its receiving person is not."

"The desk seal was valid."

"So was the count seal."

He did not like the comparison.

She asked for the lower dispatch cage register. It had a receipt for the chest, entered twenty-three minutes after Carrow's handoff. The chest seal number matched the escort office's packing register. The weight was recorded as an estimate, not a measurement. The cage's inventory listed it as held for onward delivery under the commander's traveling authority.

"Where is it now?"

"Released with the southern circuit."

"To the commander?"

"To the route steward assigned to his baggage."

"Who is that?"

The chief opened a duty roster. A name had been entered against the steward's post. The roster was signed by the route office, not by the man himself.

Atrovald copied the name into a request for direct testimony. She did not declare the man the receiver Carrow had seen. The receiving desk and the onward steward were different jobs, and twenty-three minutes separated them.

"Can you prove the chest remained sealed while it was in the cage?" she asked.

"The seal number was checked on entry and release."

"By whom?"

"Two different cage attendants."

"Then preserve their checks and interview them separately. Do not ask either to confirm what the other wrote."

The chief hesitated.

"Would you prefer to seize the chest before the commander returns?"

"I would prefer not to invent a right of seizure because your register has disappointed me."

"It is under military authority."

"And we have a request pending under that authority. A request can be answered without a raid."

Outside the cage a bell rang to announce the next freight lift. The sound carried through the wall, followed by the slow scrape of a cart being dragged over an uneven threshold.

The junction was doing what it had always done. It moved things. It recorded movements well enough to charge the correct office. It did not record them well enough to reconstruct every hand that touched a sealed object.

The difference had become important to someone who had never expected to be asked.

---

The second chain took Atrovald to a room with no freight lifts and considerably more locks.

The reconciliation office had located the watch authorization for the disputed count adjustment. It was a sheet covering twelve routine balance corrections, each bearing the same department seal. At the bottom was the personal sign of Deputy Balance Officer Seret Dain.

Dain was alive, on duty, and unhappy to see the sheet.

"I signed the watch batch," he said.

"Did you sign each correction?" Atrovald asked.

"No. The department authorization permitted the receiving clerk to process ordinary corrections. I signed that the batch was within the routine category."

"Did you examine the underlying custody reports?"

"I examined the exceptions summary."

"Does it list Hesk Venn?"

"No."

"Does it list a missing guard?"

"No."

"What does it list?"

"One escort-strength variance. Fifty-three authorized, fifty-two returned. No casualty declaration attached."

Pell looked at the summary. Its category code had been entered before the automatic closure of the inquiry. It had not been generated by the closure itself.

"Who classified the variance?" he asked.

Dain pointed to the originating column. It bore the escort office's department seal and the initials of the receiving reconciliation clerk.

"That clerk copied the code from the originating request," Dain said. "He did not decide whether anyone was missing."

"And you accepted it as routine."

"Yes."

"Why?"

Dain looked from Pell to Atrovald.

"Because the rule required an exceptional-loss declaration to remove a case from the routine batch. There wasn't one."

"Was the missing-person inquiry available to you?"

"Not in the batch packet. That register belonged to custody."

"Did you know it existed?"

"No."

Atrovald asked him to describe his work rather than defend it. The watch had contained twelve corrections, four ration adjustments, two disputed property returns and an equipment discrepancy requiring an armed escort. The deputy had six clerks, one absent for illness. The summary had been signed at the end of a long watch. Dain had checked that the seals were valid, the sums reconciled, and the exception declarations were present where the code demanded them.

He had not checked whether another office possessed an open complaint contradicting the code.

"The regulation did not require a cross-check," he said.

"It should have," Pell replied.

"Perhaps. But if you are asking whether I knew a guard had vanished and concealed it, the answer is no."

"We are not yet asking you that," Atrovald said. "We are establishing what you authorized."

Dain held her gaze.

"I authorized the batch. The fifty-two entered the balance under my authority. I did not originate the figure."

"That is the distinction we will record."

He seemed almost disappointed that she had accepted the part of his answer that implicated him.

---

They found the reason for the routine classification in a smaller document.

The escort office's old dispatch procedure required a casualty declaration only when an officer reported an identified person missing, dead, injured or otherwise unavailable. A spare authorized position returned unfilled required no such declaration. The reconciliation form had only one box for both conditions: **NO DECLARED LOSS**.

The absence of a casualty declaration could therefore mean that no one had been lost.

It could also mean that no one had been named.

Both circumstances produced the same mark.

"Who wrote this procedure?" Pell asked.

"Someone who believed every missing person would already be on a personnel roll," Atrovald said.

Dain had accompanied them to the document room. He read the form and rubbed the bridge of his nose.

"The escort office has a list of authorized positions," he said. "We receive the number actually returned. If the difference is an unfilled relief slot, there is no missing man."

"Correct."

"And if the difference is a missing man who never entered the named attendance sheet?"

"The form would treat him as an unfilled relief slot unless someone filed an exceptional loss."

Dain looked at the box again.

"Then I signed a category that could not distinguish those cases."

"You signed a category that did not distinguish them," Atrovald said. "We still do not know which case occurred."

He did not ask her to call him innocent. She did not offer.

The inquiry clerk had attempted to report a missing person. The count office had received a routine numerical adjustment. A valid seal had carried the latter through a process that closed the former. No single official had needed to see both pieces of paper.

Pell opened his notebook to the page containing Halden's statement.

"Halden received the form."

"Yes."

"Dain authorized the batch."

"Yes."

"The escort office supplied the classification."

"Yes."

"And we still do not know who supplied the fifty-two."

"Not yet."

Dain took a long time to sign the interview record. When he finished, he asked whether his signature would be removed from the original authorization.

"No," Atrovald said.

"Then I suppose this will become a disciplinary matter."

"It may become an examination of your judgment. It will certainly become an examination of the rule. Those are not the same inquiry."

"Will you recommend my suspension?"

"Not on evidence that you performed the work you were assigned."

Dain's shoulders lowered a fraction.

"Someone will want one."

"Then someone may explain which failure a suspension would repair."

---

The amended missing-person review instruction reached Atrovald before evening watch. Both custody offices had signed it. The reconciliation office had added a condition: no count adjustment involving disputed physical custody could close an inquiry until an identified officer certified the disposition of the actual person or the documented vacancy.

There was a cost attached.

The first day's review queue contained nineteen old cases requiring a human reader. Two concerned transport manifests. One concerned a prisoner whose receiving office had changed names between shifts. Sixteen appeared to be ordinary count errors. None could be closed merely because they appeared ordinary.

The custody chief brought the estimate himself.

"Four additional clerks per watch for at least three weeks," he said. "And two supervisors to sign exceptions."

"From where?"

"Index reconciliation. The historical derivative backlog."

Atrovald looked at the estimate. The derivative backlog included work she had ordered. It was the work by which the Interlocutum maintained its claim to know what had changed in humanity's written past.

"You will not take them from physical custody," she said.

"Then the historical work slows."

"Yes."

"The Logos office will ask why."

"Give them the staffing figures. Not the sealed inquiries."

He waited for a more comfortable answer.

"Matron, we cannot keep the archive accurate if we stop reconciling it."

"We cannot keep it accurate by treating a missing person as an acceptable arithmetic remainder either."

He signed the temporary reassignment with visible reluctance. Atrovald signed beneath him. For three weeks the Interlocutum would preserve fewer historical comparisons in order to read more current custody records. The choice was not free. Somewhere an old textual discrepancy would wait longer to be found.

Pell arrived with another piece of paper: Personnel had confirmed the quartermaster's back-pay instruction for the next accounting cycle. The payment had not yet cleared.

"Shall I tell her it is settled?" he asked.

"Tell her it is approved and unpaid."

"She will be angry."

"She is already angry. Let her at least be accurately angry."

He made the note.

---

The two investigation packets returned to Atrovald's desk after the final watch.

The chest had moved from Carrow to an unidentified route receiver, into the lower cage, and onward under the escort commander's traveling authority. Its recorded seal number remained consistent at each documented point. The original named attendance sheet was still not available for inspection. Carrow had supplied a description, not an identification. The route steward and both cage attendants had been requested for separate testimony.

The count adjustment had passed through Halden's receiving desk into a routine batch authorized by Seret Dain. Dain was now identified and had acknowledged responsibility for the category approval. The escort office's originating officer, and the human being who decided that fifty-two was the correct returned strength, remained unknown.

Atrovald placed the packets beside the earlier dispatch counterfoil.

Fifty-three authorized.

Fifty-three chits issued.

Fifty-two recorded on return.

A report that someone was missing.

An automatic closure that had never been a withdrawal.

There were more signatures now, and fewer excuses for confusing one with another. None of the signatures supplied Hesk Venn's face.

She checked the separate sealed notices before retiring. The tomorrow-dated return instruction remained unexecuted, with its two disagreeing clock readings preserved. Mortuary-Seventeen remained unopened. Sorn's custody objection still prevented the Logos observer from taking the ceramic container. Predulum and Witness Four remained in Null-Seven under the same order. The Venn inquiry was open.

Pell watched her put the new packets in separate drawers.

"Have we found the officer responsible?" he asked.

"We have found an officer responsible for authorizing a routine batch."

"And the courier?"

"We have found the man who handed over the chest."

"That sounds like half an answer twice."

"It is two answers to two different questions."

He considered that, then nodded toward the unsigned future instruction.

"And tomorrow?"

Atrovald closed the drawer.

"Tomorrow has not yet submitted its evidence."

The spire's old mechanical clock struck the watch. The newer timepiece above her door showed a different minute. Neither had asked the other for permission.

Atrovald left them disagreeing.

The archive had spent centuries making differences disappear from its records. Tonight she had spent an entire watch paying clerks to keep them visible.

She was beginning to suspect that this was the more expensive form of custody.
