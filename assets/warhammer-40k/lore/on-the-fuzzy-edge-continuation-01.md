# On the Fuzzy Edge

## Continuation I — The Saint of Administrative Delay

*An original Cafarron Corridor chronicle continuation. Warhammer 40,000 and associated marks and setting elements are the property of Games Workshop. This non-commercial fan work is not endorsed by or affiliated with Games Workshop.*

Javard had learned, over a career whose official length varied according to which office was asking, that there were three kinds of emergency in the Imperium.

There were emergencies that killed people.

There were emergencies that killed worlds.

And there were emergencies that required a form which nobody could find.

Pontiff Montpclair's face had managed, with admirable ecclesiastical efficiency, to involve all three.

The nave still smelled of incense, hot insulation and cooked meat. The first was intentional. The second had come from the Pontiff's left ear. The third was being politely ignored by everyone who had not been paid to carry him away.

Javard remained in the pew while the medicae attendants disappeared through a side door with Montpclair strapped to a brass-framed litter. The congregation had not yet decided whether the proper response was prayer, evacuation or remaining perfectly still until somebody of sufficient rank told them which of the first two would be less heretical.

He admired their caution.

It was the sort of caution on which the Imperium had been built, mostly because all the more decisive people had died during construction.

Beside him, one of the El Shaban factors leaned close enough that the man's breath stirred the edge of Javard's collar.

"Do we leave?"

"No."

"Do we pray?"

"If you know the words."

"I know most of them."

"Then mumble. Confidence is more dangerous than ignorance in a cathedral."

The factor nodded and lowered his head.

Javard watched the side door.

He had come to Jhasyi'apan to ask Montpclair about a book that officially did not exist, had survived a cartel that officially had not survived, and had travelled aboard a merchant vessel whose owners had produced three mutually exclusive cargo manifests proving that nothing objectionable had ever been carried in the compartment where it had been found.

The Tides of Tomorrow had belonged, at the relevant time, to Trade House Kelliam Unsara. The vessel had changed registry twice since, undergone a refit once, and acquired a devotional alcove where the suspect compartment had been. Javard considered that last detail particularly Imperial. When a place had become inconvenient, somebody eventually installed a saint in it.

Montpclair had been the clean route upward.

Now Montpclair's eye was somewhere beneath the pulpit.

Javard could see it from his pew.

The little brass-and-glass mechanism had rolled against the first step and stopped with its cracked lens pointed toward the congregation. Every few seconds the iris clicked open and shut.

Nobody touched it.

Quite sensible.

A junior deacon finally emerged from behind the rood screen. He was young enough to believe that authority was a condition rather than an improvisation. His face had the bloodless look of a man who had just discovered how much of his institution depended upon one elderly cleric remaining conscious.

"The sermon is concluded," he announced.

A woman three rows ahead raised a hand.

The deacon stared at her.

She lowered it.

"His Reverence has suffered a temporary infirmity of the flesh. There is no cause for disorder."

Behind Javard, something electrical exploded in a wall niche.

The deacon did not look around.

Javard decided he liked him.

The congregation began to file out.

He waited until the aisle had thinned, then rose with the El Shaban factors and joined the slow procession toward the transept. He did not approach the main doors. Instead he turned toward a narrow clerical passage beneath a relief of Saint Drusus trampling a collection of anatomically improbable xenos.

A verger intercepted him.

"Private precinct, factor."

Javard smiled.

"Of course. I require the office responsible for devotional shipping permits."

The verger's suspicion became boredom so quickly it was almost beautiful.

"Third registry hall. Outer cloister."

"I was directed here."

"Then you were directed incorrectly."

"I have seven crates of votive wax in bonded storage."

The verger's expression developed pain.

"Outer cloister."

"The port authority says ecclesiastical release."

"Outer cloister."

"The outer cloister says cathedral seal."

The verger closed his eyes.

There were many keys to the Imperium. Rank was one. Violence was another. Money opened a surprising number of doors.

But inconvenience remained the master key.

"Wait here."

The verger vanished through the private door.

Javard's factor looked at him.

"We don't have seven crates of votive wax."

"We may by the end of the day."

"Do we need them?"

"Almost certainly not."

The factor considered this and wisely stopped asking questions.

The verger returned with an elderly clerk in grey vestments whose right arm had been replaced by an augmetic ending in six delicate brass fingers. Unlike Montpclair's cranial work, the limb appeared to have been maintained by somebody who believed maintenance was more than an optimistic liturgy.

"Sub-Dean Caldris," the clerk said. "You have a seal dispute?"

"A shipping dispute."

"Worse."

"I was hoping you might say that."

Caldris studied him.

Javard had used the El Shaban identity for nine days. It was a small house, sufficiently legitimate to survive cursory inspection and sufficiently unimportant that nobody expected its representatives to be memorable. The vessel papers were real. The tax seals were real. The cargo was real, although most of it belonged to men who would be surprised to learn that Javard temporarily represented them.

The rosette beneath his coat was also real.

That was the dangerous document.

"Come," Caldris said.

The private precinct smelled less of incense and more of paper.

Javard preferred it immediately.

They passed two scriptoria, a reliquary accounting office and a chamber in which three adepts were arguing over whether a deceased canon's chair belonged to the cathedral, his hereditary estate or the chair's original donor. None of them appeared aware that the canon had died eighty-three years earlier.

"You have been busy," Javard observed.

Caldris glanced toward the argument.

"The chair has become complicated."

"Has anyone tried sitting in it?"

The sub-dean looked genuinely offended.

Javard decided not to make another joke. There were limits.

Caldris led him into a records chamber whose walls were hidden behind iron-fronted cabinets. A servitor sat beneath a green lumen globe, feeding parchment strips into a reader with the solemnity of a priest administering last rites.

"Name of cargo."

"Votive wax."

"Origin."

"El Shaban bonded stores, berth nineteen."

"Destination?"

Javard gave him the name of a chapel he had passed that morning.

Caldris began entering the details into a brass keyboard.

Javard waited.

The keyboard jammed on the fourth line.

Caldris struck it with his augmetic fist.

It resumed working.

Javard approved of the local machine cult's practical theology.

"And the disputed seal?" Caldris asked.

"The port claims the shipment falls under a restriction attached to Kelliam Unsara traffic."

The brass fingers stopped.

Only for a fraction of a second.

It was enough.

Javard looked at the servitor.

Caldris looked at Javard.

"Kelliam Unsara does not hold your charter."

"No."

"Then the port is mistaken."

"Ports often are. I find that is why they employ clerks."

Caldris's human hand moved away from the keyboard.

"You should return to your ship, Factor."

"I would prefer my wax."

"Buy more."

"Seven crates?"

"Eight, if it encourages you to leave."

There it was.

Not fear. Not exactly.

Recognition.

Javard had spent nine days asking careful questions about a dead cartel and a merchant ship. He had received shrugs, blank looks, contradictory ledgers and one remarkably detailed explanation of why a warehouse fire forty-two years earlier had destroyed precisely the records he wanted while sparing six centuries of tithe assessments stored on the shelf above them.

This was the first time anyone had wanted him gone before he had finished being tedious.

He reached into his coat.

Caldris's augmetic hand twitched.

Javard withdrew a folded port invoice.

"I dislike abandoning paperwork," he said.

Caldris stared at the invoice.

"It creates precedents."

The old clerk exhaled slowly.

"What do you actually want?"

"A cathedral release for seven crates of wax."

"And?"

"To know why the name Kelliam Unsara frightened a man who has spent the afternoon adjudicating ownership of an eighty-three-year-dead priest's chair."

Caldris said nothing.

The servitor pulled another strip of parchment through its reader.

Somewhere deeper in the cathedral, a bell began to toll for Montpclair.

Not a death bell.

Javard counted the intervals.

Medicae summons.

Good. The Pontiff was still alive.

Probably.

Caldris crossed the room and closed the door.

"The Tides of Tomorrow came here seventeen years ago," he said.

Javard did not move.

"I know."

"No. You know the registered visit."

That was better.

Caldris touched the cabinet nearest the servitor.

"It came back."

The words settled into the room with considerably more weight than seven imaginary crates of wax.

"When?"

"Six months later. No registry. No cargo declaration. No berth assignment. It remained beyond the inner traffic ring for eleven hours. A cathedral lighter went out to it."

"Whose lighter?"

Caldris looked toward the door.

"Montpclair's predecessor."

"What came back?"

"The lighter."

"That was not the question."

"Three men went out. Four returned."

Javard felt the old, familiar stillness settle behind his ribs.

It was not fear. Fear was useful and immediate.

This was the sensation of a cold trail becoming warm beneath one's hand.

"Name the fourth."

Caldris's face tightened.

"There isn't one."

"Everyone has a name."

"Not in the register."

Javard finally took out the rosette.

He did not flourish it. Men who flourished Inquisitorial authority generally misunderstood what made it frightening.

He placed it on the desk between them.

Caldris went very still.

The temporary authorization seals caught the green lumen light.

"You are going to show me the register," Javard said.

Caldris swallowed.

"Interrogator—"

"And then you are going to tell me where the fourth man went."

The bell continued to toll.

Caldris looked down at the rosette, then toward the iron cabinets.

"There is a problem."

Javard sighed.

Of course there was.

"What kind?"

Caldris pointed at the cabinet.

A red wax seal covered its lock.

Stamped into the wax was the skull-and-sunburst device of the Holy Ordos.

Javard leaned closer.

The seal was old.

Older than his authorization.

Older, perhaps, than Kelford del Blank's present title.

He straightened.

"Well," he said.

The factor behind him made the mistake of asking, "Is that bad?"

Javard looked at the Inquisitorial seal barring the records he had come to investigate.

"No," he said. "Bad would have been simple."
