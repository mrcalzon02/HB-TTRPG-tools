# Antegra Station

## Continuation I — An Honest Tithe

*An original Cafarron Corridor chronicle continuation. Warhammer 40,000 and associated marks and setting elements are the property of Games Workshop. This non-commercial fan work is not endorsed by or affiliated with Games Workshop.*

Governor Talbor Varik had ordered an honest tithe.

This was, Captain Ardan Krell reflected, considerably more dangerous than ordering an execution.

Executions were familiar things. Antegra Station possessed six approved methods, three unofficial methods, and a seventh which the Mechanicus insisted was merely an unfortunate interaction between an induction rail and unsecured devotional jewelry. There were forms for executions. There were witnesses. There were stamps.

Honesty had no department.

The governor’s armored mag-tram descended through the station while ice scraped the tunnel walls with the patient sound of something trying to get in.

Varik sat alone on the forward bench. He had dismissed the household attendants, the junior secretaries and the physician who had objected that the governor’s latest neural vivification cycle required twelve hours without political exertion.

Varik had informed him that Antegra had survived two hundred years without respecting medical advice and could endure another afternoon.

Krell remained standing by the door.

“High Presidio answered,” he said.

Varik did not look up. “How many times?”

“Once.”

“Then they have not understood the seriousness of the matter.”

“They acknowledged receipt.”

“That is not an answer. That is what bureaucrats do when they wish a problem to become historical.”

Krell handed him the slate.

The message was eleven lines long. Four concerned authentication. Three concerned jurisdiction. Two explained that weather conditions might delay the arrival of an Arbites inspection cadre. One promised that the petition had been assigned a priority classification.

The final line requested clarification regarding whether Antegra Station was presently in rebellion.

Varik read it twice.

“No,” he said.

Krell waited.

“Not yet.”

The tram passed beneath Sector Twelve. Meltwater hammered the roof. Somewhere above them, pumps labored against a flood fed by ice that had been part of the station longer than several noble houses had existed.

Varik handed the slate back.

“Send the tithe manifests.”

“All of them?”

“All of them.”

“The corrected manifests?”

“The real ones.”

Krell was silent for three seconds.

Varik smiled without humor. “There. You see? Even you know what sort of weapon that is.”

For twenty-one cycles the station had survived by reporting what distant offices needed to hear. Ore losses became transport variances. Frozen habitation blocks became temporary population redistributions. Missing ration stock became spoilage. Dead laborers remained on rolls because the living still required their allotments.

Some lies had kept people alive.

Others had kept councilors warm.

Varik had finally decided to discover which was which.

The tram stopped at Central Audit.

The doors opened onto armed men.

Not Varik’s men.

Commissar-Keeper Dren Solvik stood behind them in a black greatcoat, perfectly dry despite the condensation raining from the ceiling.

“Governor.”

“Commissar-Keeper.”

“You have ordered the transmission of unratified tithe data.”

“I have ordered the transmission of accurate tithe data.”

Solvik’s expression did not change.

Krell had never liked men who could hear those two sentences and immediately understand the difference.

“The Securities Advisory Board has suspended external data release pending review.”

Varik stepped off the tram.

“You do not possess that authority.”

“We possess emergency authority over information likely to cause civil destabilization.”

“My station is freezing, flooding, starving and falsifying the number of people doing all three. What precisely remains to destabilize?”

“The population’s belief that somebody is in control.”

Varik looked around at the leaking ceiling.

“That belief has shown admirable resistance to evidence.”

One of Solvik’s troopers almost smiled.

Almost.

Solvik noticed.

The trooper became interested in a wall.

“The Board requests that you return to the Sovereign precinct,” Solvik said.

“No.”

“This is not a negotiation.”

“Everything in government is a negotiation. Executions merely represent a party withdrawing early.”

Varik walked toward the audit doors.

The troopers did not move.

Neither did Krell.

For several seconds Antegra Station balanced itself on the professional discomfort of eight armed men who had not been told whether arresting their governor counted as loyalty.

Then the lights failed.

Darkness swallowed the corridor.

Emergency lumens came on red.

A second later the station vox began screaming.

“THERMAL BREACH. CENTRAL THREE. THERMAL BREACH. CENTRAL THREE.”

Solvik turned his head.

Varik did not.

“Captain,” he said.

Krell checked his slate.

“Central Three heat exchange manifold. Pressure loss across six feeder trunks.”

“How many hab blocks?”

“Eleven.”

“Population?”

Krell hesitated.

The official number was forty-two thousand.

The real number was closer to sixty.

Varik saw the hesitation.

“That,” he said quietly to Solvik, “is why the manifests are leaving.”

The Commissar-Keeper’s jaw tightened.

Then another message appeared.

Not on the public vox.

On Varik’s command slate.

MECHANICUS APPROACH AUTHORITY CONFIRMED.

A second line followed.

EXTERNAL DOCK THREE. PRIORITY CUSTODIAL ENTRY. CLEAR ALL CIVIL CLAIMS.

Krell stared at it.

“They came.”

Varik took the slate.

High Presidio had promised delay.

The Arbites had not yet answered.

But the Mechanicus had answered with a ship already close enough to demand a dock.

That meant somebody had known Antegra was failing before Varik sent his petition.

Or somebody had been waiting for permission to enter.

Neither possibility improved his mood.

Solvik read the notice over his shoulder.

For the first time that day, the Commissar-Keeper looked surprised.

Varik enjoyed it more than was dignified.

“Your information quarantine,” he said, “appears to have developed a hole.”

Solvik ignored the remark. “You requested Mechanicus intervention.”

“I did.”

“From High Presidio.”

“I did.”

“This authorization predates your petition.”

Varik looked at him.

There it was.

A small thing. A date seal. Eight digits and an authorization cipher.

In the Imperium, civilizations had burned for less.

The vox continued to howl about Central Three.

Varik handed the slate to Krell.

“Open the audit hall.”

Solvik stepped forward. “Governor—”

“Open it. Then transmit the uncorrected manifests to High Presidio, the Arbites precinct, the arriving Mechanicus vessel and every tithe authority whose address has not frozen out of the cogitator.”

“You may trigger a station-wide investigation.”

“That is the idea.”

“You may trigger your own removal.”

Varik glanced toward the red-lit corridor behind him, toward the buried station he had inherited through blood, bribery, murder, rejuvenat and two centuries of refusing to die at a convenient time.

“If Antegra survives me,” he said, “I will consider that an administrative success.”

Krell keyed the audit door.

It opened three centimeters and stopped.

A warning rune blinked.

MECHANICAL OBSTRUCTION.

Krell looked at Varik.

Varik looked at the door.

Behind it waited the true tithe ledgers of a station whose ruling class had spent years insisting that reality was a clerical error.

“Of course,” the governor said.

He drew his father’s saber.

Krell stared at him. “Sir?”

“The Imperium has denied me honesty, outside assistance and a functioning door in the same ten minutes.”

Varik pushed the blade into the seam beside the lock.

“Captain, kindly find me a crowbar.”
