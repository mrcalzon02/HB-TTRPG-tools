# Antegra Station

## Continuation VII — The Office Below

*An original Cafarron Corridor chronicle continuation. Warhammer 40,000 and associated marks and setting elements are the property of Games Workshop. This non-commercial fan work is not endorsed by or affiliated with Games Workshop.*

The impossible corridor had handrails.

Governor Talbor Varik found this offensive.

Not frightening. Antegra had exhausted frightening before breakfast. The corridor descended beyond the surveyed dimensions of the asteroid, its white walls warm beneath his fingertips, its ceiling illuminated by a sourceless violet glow, and somewhere ahead waited a city that had remembered him for sixty-eight years.

All of that was merely catastrophic.

The handrails were insulting.

They were Imperial pattern.

Captain Ardan Krell noticed his expression.

“What?”

Varik pointed.

Krell examined the rail.

“Standard transit fitting.”

“Yes.”

“Perhaps the ancient extradimensional intelligence values workplace safety.”

Senior Auditor Selene Marr ran one finger beneath it and inspected the dust.

“No dust.”

Commissar-Keeper Dren Solvik said, “Then it already exceeds Central Administration.”

Legate-Inquisitor Aveline Rhyse did not turn around.

“Try to remember that we may be walking into a hostile cognitive construct.”

Marr adjusted the maintenance pry-bar over her shoulder.

“I am remembering it very carefully.”

Witness Lysa Nineteen walked beside Rhyse, one hand wrapped around the Inquisitor's sleeve. Her blindfold remained in place.

She did not need eyes to watch the walls.

“They copied this from him,” she whispered.

Varik stopped.

“From me?”

“From what you think a corridor should be.”

Krell looked again at the handrail.

“That explains the disappointment.”

Varik resumed walking.

“I can still have you reassigned.”

“To where?”

Varik considered the impossible geometry around them.

“Somewhere more conventional.”

Krell smiled without humor.

“That is the kindest threat you have ever made.”

---

They descended for eleven minutes.

Marr timed it.

At the ninth minute she stopped pretending the measurements were useful.

Antegra's asteroid was less than four minutes deep at their present pace.

At eleven minutes, the corridor opened into a waiting room.

There were six chairs.

A desk.

A brass service bell.

And a portrait of Talbor Varik hanging on the wall.

The portrait showed him older.

Not subtly older. Not the careful, managed erosion permitted by rejuvenat treatments and expensive physicians. The man in the painting was ancient. His hair was white. His cheeks were hollow. One eye had been replaced with a black augmetic lens.

He wore the governor's chain.

Behind him rose seven moons.

Krell stared at the painting.

“My lord.”

“Yes.”

“You look terrible.”

“Thank you, Captain.”

“I thought honesty was our new policy.”

Marr approached the desk.

A neat stack of parchment waited there.

She read the top sheet.

Then she read it again.

“What?” Rhyse asked.

Marr handed it to her.

Rhyse's mouth tightened.

Varik took the document.

It was an appointment ledger.

The first entry was dated sixty-eight years earlier.

TALBOR VARIK — GOVERNOR — INITIAL CONSULTATION.

Below it were thousands of names.

Some Varik recognized as dead station officials.

Some belonged to citizens currently living on Antegra.

Some had not been born yet.

The final entry was his own.

TALBOR VARIK — GOVERNOR — FINAL CONSULTATION.

The time beside it was now.

Solvik looked at the brass bell.

“Do not ring that.”

Nobody had intended to.

The bell rang itself.

A door opened behind the desk.

Beyond it was the governor's office.

Not an imitation.

His office.

The old one.

The room Varik had occupied sixty-eight years before Central Administration had been rebuilt around a reactor accident everyone officially agreed had been caused by a valve.

The desk bore the scorch mark from his first month in office.

His father's saber rested on the wall.

Varik looked down at the saber in his own hand.

Then back at the wall.

Krell said, “I dislike duplicates.”

Solvik said, “Shoot one.”

Rhyse raised the silver hammer.

“No.”

Solvik sighed.

“Everyone objects to the efficient options.”

---

There was someone sitting behind the desk.

Talbor Varik.

Older.

White-haired.

Black-eyed.

Exactly as the portrait had shown him.

The old man folded his hands.

“You took your time.”

Krell's pistol came up.

Solvik's followed.

Rhyse did not raise a weapon.

Lysa began trembling.

Marr quietly placed a data-slate on the desk and started recording.

Varik looked at himself.

“What are you?”

The old man smiled.

“That was your first question last time.”

“I do not remember last time.”

“Yes. That was the arrangement.”

Rhyse stepped forward.

“Identify yourself.”

The old Varik regarded her with something like affection.

“Aveline Rhyse. Still carrying the hammer.”

Rhyse's face changed by less than a millimeter.

It was enough.

“You know me.”

“I remember you.”

“That is not the same thing.”

“No,” the old man said. “It is not.”

Lysa whispered, “He isn't alive.”

The figure looked at her.

“Neither is a law, child. It may still ruin your afternoon.”

Marr's stylus paused.

Varik almost laughed.

That annoyed him more than the handrails.

“What do you want?”

The old man leaned back.

“To finish the quarantine.”

Solvik's pistol steadied.

“By killing the station?”

“No.”

Rhyse said, “By spreading the memory.”

The old man looked at her.

“Closer.”

Varik felt the city behind his eyes.

White towers.

Seven moons.

Crowds standing beneath a violet sky.

But now he saw something he had not seen before.

The crowds were not looking upward.

They were looking at him.

At every version of him.

At every citizen who carried the memory.

At Antegra.

The old man tapped the desk.

“You called it contamination because you required a word that permitted fire.”

Rhyse said nothing.

“You called it infection because infection has borders. Hosts. Vectors. Procedures.”

Still nothing.

“You were wrong.”

Varik said, “Then correct us.”

The old man smiled.

“You are not remembering a city.”

Lysa made a small sound.

The old man continued.

“The city is remembering you.”

---

Silence held the office.

Krell broke it.

“That seems worse.”

“Yes,” Marr said.

“Thank you.”

“I thought honesty was our new policy.”

Varik ignored them.

“What does that mean?”

The old man looked toward the window.

There had never been a window in Varik's old office.

There was one now.

Beyond it stood the city.

White towers stretched beneath a violet sky. Seven moons hung impossibly large above narrow streets filled with motionless people.

Every face was turned toward the office.

“They built a civilization around preservation,” the old man said. “Not preservation of flesh. Not records. Not machines. Continuity.”

Rhyse's grip tightened around the hammer.

“A mnemonic construct.”

“A civilization that refused extinction.”

“Xenos.”

“Once.”

The word chilled the room.

Varik stepped closer to the window.

“What happened to them?”

“They succeeded.”

That was worse than an answer.

The old man continued.

“They learned to persist in observers. In descendants. In enemies. In anyone capable of holding enough of them to reconstruct the whole.”

Marr looked sick.

“The population of Antegra.”

“Eight million four hundred twelve thousand six hundred nine pieces of an incomplete recollection.”

Rhyse said, “And Varik?”

“The index.”

Varik turned.

The old man's black eye reflected seven moons.

“You were first because you were useful. Governor. Administrator. Connected to every department, every census, every transit authorization, every emergency order. You touched the whole station without ever touching most of its people.”

Varik understood.

The Department of Necessary Omissions.

The false census.

The rotating work crews.

The contradictory housing ledgers.

Sixty-eight years of engineered bureaucratic incompetence.

He had not merely hidden infected citizens from the Imperium.

He had kept them from connecting to one another.

“You fragmented the memory,” Marr whispered.

Varik shook his head.

“I fragmented the station.”

The old man nodded.

“And it worked.”

Solvik frowned.

“Until now.”

“Yes.”

“Why now?”

The old man looked at Varik.

“Because he told the truth.”

The honest census.

Every hidden citizen reconciled.

Every false death corrected.

Every missing worker returned to the administrative whole.

Varik had repaired Antegra's records.

And in doing so, he had assembled the city.

Marr sat down.

There were moments when an auditor discovered an error so large that sitting became an act of professional discipline.

“I would like,” she said slowly, “to formally withdraw several recommendations I made this morning.”

Krell nodded.

“I support the motion.”

---

Rhyse approached the desk.

“Can it be reversed?”

The old man looked at her.

“You asked that sixty-eight years ago.”

“And?”

“You disliked the answer.”

“I have disliked many answers.”

“This one survived.”

Rhyse lifted the silver hammer.

The old man smiled.

“So did that.”

Varik stepped between them.

“Enough.”

Rhyse's gaze snapped to him.

“You do not command me.”

“No. I command Antegra.”

“For the moment.”

“Then let us spend the moment productively.”

He looked at his older self.

“You said you wanted to finish the quarantine.”

“Yes.”

“How?”

“Antegra must forget itself again.”

Varik stared.

“Eight million people.”

“Not their lives. Their relationships.”

Marr stood.

“You want us to corrupt the census.”

“Yes.”

Solvik barked a laugh.

It was the first genuinely cheerful sound Varik had heard from him in years.

Rhyse looked appalled.

“You are proposing deliberate falsification of Imperial records.”

Everyone looked at her.

Even Lysa.

Rhyse's expression hardened.

“I am aware of the irony.”

Marr recovered first.

“We spent sixty-eight years maintaining contradictory registries by accident, conspiracy and departmental vanity.”

“Correct,” said the old man.

“And now we must reproduce that fragmentation deliberately.”

“Yes.”

Marr considered this.

“How much deliberate incompetence?”

The old man smiled.

“Enough.”

Marr looked offended.

“That is not a specification.”

For the first time, the old man looked uncertain.

Varik felt a small, vicious satisfaction.

There were things in the galaxy older than mankind, stranger than the warp, capable of preserving civilizations beyond death.

And still none of them knew how to answer an auditor who wanted a number.

---

The window changed.

The city vanished.

Antegra appeared instead.

Not the station as it existed physically, but a web of names, work assignments, family lines, ration accounts, transit histories and medical records.

Millions of points joined by threads.

The threads were multiplying.

The honest census had become a nervous system.

At its center burned one name.

TALBOR VARIK.

The old man said, “You have perhaps six hours.”

Rhyse said, “Before what?”

“Before enough connections stabilize that fragmentation becomes impossible.”

“And then?”

The old man looked through the impossible window.

“Then Antegra remembers the city completely.”

Lysa whispered, “And the city wakes up.”

“No,” said the old man.

Everyone turned toward him.

He looked suddenly tired.

“Then it remembers what killed it.”

The violet lights went out.

For one heartbeat the office was completely dark.

In that darkness Varik heard something vast breathing beyond the walls.

Then emergency lamps ignited.

The old man was gone.

The desk remained.

The window remained.

On the desk lay one paper file.

Marr picked it up.

The cover bore an Administratum heading in Varik's own handwriting.

DEPARTMENT OF NECESSARY OMISSIONS

EMERGENCY DISINTEGRATION PROTOCOL

AUTHORITY: GOVERNOR VARIK

STATUS: DENIED

Varik took the file.

“Denied by whom?”

A line appeared beneath the stamp.

BY GOVERNOR VARIK.

Krell closed his eyes.

“My lord.”

“Yes.”

“I believe you have become administratively opposed to yourself.”

Varik opened the file.

“That is hardly unprecedented.”

Rhyse looked toward the corridor.

“We have six hours.”

Marr checked her slate.

“Five hours, fifty-three minutes.”

Solvik holstered his pistol.

“What is the protocol?”

Varik read the first page.

Then the second.

His expression changed.

Krell noticed.

“What?”

Varik handed him the file.

Krell read it.

“Oh.”

Marr took it from him.

She read three paragraphs.

“No.”

Rhyse held out her hand.

Marr did not give it to her.

“That is not a plan.”

“What is it?”

Marr looked at Varik.

“It is an administrative civil war.”

Varik looked through the impossible window at eight million lives joined together by the truth.

For sixty-eight years Antegra had survived because nobody knew exactly how many people lived there, where they worked, who they were related to, or which department was responsible for them.

The Imperium had many weaknesses.

At last, Antegra had found one worth preserving.

Varik drew a breath.

“Return us to Central Audit.”

The door opened.

Beyond it waited the impossible corridor and, somewhere above, several thousand civil servants who had spent their careers making the station less comprehensible.

For the first time in Antegra's history, they were about to be appreciated.

“Captain,” Varik said.

Krell straightened.

“Governor?”

“Wake every department head.”

“It is the middle of the rest cycle.”

“I know.”

Krell smiled grimly.

“What shall I tell them?”

Varik looked at the emergency protocol.

“Tell them the truth.”

Everyone stared at him.

He allowed himself a thin smile.

“Only once. We shall spend the next six hours correcting the mistake.”
