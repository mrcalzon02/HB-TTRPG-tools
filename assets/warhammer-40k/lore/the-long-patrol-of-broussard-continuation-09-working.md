# The Long Patrol of Broussard

## XIX. THE MEASURE OF WHAT REMAINS — WORKING DRAFT

**Author:** Mrcalzon02 / Christopher Vardeman

The compressor service began before dawn and finished after the men assigned to it had expected breakfast.

Sella Varn watched the pressure trace while the mechanic cleaned the seating face, checked the fastenings and ran the bank through its low-load cycle twice. The borrowed seal held. That was the good news. The less convenient news was that the seal remained borrowed, and the recovery hauler had not acquired a replacement by waiting.

"Can we tow?" Captain Broussard asked.

"After the cooling bank has held under ordinary load," Sella said. "Not before."

The hauler chief stood by the hatch, his gloves under one arm.

"My crew have been ready since first light."

"Then they'll be pleased to learn their cargo is being kept worth recovering."

"Your cargo. My machine is the one without reserve seals."

Kelm had arrived in time to hear the exchange. He carried a slate showing the remaining service margin and the value of the produce if it survived the journey. Neither figure was reassuring alone.

"We can sign for another seal when the supply vehicle comes," he said.

"We can sign for ten," the hauler chief replied. "That doesn't put one in my hand."

Sella waited another interval before signing the service sheet. The last serviceable spare remained in the refrigeration locker, untouched. The hauler's debt was recorded against the recovery, not forgotten because a signature was inconvenient.

Broussard authorized the survey party to proceed. He did not authorize a pull.

---

The survey chief had found a new way of concealing his injured hand: he kept the bandage beneath his coat and pointed with his elbow.

His men were less impressed than he hoped. One carried the hand sounder, another the record board, and a third returned to each certified marker before the next hole was started. They had learned from the auger's destruction that a number written down in haste could cost more than a number not yet obtained.

Gabriel Hamden Jalesthesian accompanied them as security. He did not offer to operate the sounder.

At the second marker the probe returned a shallow refusal.

"Stone?" the younger surveyor asked.

"Could be."

"Could be the ridge underneath."

"That too."

The chief told him to sound a second line from the safe side. When the new probe returned a different depth, he crossed the proposed straight route off the drawing. The longer line bent inland around ice that might have supported the freighter and might have opened beneath it.

"We have two soundings," Gabriel observed.

"Three," said the chief. "One tells me I cannot trust the other two."

"Is that the usual way of counting?"

"Today it is."

The first stretch could be certified for twenty-four metres. Beyond it, a second series of holes suggested a firm shelf running toward a pressure ridge. Suggested was not proved. The chief marked a provisional thirty-one metres and withheld it until his men checked the anchor's lateral bearing.

Gabriel returned with the sketch. Broussard was beside Chimera Two, inspecting its plated vision block. The vehicle could hold a fixed position but could not safely chase anything across broken ice. Chimera Three had returned from Etten's medical transfer; its fuel reserve had not returned with it.

"Twenty-four certain," Gabriel said. "Thirty-one possible."

"Then we have twenty-four."

"The survey chief said you would say that."

"He knows me."

"He said it rather less affectionately."

Broussard signed the first route only.

---

Hessel was comparing Chimera Three's fuel consumption with the convoy's allocation sheet. The vehicle had completed the casualty exchange on the certified inbound road. It had also used more fuel than a clerk at the *Saint Malderson* expected from the distance alone.

"The clerk wants to know why," Hessel said.

"Tell him we drove in winter."

"I considered that. It seemed insufficiently technical."

"Cold starts, low-speed marker checks, heater load and the return journey. Give him the driver's figures."

Hessel had them. He had hoped for a shorter answer.

The convoy acknowledged the medical handoff and the previous day's ninety-four metres of movement. It had not released another fuel allotment. The replacement auger remained assigned to the next scheduled supply vehicle, which had not departed. The quartermaster wanted the refrigeration service hours before considering further movement allowance.

Kelm read the dispatch.

"They want to know what it costs to keep my food cold before they'll spend fuel to recover it."

"They want to know which account pays," Broussard said.

"Does the account matter if the food spoils?"

"It will matter to the man ordered to replace it."

Broussard sent the signed compressor sheet, actual vehicle consumption and surveyed distance. He offered neither an arrival date nor a claim that the provisional thirty-one metres had already been traversed.

Gabriel took the message to the relay post. He passed the survey chief's two assistants sharing a cold meal. The empty place beside them had belonged to Etten.

"Any word?" one asked.

"He's at the road infirmary. The surgeon has not decided about his foot."

"That's what they told us yesterday."

"It is still what they know."

The man nodded and returned to his food. Gabriel did not improve the news for him.

---

On the bridge, Holt had recorded the compressor service against the cargo temperature trace. The controlled low-load interval had not pushed the forward holds beyond their working range. He marked the time so a later buyer could distinguish planned maintenance from refrigeration failure.

Kelm examined the twelve-bin report.

"Still the same?"

"Ten within tolerance, one light, one twenty-seven kilograms over its declared estimate. No new weighing. No opening."

"Good."

"The heavy one has a fresh restraint mark. The watch saw it after the last tow. The strap is sound, but the witness paint has shifted."

"Broken fastening?"

"None visible."

"Then write down what they saw."

"I have."

Holt's sheet recorded the time, watch, strap position and absence of visible damage. It did not change the weight or guess at the contents.

"Leave the seal in place," Kelm said. "We'll inspect it where the produce cannot become another accident."

"Another weighing?"

"Not while the cooling plant needs its service margin and the hauler is waiting. A scale won't tell us why paint moved."

Holt filed the report with the earlier measurements. The fourteen seconds missing before the original impact remained in the bridge archive on another sheet. He did not combine two unrelated absences into a theory.

Below the bridge, the forward bins rested behind cold baffles. The disputed restraint held. Beneath the upper roots the cargo had settled against the partition at a new angle, leaving a shallow hollow where none had been before. The watchman saw only the sound strap and intact seal.

The first twenty-four metres of the next tow remained to be earned.

---

The first movement was not permitted until the survey chief had read his own figures aloud.

He disliked this procedure. He disliked it more because he had suggested it after the auger was lost.

"Twenty-four metres," he said. "Sounded on two lines, tied to the present mark, anchor bearing checked against the inland ridge. The following thirty-one are not part of this certificate."

The recovery chief had already drawn a neat continuous line through both stretches. He crossed out the second portion while the survey chief watched.

"That will be another hour," he said.

"It might be," the survey chief answered. "I would prefer it to another man."

Sella compared the bearing with the revised tackle drawing. The line of pull would change as the freighter moved. The new anchor could carry the first stage, but not the next without resetting the hauler on ground the sounders had not yet certified.

"Twenty-four," she agreed. "One pull. Then the cable comes slack and we inspect."

Broussard read both signatures. He did not improve them by adding his own opinion of the ice.

Chimera Two took the inner perimeter with its damaged track pads and plated vision block. Chimera Three remained near the original approach, where its reduced fuel reserve would still permit an orderly return. Hessel was assigned to the marker post rather than the forward sweep. He examined the order as if it had been written to offend him personally.

"I've been promoted from mobile infantry to a signpost."

"A signpost with a lasgun," Broussard said.

"An unusually expensive signpost."

"Then try not to get stolen."

Hessel took his place without further argument. Gabriel walked the hard-line cable to the outer observer and checked the return signal. It carried clearly until a gust drove loose snow against the junction box. He had the box covered and tested it again. No one had to learn about a failed relay by needing it.

---

The hauler took the strain slowly enough that the men beside the tackle could hear the cable begin to sing.

The freighter moved nine metres before the first stop. A pressure trace climbed at the starboard brace, flattened and then climbed again. Sella ordered the pull reduced. The fitter at the guide found no fresh contact, but the angle had changed enough to bring the vibration closer to the earlier limit.

"Can you correct it?" the recovery chief asked.

"With the line unloaded."

He looked at the hauler, at the freighter and at the men waiting in the cold. Then he ordered the line unloaded.

The correction required moving the guide shoe and checking its clearance against the repaired frame. A fitter worked from a secured platform while another watched the trace. The work took thirty-seven minutes. The hauler crew kept its engine warm and used fuel nobody had yet agreed to reimburse.

"That is what the margin is for," Sella said when the chief showed her the clock.

"The margin is nearly gone."

"Then we should stop spending it pretending."

At the next test the trace held. Broussard authorized the remaining fifteen metres under the same load ceiling, not a higher one. The hauler moved. The *Hesperant Vale* followed, slowly, with its own restricted thrust contributing only after the tackle accepted the weight.

At twenty metres a ridge sent a short vibration through the ground. The outer observer called it in, and every man on the line stopped what he was doing. There was no second movement. The survey chief checked the nearest flagged hole from the certified side and found its measured depth unchanged. No crack had reached the working plate. He wrote down the observation rather than offering a theory of what had moved beneath them.

Four more metres carried the freighter to the signed mark.

The recovery chief called the halt himself. The tackle went slack. Sella inspected the brace, the guide shoe and the anchor before accepting the stage. The survey chief inspected the ground again, using his good hand to hold the record board while an assistant took the measurements.

Holt entered the new total on the bridge: four hundred and four metres from the original recovery site. He kept the previous three hundred and eighty beneath it. Twenty-four metres of progress had consumed most of a watch.

"Can we take the next thirty-one?" Kelm asked over the bridge circuit.

"Not on that certificate," Broussard replied.

The provisional route still existed on paper. So did the pressure ridge. Neither had become safer because the ship was nearer to them.

---

The second inspection of the forward restraint was conducted with two witnesses and no opened lids.

Holt had asked for a cargo hand from the outgoing watch and another from the incoming one. They examined the heavy bin's strap, its buckle, the paint mark on the runner and the seal at the hatch. The buckle was locked. The strap remained under tension. The paint had shifted a little farther since the previous entry, but there was no corresponding tear in the restraint webbing.

"Settling?" the incoming hand asked.

"Possibly," Holt said.

"Something underneath it?"

"That is another possibility."

"Then why don't we look?"

Holt glanced at the temperature recorder. The compressor had returned to its normal duty cycle after the service. The next opening would admit warmer air into a hold already operating on borrowed maintenance margin. The bins were built to contain loose produce, not to offer a safe inspection chamber after a collision.

"Because we cannot open it safely here. Because I cannot tell you which way the load will move if the baffle has failed. And because a man with a pry bar is not a substitute for a receiving berth."

The outgoing hand wrote the new witness mark against the previous mark. Holt countersigned it. The twenty-seven-kilogram excess remained the last measured difference, not a new weighing and not an estimate of whatever might be inside.

Kelm met him outside the cold bank.

"Do you believe the cargo has moved?"

"I believe the mark has moved. I don't know whether the weight has."

"That answer won't satisfy the buyer."

"Then we will have to disappoint the buyer with an accurate answer."

Kelm studied him for a moment. Holt had once answered a question like that by looking for the number his captain wanted. Now he kept an untidy ledger and waited to be told whether it was acceptable.

"Make sure the next watch sees the mark," Kelm said. "And leave the lid alone."

Holt had already given that order. He entered Kelm's confirmation beneath it, because an instruction repeated by a captain was still an instruction worth recording.

The cold bank's motor changed pitch behind the bulkhead. Nothing in the hold requested permission to remain hidden.

---

The convoy's next message contained three decisions and no replacement auger.

The quartermaster accepted the signed refrigeration service sheet as a proper charge against the recovery. He accepted Hessel's fuel log for Chimera Three's medical journey after the return distance and heater hours were added. He did not authorize the additional allotment yet. A fuel carrier could be attached to the scheduled supply movement, subject to the carrier's own route inspection and capacity.

The road infirmary confirmed that Etten had been reviewed again. The swelling was still too great for the surgeon to promise a final outcome. Circulation remained present. The next examination was scheduled. The report said nothing about guilt, equipment liability or the value of the young man's foot to the Munitorum.

Gabriel read the medical paragraph twice.

"He'll want to know what they decided."

"They haven't," Broussard said.

"That is going to be a difficult message."

"Better than a comforting one they cannot support."

Gabriel folded the paper. His own Schola had taught him how efficiently certainty could be manufactured when a superior wanted an answer. He had learned the method. He did not intend to apply it to a wounded survey assistant merely because uncertainty was unpleasant.

At the survey shelter the chief was checking the lateral bearing for the provisional thirty-one metres. He looked up when Gabriel arrived.

"Etten?"

"Still has circulation. The surgeon has not made the next decision."

The chief nodded. His injured hand remained swollen, though he had finally allowed the medic to change the dressing.

"Will they charge him for the auger?"

"No finding has been made."

"That wasn't my question."

"I know."

Gabriel gave him the exact wording of the medical dispatch. He did not give him a prediction. The chief took it back to his men, where an accurate uncertainty would have to serve for the evening.

---

Broussard's final report carried the twenty-four metres, the revised brace clearance, the relay repair, the withheld second route and the unchanged restrictions on both damaged Chimeras. It carried the hauler's additional engine hours and the refrigeration spare still reserved for the cold bank. It carried the cargo restraint's new witness mark without converting a shifted line of paint into an accusation.

Kelm signed the ship's portion. He had stopped arguing about whether the journey was taking too long. He had begun arguing about which delay would cost him his cargo. Broussard considered that a more useful disagreement.

"Four hundred and four," Kelm said.

"Four hundred and four measured."

"And the next thirty-one?"

"When the bearing and anchor are signed."

"By the time we reach the convoy, the clerks will have enough pages to pave the road."

"Then perhaps they'll finally give us a road."

Kelm almost smiled. He took his copy and went back to the bridge.

Outside, Hessel was relieved from the marker post and asked whether the signpost would receive an evening ration. Broussard told him it would, provided it returned the issued ammunition. Gabriel heard the exchange and said nothing. There were worse ways for men to end a day than complaining about food they would actually receive.

The next supply vehicle had not departed. The predator beneath the ice had not been found. The disputed bin remained sealed. Etten's foot remained in the surgeon's care, and the freighter had advanced twenty-four metres without surrendering its remaining cooling margin.

Broussard marked the next watch's first duty: verify the lateral bearing, not move the ship.

It was a small distinction. On this ice, it was the sort that kept men alive.
