The borrowed release ram came back with a serviceable seal and a replacement charge.

Rix held the receipt beneath a flickering dock lumen. Emergency issue, off-berth return, inspection, and damage deposit appeared as four separate lines.

“That's more than the job paid.”

The equipment clerk looked at the lines. “The job still has an allowance.”

“An allowance you've put on hold.”

“I didn't put it anywhere. The system did when the damage report arrived.”

The ram had moved the first cargo load when the tug's own release gear failed inspection. Its handle was scored; the return hand had certified that it actuated under load, not that it was undamaged. Rix had not seen it before the emergency issue. The clerk had not seen it used.

“Why charge replacement before inspection?”

“Because an account cannot close with an empty figure.”

“Then don't close it.”

The clerk sighed. He had the authority to accept a return, not to determine the cause of damage or forgive a debt. Behind him stood a rack of equipment awaiting the same judgment.

Rix pushed the docket back. “Mark liability disputed. I can sign for the return. I won't sign that I broke it.”

The clerk wrote **LIABILITY DISPUTED — INSPECTION PENDING**, pressed his seal and handed over a carbon copy. The money did not reappear. The record, at least, no longer described an allegation as an established fact.

---

At the Xanadu duty board, the foreman offered Rix an earlier tug slot.

“With what release gear?” Rix asked.

“The station issue.”

“Under inspection. Our own failed inspection. That's why we borrowed.”

“If we miss the berth, the hauler charges us.”

“If we move the load with an uncertified ram, we may have more to pay than a berth fee.”

The foreman rubbed his face. “I know. Don't talk as though I don't know what a release can do.”

Rix stopped. The foreman was not careless. He was trapped between a traffic fee due immediately and an equipment dispute that might take weeks. Neither man had authority over both accounts.

“Let me reposition the empty tug,” Rix said. “Keep the hauler's cargo slot. We take the load after maintenance signs the gear.”

“That needs another traffic movement.”

“Is it cheaper than losing the berth?”

The foreman checked, submitted the request, and received a conditional approval. It cost money and part of Rix's rest interval. Rix insisted that the additional duty hours be recorded. The foreman entered them.

He passed the berth where Holt had been injured without asking to see the sealed statements. He had not examined Holt's fitting. The borrowed ram was a separate item, and its disputed scoring proved nothing about the earlier accident.

---

The next-cycle infirmaria roster was complete except for the annex custodian's signature.

“Inventory duty,” Bethany told Arbentia. “Delayed, not absent.”

The trainee's displaced study hour remained booked for restoration. The outgoing sister had agreed to delay one meal, not to surrender future breaks. Bethany's emergency coverage was limited to the signed interval. Moving Arbentia's appointment would require changing all of those promises.

“Can I move the stable dressing reviews forward?”

“Yes. That doesn't create a second nurse.”

“I know.”

Bethany examined her. “You look ready to give away your sleep.”

“Only half.”

“Keep it. I need you conscious.”

The custodian arrived forty minutes late. Eighty minutes remained before the annex closed. Bethany checked that an experienced sister, not the trainee, would escort the scheduled oxygen cart through the crossing. The custodian stamped the reduced appointment. The trainee's owed hour was not reduced to match it.

The annex held no secret cure. Its pulmonary tables had been assembled under different rest intervals, exposure classes and employment conditions. One apparently successful follow-up group excluded workers whose paid berths had ended before the next review.

“Then the recovery figure is for men who could afford to stay,” Arbentia said.

“For men who remained in the recorded follow-up,” the custodian corrected. “The reason for every absence isn't known.”

Arbentia changed her note. She had almost mistaken missing records for healthy patients. She copied definitions and comparability limits, not identities or private charts. Nothing in the tables authorized a new prognosis for Holt.

At the closing bell the custodian stamped her supervised extract and retained a duplicate. Arbentia had obtained eighty minutes of genuine access, at a cost in other people's labor, and had found a question more useful than the easy answer she had wanted.

The crossing's rated fuse was still missing when she returned. The emergency strips had held during the previous interruption, and the manual watch was covering scheduled patient transfers. Station stores had not certified the generic insert on the incoming tender. Bethany refused an unsafe substitution and had the diverted maintenance hours recorded against the ward's service delays.

The lights held. The debt remained.

Arbentia placed her notes beside Holt's next review and waited for Abraxas to finish with his patient. No treatment would change until the ward had considered what she had actually learned.
