## Chapter VI — The Price of Borrowed Things

The borrowed release ram came back with a serviceable seal and a replacement charge.

Rix held the receipt beneath a flickering dock lumen. Emergency issue, off-berth return, inspection, and damage deposit appeared as four separate lines.

“That's more than the job paid.”

The equipment clerk looked at the lines. “The job still has an allowance.”

“An allowance you've put on hold.”

“I didn't put it anywhere. The system did when the damage report arrived.”

The ram had moved the first cargo load when the tug's own release gear failed inspection. Its handle was scored; the return hand had certified that it actuated under load, not that it was undamaged. Rix had not seen it before the emergency issue. The clerk had not seen it used.

“Why charge replacement before inspection?”

“Because an account cannot close with an empty figure.”

“Then don't close it.”

The clerk sighed. He had the authority to accept a return, not to determine the cause of damage or forgive a debt. Behind him stood a rack of equipment awaiting the same judgment.

Rix pushed the docket back. “Mark liability disputed. I can sign for the return. I won't sign that I broke it.”

The clerk wrote **LIABILITY DISPUTED — INSPECTION PENDING**, pressed his seal and handed over a carbon copy. The money did not reappear. The record, at least, no longer described an allegation as an established fact.

---

At the Xanadu duty board, the foreman offered Rix an earlier tug slot.

“With what release gear?” Rix asked.

“The station issue.”

“Under inspection. Our own failed inspection. That's why we borrowed.”

“If we miss the berth, the hauler charges us.”

“If we move the load with an uncertified ram, we may have more to pay than a berth fee.”

The foreman rubbed his face. “I know. Don't talk as though I don't know what a release can do.”

Rix stopped. The foreman was not careless. He was trapped between a traffic fee due immediately and an equipment dispute that might take weeks. Neither man had authority over both accounts.

“Let me reposition the empty tug,” Rix said. “Keep the hauler's cargo slot. We take the load after maintenance signs the gear.”

“That needs another traffic movement.”

“Is it cheaper than losing the berth?”

The foreman checked, submitted the request, and received a conditional approval. It cost money and part of Rix's rest interval. Rix insisted that the additional duty hours be recorded. The foreman entered them.

He passed the berth where Holt had been injured without asking to see the sealed statements. He had not examined Holt's fitting. The borrowed ram was a separate item, and its disputed scoring proved nothing about the earlier accident.

---

The next-cycle infirmaria roster was complete except for the annex custodian's signature.

“Inventory duty,” Bethany told Arbentia. “Delayed, not absent.”

The trainee's displaced study hour remained booked for restoration. The outgoing sister had agreed to delay one meal, not to surrender future breaks. Bethany's emergency coverage was limited to the signed interval. Moving Arbentia's appointment would require changing all of those promises.

“Can I move the stable dressing reviews forward?”

“Yes. That doesn't create a second nurse.”

“I know.”

Bethany examined her. “You look ready to give away your sleep.”

“Only half.”

“Keep it. I need you conscious.”

The custodian arrived forty minutes late. Eighty minutes remained before the annex closed. Bethany checked that an experienced sister, not the trainee, would escort the scheduled oxygen cart through the crossing. The custodian stamped the reduced appointment. The trainee's owed hour was not reduced to match it.

The annex held no secret cure. Its pulmonary tables had been assembled under different rest intervals, exposure classes and employment conditions. One apparently successful follow-up group excluded workers whose paid berths had ended before the next review.

“Then the recovery figure is for men who could afford to stay,” Arbentia said.

“For men who remained in the recorded follow-up,” the custodian corrected. “The reason for every absence isn't known.”

Arbentia changed her note. She had almost mistaken missing records for healthy patients. She copied definitions and comparability limits, not identities or private charts. Nothing in the tables authorized a new prognosis for Holt.

At the closing bell the custodian stamped her supervised extract and retained a duplicate. Arbentia had obtained eighty minutes of genuine access, at a cost in other people's labor, and had found a question more useful than the easy answer she had wanted.

The crossing's rated fuse was still missing when she returned. The emergency strips had held during the previous interruption, and the manual watch was covering scheduled patient transfers. Station stores had not certified the generic insert on the incoming tender. Bethany refused an unsafe substitution and had the diverted maintenance hours recorded against the ward's service delays.

The lights held. The debt remained.

Arbentia placed her notes beside Holt's next review and waited for Abraxas to finish with his patient. No treatment would change until the ward had considered what she had actually learned.

---

Abraxas read Arbentia's supervised extract without asking her to explain the columns. She had left space beside each definition for corrections. He used most of it.

"The figures are not false," he said. "Their denominators are different."

"That makes them difficult to use."

"It makes them dangerous to use carelessly."

He tapped the table for the longest follow-up interval. Patients who remained in paid convalescent berths appeared again and again. Those transferred, discharged to private lodgings, or removed from company rolls disappeared into a category labelled *unavailable for review*. Nothing in Arbentia's extract established whether they had recovered, died, or returned to work.

She had wanted an answer to Holt's question. Instead she had found a record of the people who could afford to keep asking it.

"Can we request the original charts?"

"Not under your allocation. Nor mine without a different authority and a stated purpose."

"I don't need their names."

"Then ask whether the annex can distinguish transfer, discharge and nonattendance in its aggregate returns."

He drew three boxes in the margin. Arbentia had been treating the blank spaces as an obstacle to overcome. Abraxas wanted to know whether the information existed at all.

"Would you use any of this for Holt?"

"Not to predict his recovery. I would use it to reject a prediction made from the larger number."

Bethany came in carrying the transfer ledger. The crossing watch had covered another scheduled oxygen-cart passage. The worker assigned to it had missed another maintenance task. The clerk had entered that time against the open fuse requisition.

"Are you changing his treatment?" Bethany asked.

"No," Abraxas said.

"Good. I have no second pair of lungs to issue him if the first trial fails."

Arbentia held out the extract. Bethany read the headings and annotations, not the whole table.

"We could at least record whether Holt comes back after the company stops paying," Arbentia said.

"We can record whether he returns for an appointment we have authority to offer. We cannot follow every man who leaves the station."

"One additional review, then. Requested through the ordinary book."

Bethany studied the duty slate. Neither she nor Abraxas supplied a convenient extra nurse.

"Put the request in," Bethany said. "Tell him that a request is not an appointment."

Arbentia wrote it that way. The trainee's restored study hour remained on the next-cycle slate. She checked that too.

---

The equipment inspector returned Rix's borrowed release ram to the cage. Its scored handle was real. The tool still actuated under the test load, but the old issue entries did not describe its condition well enough to date the scoring.

The inspector would not certify that the damage predated Rix's use. Nor would he certify that Rix caused it.

The replacement assessment was suspended pending review. The inspection fee remained, as did the traffic charge for repositioning the empty tug and the hours Rix had spent waiting for certified gear.

"You can take the undisputed allowance now," the clerk told him.

The sum would buy a decent meal. It would not clear the older equipment debt that had made the job worth taking.

"The deposit?"

"Still held."

"And if the earlier users can't be found?"

"Then the account goes to the dispute desk."

Rix looked at the queue number and sighed. The clerk had no power to settle liability. Rix had no evidence with which to settle it for him.

"Release the part you can," he said. "Keep the rest disputed."

The clerk stamped the paper. Rix checked that his additional duty hours remained recorded before leaving. He had not been paid for them. At least they had not disappeared.

At the duty board a new tug watch had been offered, contingent on a certified ram arriving before the berth window. It overlapped the hour Rix had hoped to spend with Arbentia.

He accepted on the condition that the gear be signed out before he took the tug. Then he sent her a message saying he would be late.

He did not mention Holt's sealed statements. He had not seen them. The disputed borrowed ram was not the fitting that had injured Holt, and its inspection settled nothing about the accident.

He had found a replacement knob for Arbentia's receiver. He hoped it would fit.

---

Holt's next review took longer than the printed appointment allowed.

The first measurement followed the prescribed rest. The second followed a short supervised walk. Arbentia made him sit before he asked. He was angry about it, but his breathing was already growing shallow.

"Did the old books tell you how long this lasts?" he asked.

"Not for you."

"Not even roughly?"

"Not in a way I would trust."

He turned his bandaged hand over. The new skin pulled when he moved his fingers. Bethany had warned him that a graft did not become comfortable merely because it was healing.

"The company wants a date."

"I know."

"They say the berth is a kindness until the claim is decided."

"Did they put that in writing?"

"They put the charge in writing."

He unfolded an account sheet. The convalescent berth appeared as an accumulating advance, but the column for a final liability decision was blank. Arbentia understood why the clerk had wanted a fitness date. Every day Holt remained there made the unresolved sum larger.

She could not strike the charge out. She could not rule on the release fitting or the accident. She could record the conditions under which Holt failed the exertion test, and keep the next assessment from becoming a simple count of steps.

"Will you let us send the same limited restriction again?"

He watched her for a long moment.

"Will they see what you wrote about the pain?"

"No. Only what you agree to send."

"Then send it. And tell them I tried."

"I'll record what you did. I won't call it failure of effort."

That seemed to matter to him. He signed with his uninjured hand. Arbentia made one copy for him and another for the dispatch book. The additional review remained a request, subject to authorization. She told him before he left.

Abraxas accepted the standardized measurements without claiming that the archive had improved Holt's prognosis. Bethany inspected the graft and confirmed the rest limit. Their work agreed where it needed to, without either practitioner surrendering an argument about what healing meant.

The tender's general stores arrived without the rated secondary-feed fuse. Maintenance recorded the absence against the existing issue number and confirmed the crossing watch for the next scheduled bed transfer. Bethany required a fresh staffing review at the next change of watch. She would not allow a temporary precaution to become an uncounted permanent post.

No one died at the crossing. Someone missed another task to keep it safe. Both facts belonged in the record.

---

Rix came to Arbentia's quarters after the last cargo alarm, carrying the receiver knob in a square of cloth.

It was the wrong size.

He held it against the spindle, turned it once, and stopped before forcing it.

"Almost," he said.

"That is what you said about the recaf."

"The recaf was excellent."

"The recaf was expensive."

"That is not a contradiction."

She sat on the edge of the narrow bed and watched him wrap the knob again. His hands were stained with dock grease. The pale burn on one knuckle had opened, but he had covered it with a clean strip. She did not turn their evening into another examination.

"How much did they pay you?"

He gave her the smaller figure, the amount he had actually received. He did not call the full job allowance wages.

"That bad?"

"Not ruined. Not paid."

He showed her the carbon copy. She read the disputed lines and handed it back. He had not brought a secret fitting report or an accusation against Holt. She was relieved to discover she had expected neither.

"I had eighty minutes in the annex," she said.

"Did you find your answer?"

"I found out that some of the men counted as recovered had somewhere to stay."

"And the others?"

"They stopped being counted."

He was quiet. He understood a ledger that ceased following a person when the person ceased being profitable. It was not the same ledger that held his disputed ram. The resemblance needed no explanation.

"Can you do anything?"

"Ask a narrower question. Make another request. Keep one man from being declared fit because he walked farther before he had to sit."

"That sounds like work."

"It is."

Rix put the cloth-wrapped knob beside the receiver and took her hand.

"I took another watch," he said. "I should have told you before I accepted."

"You did tell me."

"After."

"Yes."

"I needed the credit."

"I know."

She could have said she needed the hour with him. She did. That did not make his debt disappear, or her next watch shorter. She leaned against his shoulder and let the disappointment remain ordinary.

The receiver played a thin, wavering passage of the old low-g music. Rix reached for the switch.

"Leave it," she said.

He left it.

Beyond her door the station carried its injured and its cargo, its unpaid allowances and unfilled requisitions, its sleeping workers and those who had agreed to remain awake. Nothing in Arbentia's little room had been repaired. She had eighty minutes of notes, a wrong-sized knob, and a man who had come despite having to rise again too soon.

For tonight, she kept the music on.
