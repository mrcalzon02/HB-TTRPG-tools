# Antegra Station

## Continuation V — The Reasonable Men

The first thing Governor Talbor Varik did with his remaining nineteen hours was order breakfast.

Captain Ardan Krell stared at him.

“My lord.”

“Yes?”

“The station may be nineteen hours from extermination.”

“Which is precisely why I refuse to meet the people considering it while hungry.”

Commissar-Keeper Dren Solvik looked at the severance notice.

“You assume they intend a meeting.”

“No. I intend a meeting. Their intentions are presently sealed, which I am beginning to regard as a personality defect.”

Senior Reconciliator Halbrecht Senn laughed once.

It became a cough halfway through.

Nobody commented.

They had returned to Central Audit because Substructure XVIII had finally stopped attempting to reconcile the memories of several million citizens into something that might answer back. The southern heating trunk was functioning. The northern habitation stacks were climbing above emergency minimum. Three hundred and eleven thousand citizens remained under thermal rationing.

This qualified as good news.

Antegra had unusually strict standards for optimism.

Varik stood over a table buried beneath sixty-eight years of lies.

Most governments concealed their crimes in locked vaults.

Antegra had concealed its survival in triplicate.

Selene Marr entered carrying a tray.

Krell looked at it.

“That is breakfast?”

“Officially.”

“What is it?”

“Protein.”

“What kind?”

“The requisition form did not ask.”

Varik took a grey square.

It tasted faintly of pepper and strongly of administrative surrender.

“Status.”

Marr placed a data-slate beside the food.

“Varik-Omega transmitted on an astropathic channel that does not appear in the station registry.”

“Of course it doesn't.”

“The receiving acknowledgement carries an Ordo cipher family.”

Solvik looked up.

“Which Ordo?”

Marr hesitated.

“That is the difficulty.”

Krell sighed.

“We have several.”

“The cipher is valid for three.”

Silence settled over the table.

Varik put down his breakfast.

“Explain.”

“The outer seal authenticates as Ordo Xenos. The transit authority authenticates as Ordo Hereticus. The internal custody mark authenticates as Ordo Malleus.”

Krell rubbed his face.

“That seems excessive.”

Senn spoke from his chair.

“It was considered prudent.”

“By whom?”

“Reasonable men.”

Solvik's expression hardened.

“I have never seen three Ordos agree on anything.”

Senn nodded.

“That is why we were frightened.”

Varik looked at him.

“You remember the original review.”

“I remember pieces.”

“You have spent sixty-eight years claiming otherwise.”

“I spent sixty-eight years following your orders.”

“My orders appear increasingly irritating.”

“They were extremely thorough.”

“Was I always like this?”

Krell answered before Senn could.

“Yes.”

Varik ignored him.

Senn folded his artificial hands.

“The first investigators could not agree what the vault represented. Xenos believed the memories originated from a nonhuman civilization. Hereticus believed they represented contamination of Imperial identity. Malleus believed the act of remembering might itself be an invocation.”

“And their compromise?”

“Do not let anyone remember enough to find out.”

Marr stared at him.

“That was the official policy?”

“No. The official policy was nine hundred and twelve pages.”

Krell nodded gravely.

“Naturally.”

“The meaning was shorter.”

Varik turned to the station schematic.

“Then we prepare for people who may arrive expecting to kill everyone.”

Solvik's hand rested on his holster.

“How?”

“By behaving as though they are reasonable.”

Krell looked offended.

“My lord, there are limits to optimism.”

“Not reasonable in disposition. Reasonable in procedure.”

“That is worse.”

Varik enlarged the docking rings.

“Any authority contemplating severance will require evidence before expenditure. Killing a station is easy. Explaining why the station's tithe disappears afterward is harder.”

Marr considered that.

“The tithe fraud protects us.”

“The honest tithe protects us.”

Senn's head rose.

Varik tapped the population ledger.

“For sixty-eight years Antegra understated its population to keep memory carriers here. We now possess the real census.”

Solvik understood first.

“If they sever the station, they must report the actual loss.”

“Precisely.”

Krell smiled without humor.

“Eight million dead citizens looks worse than five million.”

“Eight million, four hundred and twelve thousand, six hundred and nine.”

Marr corrected him automatically.

Everyone looked at her.

She shrugged.

“We spent all night counting them.”

Varik pointed at her.

“That is why you are attending.”

“Attending what?”

“The hearing.”

“There is a hearing?”

“There will be.”

Solvik frowned.

“You cannot compel the Holy Ordos to hold one.”

“No. But I can make proceeding without one administratively embarrassing.”

Senn closed his eyes.

“There he is.”

Varik looked at him.

“What?”

“The man who built the Department.”

The words landed harder than Varik expected.

For a moment he saw violet sky again.

Seven moons.

White towers.

Something moving behind them.

He tasted blood.

Krell caught his shoulder.

“My lord.”

Varik realized he had bitten through the inside of his cheek.

“I am fine.”

“You are bleeding.”

“I have governed Antegra for two centuries. This is not the first meeting to cause that.”

Senn leaned forward.

“What did you see?”

“The city.”

“Anything new?”

Varik looked at the schematic.

A name hovered behind his teeth.

He did not speak it.

“There were people in the street.”

Senn went still.

Krell noticed.

“That is new.”

“Yes.”

“What were they doing?”

Varik shut his eyes.

The memory waited.

It was not like recollection.

It was like a room whose door had always been open.

Men and women stood beneath the seven moons.

Human in shape.

Human enough.

Every one of them faced the same direction.

Toward the windowless tower.

“They were waiting.”

“For what?” Solvik asked.

Varik opened his eyes.

“For someone to remember them.”

Nobody touched breakfast after that.

---

Twelve hours remained when the first external augur contact appeared.

It was not a warship.

That worried Krell more.

The vessel translated at the edge of Antegra's controlled volume without escort, heraldry, or transponder flourish. It was narrow, dark, and old enough that its armor had acquired the uneven texture of repeated repairs.

Marr read the return twice.

“Administratum courier displacement.”

Krell stared at the tactical display.

“That is not an Administratum courier.”

“No.”

“What is it?”

“The transponder says *Quietus Ratio*.”

Senn whispered something.

Varik turned.

“What?”

“I know that ship.”

“From sixty-eight years ago?”

“No.”

Senn's prosthetic fingers tightened against the chair.

“From before.”

The bridge vox chimed.

One clean tone.

Then another.

The station's command systems accepted an incoming priority handshake without asking permission.

Krell's expression became murderous.

“I dislike that.”

“So do I.”

A voice filled Central Audit.

It was an old woman's voice, calm and almost warm.

“Antegra Station, this is the Imperial vessel *Quietus Ratio*. By authority of the sealed Severance Concordat, prepare Docking Reliquary Three.”

Varik looked at Marr.

“We do not have a Docking Reliquary Three.”

Marr was already searching.

Her face changed.

“We do.”

“Where?”

“Inside the station.”

Krell blinked.

“That is not how docks work.”

“Apparently ours has been keeping secrets from you.”

The voice continued.

“Governor Talbor Varik is to attend personally. Captain Ardan Krell may accompany him. Commissar-Keeper Dren Solvik will surrender his sidearm before entry.”

Solvik stared at the vox grille.

“No.”

There was a pause.

The old woman answered.

“That response is recorded.”

Solvik looked at Varik.

“I dislike her.”

“You have known her for seven seconds.”

“I am efficient.”

The voice continued.

“Senior Reconciliator Halbrecht Senn is ordered to remain where he is.”

Senn's face went pale.

Varik noticed.

“You know her.”

Senn said nothing.

The vox clicked off.

Varik waited.

Senn looked at the floor.

“At the first review,” he said, “there were five observers.”

“How many left?”

“Four.”

“And she was one of them.”

“Yes.”

“What is her name?”

Senn looked up.

“Legate-Inquisitor Aveline Rhyse.”

Krell folded his arms.

“Ordo?”

“That depends who is asking.”

“Wonderful.”

Varik took his coat from the chair.

Senn stood too quickly.

His artificial knee locked.

He nearly fell.

Krell caught him.

“Governor.”

Senn's voice had changed.

There was no dry humor in it now.

“If Rhyse is here, do not let her see the city.”

Varik froze.

“She already knows about it.”

“No.”

Senn gripped his sleeve.

“She knows there is a memory.”

“That is not the same thing?”

“No.”

The old reconciliator's eyes were wet.

“Last time, none of us could see the people.”

---

Docking Reliquary Three lay behind a wall that had been listed for one hundred and forty-three years as structural ballast.

Marr found the release mechanism beneath a brass plaque commemorating a sanitation refurbishment that had never occurred.

Krell regarded the plaque.

“Appropriate.”

“What is?”

“The door to our secret Inquisitorial dock is hidden behind a fictional sewer project.”

Varik stepped through.

“On Antegra that may qualify as camouflage.”

The reliquary was a cylindrical chamber large enough to swallow a frigate's launch bay. Ancient docking machinery hung from the walls. Servo arms unfolded from alcoves where no alcoves appeared on any station plan.

At the center waited a black shuttle.

No heraldry.

No escort.

No visible weapons.

Krell counted six anyway.

The shuttle door opened.

Three people emerged.

The first was a woman in plain grey robes with white hair bound behind her head. She walked with a wooden cane.

The second was a hooded adept carrying a case chained to both wrists.

The third was a child.

Varik stopped.

The child could not have been older than nine.

She wore a simple black dress and no shoes.

Her eyes were covered by a strip of red cloth.

Krell's hand moved toward his weapon.

The old woman noticed.

“Captain Krell.”

“You know me.”

“I know your file.”

“That is less comforting.”

She smiled faintly.

“Good.”

Varik stepped forward.

“Legate-Inquisitor Rhyse.”

“Governor Varik.”

“You look well for a woman Senn remembers from sixty-eight years ago.”

“So do you.”

“I pay people for that.”

“So do I.”

For the first time that day, Varik liked someone immediately.

He distrusted the feeling on principle.

Rhyse looked past him.

“Where is Solvik?”

“Refusing to surrender his sidearm.”

“Still?”

“You expected otherwise?”

“No.”

She tapped her cane once against the deck.

The blindfolded child turned her head toward Varik.

Varik felt the city return.

Violet horizon.

Seven moons.

White towers.

Waiting people.

The child whispered.

“Oh.”

Rhyse's smile vanished.

The chained adept took one step backward.

Krell drew his pistol.

Varik did not.

The child raised one hand and pointed directly at him.

“He opened the door.”

Rhyse stared at Varik.

“What door?”

The girl smiled beneath the red blindfold.

“The one they built the station around.”

Deep beneath Antegra, something struck the inside of Substructure XVIII.

Once.

The deck moved beneath their feet.

Dust fell from the hidden dock's ceiling.

Then came a second impact.

Slower.

Heavier.

Patient.

Rhyse looked toward the floor.

Varik watched sixty-eight years of careful policy collapse behind her eyes.

He found that strangely reassuring.

At least one reasonable person had finally arrived.

Unfortunately, she appeared to be terrified.
