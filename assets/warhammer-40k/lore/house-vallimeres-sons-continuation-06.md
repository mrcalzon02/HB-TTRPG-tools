# House Vallimere's Sons

## Continuation VI — The Test Outside the Room

The fleet instruction office sent forty-seven pages of conditions under which it would accept an answer to one question.

**DOES THE REVISED PILOT PRODUCE BETTER OFFICERS?**

Prite read it over breakfast.

"No," he said.

Renault looked up. "That was quick."

"We cannot prove it."

Darcelle took the pages. "We can prove the new timing display works."

"That's not the same thing."

"Or that the cadets explain their decisions better."

"Also not the same thing."

The fleet office wanted an independent examination: unfamiliar scenarios, sealed command logs, instructors who had not helped build the pilot, and no comparison between cadets and officers with twenty years at sea. The last condition was particularly irritating because it was reasonable.

"They want to know whether the lesson survives a room we didn't build," Prite said.

Vane appeared at the doorway with the observer list. House Caldrake had requested a seat. No duel petition accompanied the request.

"So far," Vane said when Renault asked.

---

The independent examination occupied two simulation halls.

The first squadron had to protect an orbital depot while a civilian carrier unloaded replacement reactor shielding. A damaged escort could investigate a suspected raider. The depot commander, however, controlled his own traffic corridor and could refuse passage.

The cadet commanding the squadron requested access. The depot refused.

He repeated the request. The depot refused again.

The cadet spent two more minutes composing an order invoking squadron authority over a command that did not answer to him. By the time he redirected his escort, the corridor had closed. The raider struck the unloading gantry. The carrier lost its remaining cargo, though the depot retained its defense craft.

Renault watched from the gallery.

"He knew the boundary."

"He knew the regulation," Prite said. "That's different."

In the other hall, a convoy commander requested an independent mine tender to sweep an uncertain approach. She asked how long the tender could remain available and set a decision point before the harbor window closed.

Darcelle watched the timestamps.

"She remembered."

"Yes."

The tender found no mines. The cadet proceeded, relieved that one uncertainty had disappeared. During the delay an enemy pursuit craft changed position. It damaged the convoy's last lighter on the approach.

"She didn't update the enemy picture," Renault said.

"No," Prite answered.

"After doing everything we taught."

Prite said nothing. He had nearly praised the cadet himself.

---

The fleet evaluator interviewed each commander before showing the after-action record.

The first cadet admitted he had known the depot could refuse passage.

"Then why did you repeat the request?" the evaluator asked.

"I thought operational necessity would persuade him."

"Did it?"

"No, sir."

"What did your third message achieve?"

A silence.

"I spent the last useful minutes trying to make another officer change his authority."

The second cadet remembered the mine tender's report.

"Why did you proceed without a new enemy contact?" the evaluator asked.

"I thought the important uncertainty had been settled."

"Was it?"

"Only the mine warning, sir."

"Then?"

"I stopped updating the problem."

Prite thought both answers worth having. Neither brought back the carrier's cargo or repaired the damaged lighter.

The evaluator sealed his assessments before revealing which cadets had received the pilot.

Both had.

Two conventional-course teams had run different, equivalent exercises in a third hall. One preserved its convoy through a costly diversion. The other misunderstood a depot closure and lost its carrier. The pilot cadets reconstructed their mistakes more precisely; they did not produce uniformly better tactical outcomes.

"Four exercises," the evaluator said. "Not enough to separate teaching from individual ability, scenario variation or the knowledge of being observed."

Renault read the figures again.

"So what have we proved?"

"That two of your cadets can still make mistakes."

"We knew that."

"Then the Academy need not pay to establish it again."

The evaluator's face was almost expressionless. Almost.

---

Darcelle spread the records across a maintenance bench beneath Hall Four.

"The simulator treats other commands as messages," he said. "If the depot refuses, the cadet assumes there's another request that will unlock the route. If the tender agrees, the cadet assumes that part of the problem is finished."

Prite examined the order log. Darcelle was right. They had trained cadets to account for the cost of delay, but not to treat another competent commander as someone with independent obligations.

"We could add a score," Darcelle suggested.

"No."

He looked at Prite.

"We're not building another number for them to chase."

"Then what?"

"Put an officer on the other side of the channel. Give him a real brief, authority to refuse, and reasons to do it."

"That will need more instructors."

"Yes."

"And independent records for appeals."

"Yes."

Darcelle sighed. "Curriculum will love us."

Renault appeared on the stair.

"Only if we provide a rubric for how much."

Prite smiled despite himself.

---

The Headmaster received their report with the fleet evaluator present and Vane guarding the door.

"Can you demonstrate better officers?" he asked.

"No," Prite said.

"Good. What can you demonstrate?"

Darcelle explained the difference between recorded decisions and independent authority. His voice did not shake.

"The simulation can make a depot refuse," he said, "but it cannot give the depot commander a reason to keep refusing after the cadet asks the right question. We are teaching them that other people's authority is a puzzle with a solution."

The fleet evaluator considered this.

"A human officer could still be unreasonable."

"Yes," Darcelle said. "Then the cadet must deal with that too."

The Headmaster approved a limited joint trial for the next review period, not a general curriculum change. The conventional course would remain. The pilot would continue through the term. No old marks would be reopened. Fleet instruction would design the independent assessment.

"One condition," the Headmaster said. "Do not call it Lesson Four until it survives the test."

Prite opened his slate beneath the three existing principles.

**Who is allowed to say no?**

He did not number it.

Outside, cadets crossed the Academy court arguing about marks and examinations. House Caldrake's observer had requested the signed findings, not the sealed raw records. Vane confirmed that the observer terms allowed the former and not the latter.

A year ago Prite would have considered that distinction an administrative nuisance. Now it was the difference between public accountability and a noble House acquiring a cadet's private command history.

The fleet had asked whether the Academy was producing better officers. It had received four exercises, an inconclusive report and a proposal that would consume more staff time than anyone had budgeted.

It was not victory.

It was a problem they could no longer pretend did not exist.
