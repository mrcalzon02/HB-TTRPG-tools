# Antegra Station

## Continuation XXVII — The Intended Recipient

*An original Cafarron Corridor chronicle continuation. Warhammer 40,000 and associated marks and setting elements are the property of Games Workshop. This non-commercial fan work is not endorsed by or affiliated with Games Workshop.*

The name took eleven minutes to arrive.

Selene Marr spent all eleven refusing to look at the door.

This was not cowardice.

It was procedure.

Cowardice, in Marr's experience, generally involved less paperwork.

Dock Fourteen had settled into the particular silence of a room in which everyone knew something terrible was happening and had privately decided that staring at it would constitute encouragement.

The red maintenance door remained shut.

Behind it, a pen scratched across paper.

Slowly.

Deliberately.

Scratch.

Pause.

Scratch.

Captain Ardan Krell watched the door with his pistol resting against one thigh.

"How long does a name take?"

"Depends on the name," Marr said.

"Mine takes four letters."

"Your name is not presently holding open a nine-century hearing."

"I knew there was a reason I liked it."

Young Talbor Varik stood beside his older self, which remained an arrangement neither man enjoyed.

Old Varik held the first-hearing docket against his chest.

The page still displayed three dates.

Seventy-one years ago.

Nine hundred and eleven years ago.

Tomorrow.

Tomorrow had begun to feel less like a date than a threat.

The brass automaton waited at its table.

Its stamp rested on the docket.

Respondent Prior stood motionless beside the red door, its outline refusing to settle into a single posture.

Sometimes it faced Marr.

Sometimes the door.

Once, for perhaps half a second, it was not there at all.

The station clocks disagreed by seventeen minutes, three hours, two days, and one clock in Stores Annex C that insisted it was the Feast of the Emperor's Ascension and would not be corrected.

Marr found this reassuring.

Agreement had become suspicious.

The printer chattered.

FROM: ORIGIN

RECIPIENT PREPARATION STATUS?

Marr tore off the sheet.

"Pending."

The printer immediately produced another.

RESPONSE NOTED.

DEFINE PENDING.

"No."

A pause.

Then:

RESPONSE NOTED.

Krell glanced at her.

"Can you simply refuse to answer Origin?"

"Apparently."

"Useful."

"Do not get ambitious."

The scratching stopped.

Every person in Dock Fourteen looked at the red door.

Marr sighed.

"So much for procedure."

Something slid beneath the door.

A strip of paper.

Not the receipt.

Not a docket.

A narrow filing label, yellow with age.

Rhyse approached it first, pistol drawn.

"Do not shoot the stationery," Marr said.

"I remember."

"You looked tempted."

"I remain adaptable."

Rhyse nudged the label with one boot.

Nothing happened.

Vhal scanned it.

"No detectable energetic, chemical, biological, radiological, warp-reactive, or noospheric hazard."

Krell nodded.

"So it's definitely dangerous."

Marr picked it up.

The label contained one typed field.

RECIPIENT CAPACITY:

Below it, handwritten:

THE ONE WHO CAN SAY NO.

Nobody spoke.

Marr read it again.

"That is not a name."

The automaton stamped.

CORRECT.

"It is not even an identity."

STAMP.

CORRECT.

"It is a function."

The automaton hesitated.

STAMP.

CAPACITY.

Marr looked at the seventh seat on the ancient plan.

Recipient.

The one who could say no.

Respondent Prior shifted.

For the first time Marr understood why it had wanted inside.

Alternatives were meaningless if no one could reject the answer imposed upon them.

The Collector reconciled.

The dead-letter mechanism disposed.

Origin sent.

The court adjudicated.

But somewhere in the machinery there had to be a point at which a message could be received and refused.

A person—or something occupying the legal capacity of one—who could say no and make the refusal matter.

Krell reached the same conclusion more quickly than Marr expected.

"So the seat is for whoever can refuse the petition."

"Not whoever," Marr said.

"Why not?"

"Because if capacity alone were sufficient, the Collector could have manufactured a refusal centuries ago. Or an acceptance. The seat is reserved. That means the court recognizes a particular recipient, even if the name is missing."

Old Varik opened the first-hearing docket.

"At the other hearing, the seat was empty."

"Misdelivered," Marr corrected.

"Yes."

"Did anyone address it?"

Varik's face changed.

Marr saw memory arrive badly.

"Someone did."

Young Varik straightened.

"Who?"

Old Varik looked at Marr.

"You."

Dock Fourteen became quiet again.

Marr rubbed the bridge of her nose.

"Naturally."

"You were standing where you are now."

"I was not alive nine hundred and eleven years ago."

"You said that then too."

Krell looked between them.

"I am beginning to dislike testimony that anticipates objections."

Old Varik continued.

"You looked at the empty seat and said: 'My client is not present.'"

Marr stopped rubbing her nose.

The unidentified beacon outside pulsed once.

The automaton stamped.

COUNSEL RELATIONSHIP ASSERTED.

"I have asserted no such thing."

STAMP.

ASSERTION PREVIOUSLY FILED.

"By whom?"

The stamp came down hard enough to dent the table.

YOU.

Marr stared at it.

"When?"

The automaton rotated its head toward the first-hearing docket.

Old Varik answered for it.

"Tomorrow."

Krell muttered something devotional and anatomically improbable.

Marr ignored him.

"No."

The word came out quietly.

Respondent Prior moved closer.

The red door knocked once.

Marr held up the filing label.

"This says the recipient is the one who can say no. It does not say I represent that recipient."

The printer activated.

FROM: ORIGIN

COUNSEL OF RECORD: SELENE MARR.

CLIENT: ____________________

"Blank," Marr said.

The printer answered.

CORRECT.

"Then no client has been established."

CORRECT.

"Then I cannot prepare a recipient whose identity is unknown."

The printer was silent for seven seconds.

Then:

COUNSEL MAY IDENTIFY CLIENT.

Marr laughed once.

It was not a happy sound.

"Absolutely not."

The dead-letter darkness beyond the perimeter pressed inward.

The unidentified ships adjusted formation.

Krell glanced toward the observation slit.

"Was that significant?"

Vhal checked the auspex.

"Perimeter contracted by approximately eleven kilometres."

"So yes."

Marr set the label on the automaton's table.

"The court will note that Origin has invited counsel to choose the recipient."

STAMP.

NOTED.

"And that choosing the recipient would allow counsel to determine who possesses authority to accept or refuse the petition."

STAMP.

NOTED.

"Which would permit counsel to decide the outcome by selecting a compliant client."

The automaton's gears slowed.

STAMP.

CONFLICT.

"Precisely."

Krell looked almost impressed.

"You found a conflict of interest with yourself."

"It was inevitable."

"I admire your commitment."

"Don't."

The printer began feeding paper continuously.

COUNSEL REQUIRED.

RECIPIENT REQUIRED.

DELIVERY REQUIRED.

HEARING REQUIRED.

The same four lines repeated.

Again.

Again.

Again.

Then a fifth line appeared.

REFUSAL REQUIRED.

Marr tore the paper free.

"There."

Old Varik frowned.

"There what?"

"The petition was not sent merely to be delivered."

She put the page beside the ancient receipt.

"Someone expected it to be refused."

Respondent Prior struck the deck with one foot.

The sound echoed farther than Dock Fourteen allowed.

The Collector appeared in every reflective surface.

Not physically.

Not yet.

Black glass filled the observation slit.

Its shape occupied the polished casing of the automaton.

Its hands appeared in the reflection of Krell's pistol.

Every image looked toward the seventh seat.

The station clocks began agreeing.

Marr spoke quickly.

"Objection. Premature manifestation."

STAMP.

SUSTAINED.

The reflections shattered into ordinary reflections.

Krell inspected his pistol.

"I miss enemies with organs."

"You say that now."

The red door opened.

Not one centimetre this time.

Fully.

Beyond it was not a maintenance corridor.

There was a room.

A small one.

Imperial in construction, though old enough that its proportions belonged to an architectural standard Antegra's enginseers would have considered archaic and therefore sacred.

One lumen burned overhead.

Seven chairs surrounded a narrow table.

Six were occupied.

Marr could not see by whom.

The seventh faced the doorway.

Empty.

On the table before it sat a nameplate.

The letters were hidden beneath a sheet of paper.

Respondent Prior stepped aside.

The automaton stamped.

VENUE ACCESS GRANTED.

Marr did not move.

"To whom?"

STAMP.

COUNSEL.

"And my client?"

The automaton's stamp hovered.

For once, no answer came.

Then the room beyond the door supplied one.

A voice.

Human.

Tired.

Very far away.

"Not yet."

Old Varik went white.

"That's the voice."

Marr looked at him.

"Whose?"

"The one from the first hearing."

"The recipient?"

Varik shook his head.

"No."

The voice spoke again.

"Counsel will enter. Witness will remain."

Marr looked at the automaton.

"Authority?"

The little machine stamped.

DISPUTED.

"Comforting."

She stepped through the red door.

Krell moved to follow.

The doorway lengthened between them.

He stopped.

"Marr."

"I noticed."

"If it closes?"

"File an objection."

"I was thinking explosives."

"That is why I am counsel."

The door shut.

The room was colder on the other side.

The six occupied chairs were not occupied by people.

They were occupied by records.

Stacks of paper.

Thousands of sheets compressed into human silhouettes, bound with cord and seals, each seated where a participant should have been.

PETITIONER.

COUNSEL.

WITNESS.

ADJUDICATOR.

RESPONDENT.

RESPONDENT PRIOR.

The seventh chair remained empty.

RECIPIENT.

Marr approached the table.

The paper figure in the adjudicator's chair moved.

Pages turned inside it like breathing.

"Counsel Selene Marr."

"Allegedly."

"You have delayed delivery."

"Successfully."

"You have prevented substitution."

"Correct."

"You have preserved the recipient's right of refusal."

Marr paused.

"Apparently."

The paper figure leaned forward.

"Then you have prepared the recipient."

"No. I have prepared the seat."

A long silence.

Somewhere in the walls, machinery older than Antegra turned once.

The paper figure sat back.

"Distinction accepted."

Marr exhaled.

"Good. Now identify my alleged client."

The adjudicator raised one paper hand and pointed to the covered nameplate.

"Counsel may inspect."

Marr did not touch it.

"Why me?"

"Because you refused to choose."

"That is not an answer."

"It is the qualification."

Marr stared at the covered plate.

The room smelled of dust and ink.

Beyond the walls she could hear something enormous moving around the perimeter of the venue.

The dead-letter mechanism, perhaps.

Or the Collector.

Or something for which both were merely departments.

She lifted the sheet.

There was no name beneath it.

There was a sentence.

THE RECIPIENT IS THE FIRST PERSON WHO WAS NEVER ASKED.

Marr read it twice.

"That is still not an identity."

The adjudicator's pages rustled.

"Correct."

"Who was never asked what?"

The six paper figures answered together.

"Whether Antegra Station should exist."

Marr felt the cold settle into her bones.

Nine hundred and eleven years of petitions.

Seventy-one years of appeals.

Contradictory stations.

Dead citizens who had never existed.

Variks multiplying across histories.

The Collector forcing possibility into certainty.

Respondent Prior keeping alternatives alive.

Origin sending and resending the same impossible question.

And somewhere beneath all of it, a person who had not been consulted.

"A citizen?"

The adjudicator did not answer.

"An inhabitant?"

Silence.

"Someone aboard the original station?"

The paper figure tilted its head.

"Question assumes an original station."

Marr closed her eyes.

"Of course it does."

The table shook.

A black line appeared beneath the door.

The Collector had found the venue.

The paper figures began losing pages.

One sheet at a time, drawn toward the black line.

The adjudicator spoke faster.

"Delivery attempt resumes tomorrow."

"I know."

"Recipient must be present."

"I know."

"Counsel must produce recipient."

"You have not given me a name."

"A name would reconcile identity."

Marr opened her eyes.

There it was.

The trap.

A named recipient would become singular.

Singular could be found.

Found could be reconciled.

Reconciled could be replaced.

The Collector did not need the correct person.

It needed a person definite enough to overwrite.

"So I am not looking for a name."

The adjudicator's paper face folded into something almost like approval.

"Counsel understands."

"I am looking for someone who satisfies the capacity without being reduced to a singular identity."

"Counsel understands."

"Someone Antegra failed to ask."

The black line widened.

The RESPONDENT paper figure collapsed into loose pages.

The Collector's hand emerged beneath the door.

Marr backed away.

"One more question."

"Proceed."

"How many people satisfy the capacity?"

The adjudicator answered immediately.

"All of them."

Marr stopped.

The Collector stopped too.

For one impossible instant, she had the distinct impression it had not expected that answer.

"All Antegra citizens?"

"All persons affected by the petition who were never asked whether its consequence should exist."

"How many?"

The paper adjudicator began shedding pages.

"Identity disputed."

Marr smiled.

It was the first pleasant thing she had heard all day.

"Excellent."

The Collector forced the door inward.

Marr seized the blank nameplate.

"Counsel removes exhibit pending recipient identification."

The adjudicator stamped the table with a paper fist.

AUTHORIZED.

Marr ran.

The red door opened before her.

She fell through into Dock Fourteen as black fingers closed on empty air behind her.

Krell caught her by one arm.

Rhyse caught the nameplate.

The door slammed.

Respondent Prior drove both hands against it.

The Collector struck from the other side.

The entire station shuddered.

Krell hauled Marr upright.

"Did you find your client?"

Marr took the nameplate from Rhyse.

"Yes."

Old Varik stared at her.

"Who?"

"Everyone."

Young Varik frowned.

"That is not a person."

"Precisely."

The automaton stamped.

RECIPIENT IDENTITY?

Marr placed the blank nameplate on its table.

"Disputed."

STAMP.

RECIPIENT CLASS?

"All persons materially affected by the petition who were not afforded prior opportunity to accept or refuse its consequence."

The automaton's gears screamed.

It had not been built for class actions.

This pleased Marr enormously.

The printer activated.

FROM: ORIGIN

RECIPIENT MUST BE SINGULAR.

Marr wrote beneath it:

OBJECTION.

The printer pulled the page back inside.

Thirty seconds passed.

It returned.

BASIS?

Marr wrote:

SHOW ME WHERE.

The printer swallowed the page.

A minute passed.

Two.

The dead-letter perimeter halted.

The Collector stopped hammering against the red door.

The station clocks disagreed more violently than ever.

One ran backward.

Krell watched it.

"Is that good?"

"Probably not."

"You sound pleased again."

"I have discovered an undocumented assumption."

"Emperor preserve us."

The printer finally produced a page.

No answer.

Only a form number.

RECIPIENT DESIGNATION — MASS / COLLECTIVE / DISPUTED PERSONHOOD

FORM 7-R.

Marr stared at it.

Then she began to laugh.

Not because anything was funny.

Because somewhere, nine centuries ago, some exhausted Imperial clerk had encountered precisely this problem and had responded in the traditional manner.

They had made a form.

The automaton stamped.

FORM VALID.

Origin transmitted again.

DELIVERY ATTEMPT TOMORROW.

RECIPIENT COUNT REQUIRED.

Marr looked through the observation slit at Antegra Station, at the ships holding a nameless perimeter, at the dead-letter dark beyond them, and at the millions of lives currently attached to histories that could not agree whether they had happened.

She looked at Young Varik.

Old Varik.

Rhyse.

Krell.

Vhal.

Edrin Vale's impossible maintenance backlog waiting somewhere in the station.

The citizens who had existed.

The citizens who had been erased.

The citizens who might yet exist if Respondent Prior had its way.

Then she wrote one answer on Form 7-R.

RECIPIENT COUNT: DISPUTED.

The automaton stamped it.

ACCEPTED FOR FILING.

Origin was silent.

For seventeen full seconds.

Then the printer produced a final sheet.

COUNSEL, PRODUCE THE CLASS TOMORROW.

Below it appeared another line.

ALL MEMBERS MUST BE GIVEN OPPORTUNITY TO REFUSE.

Krell read over her shoulder.

"How many people is that?"

Marr folded the page.

"Potentially everyone Antegra has ever affected."

"How long do we have?"

The station chronometers all changed at once.

Not to the same time.

To the same date.

Tomorrow.

Marr looked at the clocks.

Then at the red door.

Then at the forms beginning to pour from every printer on Antegra Station.

Millions of them.

Each bore a single question.

DO YOU ACCEPT?

Two boxes waited beneath it.

YES.

NO.

And, because the Imperium had survived ten thousand years by never allowing reality to constrain its paperwork, a third box had been added by hand.

DISPUTED.
