## Continuation X — The Missing Receipt (Part One)

The appeals office sent its examining officer at eight hundred, when the Academy's records chamber was supposed to be quiet.

House Caldrake's observer arrived carrying a copy of his challenge. Interrogator Vane arrived without one. He already knew what it said.

The examining officer put her warrant on the counter. It authorized inspection of the seminar's seal sequence, custody receipts, transfer times and authorized access register. It did not authorize disclosure of the cadets' first accounts, their private debriefs or the content of the opposing command briefs.

"I asked whether the professors altered the accounts after the exercise," the observer said.

"You asked to inspect the means by which that possibility was excluded. That is the question I can examine."

"And if the means were inadequate?"

"Then the finding will say so."

Vane stepped away from the counter. He had secured the room and confirmed the officer's appointment, but he was not the reviewing authority. The appeals office was outside the instructional department, with a written right to preserve adverse findings. It was still an Academy office. Nobody pretended otherwise.

The custodian produced the seal register.

There were two original account slates, one for each cadet. Each had been closed and sealed before the opposite brief was released. The time entries matched the simulator's session log. The seals themselves remained intact in the storage cabinet, where the officer could examine their numbers and impressions without opening either slate.

The observer checked the first four entries and found no discrepancy.

He checked the fifth.

"Where is the receiving signature?"

The custodian leaned over the page.

It was a transfer entry from the examination desk to the overnight cabinet. The dispatch signature was present. The receiving box held a reference to the cabinet's locking record, not a person's name.

"The cabinet was secured," the custodian said.

"That wasn't my question."

Vane looked at the entry. It was exactly the sort of defect an overworked office might leave when two procedures each assumed the other was recording the handover. That observation did not supply the missing signature.

The examining officer made a copy of the page and sealed it under her own warrant.

"No one opens the cadets' records," she said. "We will establish who had custody of the cabinet, what the lock record proves, and what it does not."
