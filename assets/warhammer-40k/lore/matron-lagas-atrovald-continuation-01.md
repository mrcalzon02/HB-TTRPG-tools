# Matron Lagas Atrovald

## Continuation I — The Authorized Original

*An original Cafarron Corridor chronicle continuation. Warhammer 40,000 and associated marks and setting elements are the property of Games Workshop. This non-commercial fan work is not endorsed by or affiliated with Games Workshop.*

Matron Lagas Atrovald had just finished deciding that truth belonged to the Emperor when Arthus Pell knocked on the door.

Three times.

Then once more.

Atrovald looked up from her lectern.

Pell knew better than to knock four times.

Four knocks denoted a sealed interruption under the Fourth Order of Archival Necessity, a classification intended for events such as catastrophic vault breach, authenticated pre-Unification discovery, hostile inquisitorial seizure, or the sudden appearance of a superior who had forgotten to schedule himself.

The last category occurred more often than the regulations implied.

"Pell."

The door opened six centimeters.

His face appeared in the gap.

"Matron."

"Did Lord Inquisitor Predulum come back?"

"No."

"Then why did you knock four times?"

Pell considered this.

"Because I thought you would be angrier if I knocked three."

Atrovald stared at him.

Pell, to his credit, did not retreat.

"Come in."

He entered holding a black dispatch case with both hands.

The case was new.

That was the first unpleasant thing.

Objects inside the Interlocutum were rarely new. New objects had not yet accumulated history, and objects without history were difficult to trust. A chair that had endured fourteen generations of archivists had at least demonstrated a certain institutional loyalty. A newly manufactured chair might collapse, conceal a listening device, contain an assassin, or simply have been assembled according to a revised standard nobody had bothered to circulate.

The dispatch case had sharp corners.

No scratches.

No wax residue.

No old blood.

Atrovald disliked it immediately.

Pell placed it on the edge of her desk.

The seal on its lid bore the crossed quills and open eye of the Logos Historica Verita.

Across the spire-way.

Of course.

Atrovald closed her eyes.

Predulum had departed twenty-seven minutes ago.

She had allowed herself one cup of recaf, half of which had gone cold while she reread his destruction petition.

She had not yet completed the mandatory post-Inquisitorial-contact memorandum.

She had not yet amended the access ledger to reflect the fact that Predulum had, for the eighth or possibly ninth time, rediscovered a facility he was not supposed to remember.

She had not yet had lunch.

History had chosen its moment with malicious precision.

"Did they send a courier?"

"Three."

"Three?"

"One carried the case. One certified that the first had carried it. The third certified the certification."

Atrovald opened her eyes.

"Competent."

"I thought so."

"Did we certify their certificates?"

"Naturally."

"Good."

She reached for the dispatch case.

Pell did not move.

Atrovald looked at him.

"Why are you still here?"

"I am listed as witnessing subordinate."

"Of course you are."

Pell stepped to the side of the desk and assumed the expression of a man determined to become furniture.

Atrovald pressed her thumb to the seal.

The case clicked.

A small machine-spirit chirped.

A strip of parchment emerged.

She read it.

Then read it again.

Pell watched her face carefully.

"Bad?"

"No."

"Worse?"

"Potentially."

The request was brief.

That, in itself, was suspicious.

Imperial institutions did not communicate briefly unless they were trying to kill someone, conceal something, or demonstrate that they outranked the recipient.

The Logos Historica Verita requested temporary concordance access to an authenticated primary source held by the Interlocutum.

Title:

*The Precipice of Thought.*

Atrovald sat very still.

Pell stopped pretending to be furniture.

"That one?"

"That one."

"The Inquisitor's one?"

"The Inquisitor's recurring one."

Pell looked toward the door.

"Should we lock it?"

"It is already locked."

"The door."

"The door is also already locked."

"More?"

Atrovald almost smiled.

Almost.

Instead she removed the second parchment strip from the case.

The request specified a textual discrepancy.

The Logos office possessed a copy of *The Precipice of Thought* bearing a provenance chain older than the version currently catalogued by the Interlocutum as the Authorized Original.

Atrovald read the sentence three times because, occasionally, repetition improved reality.

It did not.

"That is impossible," Pell said.

"Yes."

"Comforting."

"No."

Atrovald rose.

Her knees objected.

She ignored them.

"Vault tier?"

Pell already had his slate out.

"Sub-Archive Theta-Seven. Philosophical Deviance, Pre-Ecclesiarchal. Shelf authority S-Rank. Secondary warding. No active memetic seals."

"Stasis?"

"Intermittent."

"Meaning?"

"It works when supervised."

"That is not what stasis means."

"It is what ours means."

Atrovald took her key-chain from the lectern.

There were thirty-seven keys on it.

Only five opened anything.

The remaining thirty-two existed because nobody had been willing to sign the destruction forms.

"Come."

They descended.

The Interlocutum beneath Spire Methelium Beta was less an archive than a geological formation made of paper.

Corridors had become vaults.

Vaults had become corridors.

Entire administrative eras survived as architecture.

One stairwell had been declared obsolete six centuries earlier but remained the fastest route to the lower stacks, which meant every archivist used it while officially denying that it existed.

Atrovald and Pell used it.

At the bottom, a servitor clerk rotated toward them.

Its parchment-feed mouth opened.

"ACCESS PURPOSE."

"Concordance review."

"FORM."

Atrovald handed it Form Nineteen-C.

The servitor scanned it.

"INCORRECT."

"How?"

"CONCORDANCE REVIEW OF AUTHORITATIVE TEXT REQUIRES FORM NINETEEN-C-A."

"There is no Nineteen-C-A."

"CORRECT."

Pell coughed into his fist.

Atrovald leaned closer to the servitor.

"Matronal override."

"STATE JUSTIFICATION."

"Because I am the Matron."

"INSUFFICIENT."

"Because I am the S-Rank Matron."

"SUFFICIENT."

The servitor stamped her form.

Imperial government remained beautiful in the way only a weaponized illness could be beautiful.

They continued.

Sub-Archive Theta-Seven smelled of old cloth, ozone, and dust that had acquired legal standing.

Pell activated the lumen strips.

Only four of seven answered.

The fifth flickered in a manner suggesting personal resentment.

Atrovald approached the sealed cabinet.

The cabinet bore seven wax impressions, three purity slips, one Inquisitorial seizure mark, one Inquisitorial cancellation of seizure, and a handwritten note reading:

**DO NOT MOVE SHELF THREE.**

Pell pointed.

"Why?"

"I don't know."

"Should we—"

"No."

Atrovald opened shelf two.

The Authorized Original of *The Precipice of Thought* lay inside a climate casket.

Black binding.

Flaking corners.

No ornamentation.

No title on the cover.

She had seen it before.

She had cited it before.

She had defended its preservation to Predulum less than an hour ago.

She had told him, with complete conviction, that originals mattered because without an original there could be no measurement of corruption.

Atrovald placed the volume on the reading table.

Pell connected the concordance imager.

The machine hummed.

The first pages appeared in amber projection.

"Compare Logos fragment," Atrovald said.

Pell inserted the data wafer supplied in the dispatch case.

Two columns appeared.

Left: Interlocutum Authorized Original.

Right: Logos Historica Verita claimed precursor.

The opening sections matched.

The argument on governance matched.

The chapter on free will matched.

The passages Predulum considered intolerable matched almost word for word.

Pell's shoulders slowly relaxed.

Then page eighty-seven appeared.

He stopped breathing.

Atrovald did not.

She had spent too many years reading history to waste oxygen on surprise.

The left column contained four paragraphs.

The right contained eleven.

Atrovald leaned closer.

The missing section was not daemonic.

Not xenos.

Not prophecy.

Not blasphemy.

It was worse.

It was an instruction to archivists.

The author argued that any institution entrusted with preserving truth would, over time, become more interested in preserving its authority over truth than truth itself.

The wording was clinical.

Almost polite.

It described curators who would remove inconvenient ambiguities in the name of stability.

Editors who would replace uncertainty with certainty.

Custodians who would eventually confuse consistency with accuracy.

Pell read the passage in silence.

Then he looked at Atrovald.

"That seems pointed."

"Most knives do."

"Could the Logos copy be forged?"

"Yes."

"Good."

"It could also be authentic."

"Less good."

Atrovald enlarged the provenance metadata.

The Logos copy claimed to descend from an archaeological recovery conducted during the final centuries before the formal establishment of the Interlocutum's present custodial charter.

She checked the paper chemistry.

The ink profile.

Script formation.

Compression artifacts in the pict-record.

Custodial marks.

Every test returned the same irritating result.

Plausible.

Plausible was the most dangerous word in archival work.

False could be sealed.

True could be classified.

Plausible demanded committees.

"Pull our provenance chain," she said.

Pell hesitated.

"All of it?"

"All."

"That may take—"

"Yes."

He obeyed.

The chain unfolded across three hololithic panes.

Copy to copy.

Custodian to custodian.

Vault to vault.

Transfer seals.

War-loss annotations.

Restoration notices.

One section had survived a fire.

Another had been evacuated during a succession crisis.

A third had been recopied after fungal contamination consumed half a wing.

Atrovald followed the lineage backward.

Two thousand years.

Four.

Seven.

Nine.

Then stopped.

There was a correction.

Small.

Routine.

The kind of mark an archivist might pass a thousand times without seeing.

**CONTEXTUAL HARMONIZATION — AUTHORIZED.**

No attached justification.

No named Matron.

No preserved vote.

No Inquisitorial seal.

Only a date.

Atrovald felt something cold settle behind her ribs.

"Pell."

"I see it."

"Retrieve the pre-harmonization witness catalog."

Pell stared at the screen.

"Matron, that catalog was deprecated."

"Retrieve it."

"It may not exist."

"Then retrieve the record explaining why it does not exist."

Pell swallowed.

"Yes, Matron."

He left at a run.

Atrovald remained alone with the book.

The Authorized Original sat on the table.

For the first time in her career, the title felt sarcastic.

She opened the volume manually.

Page eighty-seven.

Four paragraphs.

A seam in the binding.

She tilted it under the lumen.

There.

A difference in thread tension.

A replaced gathering.

Not recent.

Ancient.

Expert.

Official.

Atrovald sat down.

She had spent decades believing that the great danger was alteration without custody.

Unauthorized drift.

Public myth.

Popular memory.

Political convenience.

The thousand soft violences by which a civilization rewrote itself.

She still believed that.

That was the problem.

Because the knife in front of her had not been wielded by a mob.

It had been wielded by someone exactly like her.

A custodian.

Someone with seals.

Someone with authority.

Someone who had looked at eleven paragraphs and decided four were safer.

The door opened.

Pell returned carrying a box.

Not a data-slate.

A box.

That was worse.

"Found it."

"Where?"

"Inside a disused mortuary catalog."

"Why?"

"The mortuary catalog had better environmental controls."

Atrovald nodded.

Reasonable.

Imperial archives often survived by being misfiled into departments with larger budgets.

Pell set the box down.

Inside were brittle index cards.

Handwritten.

The pre-harmonization witness catalog.

Atrovald searched the entries.

*The Precipice of Thought.*

Three witnesses.

One destroyed.

One lost.

One transferred to contextual review.

She found the final card.

The transfer destination had been scratched out.

Not faded.

Scratched.

Pell leaned over.

"Can we recover it?"

"Probably."

"Should we?"

Atrovald looked at him.

He corrected himself.

"When do we?"

"Now."

They used oblique lumen.

Then chemical contrast.

Then a multispectral reader whose machine-spirit protested throughout the operation by emitting a thin ecclesiastical whine.

The erased destination emerged one letter at a time.

Atrovald read it.

Pell read it.

Neither spoke.

The witness had been transferred to the Matronal Office.

Not this Matronal Office.

The office.

Her office.

The chain of custody ended with her predecessors.

Not an unknown outside faction.

Not an Inquisitorial purge.

Not the Ecclesiarchy.

The Interlocutum had done it.

Pell lowered his voice.

"Could have been necessary."

Atrovald turned toward him.

He looked embarrassed.

"I mean—at the time."

"Yes."

"Perhaps the passage caused unrest."

"Perhaps."

"Perhaps people misunderstood it."

"Certainly."

"Perhaps preserving it publicly would have caused damage."

"We do not preserve anything publicly."

"Right."

Pell looked back at the text.

"That does complicate the argument."

Atrovald stood.

"Seal the room."

"Under what authority?"

"Mine."

"Which classification?"

She considered.

"Historical contamination."

Pell frowned.

"That classification usually refers to corrupted records."

Atrovald looked down at the Authorized Original.

"Yes."

He did not ask again.

They returned to the Matronal chamber two hours later.

Predulum's unsigned destruction request still sat on one side of the lectern.

The Logos dispatch case sat on the other.

Atrovald between them.

It was, she thought, an elegant summary of Imperial scholarship.

One man wanted the dangerous text burned.

One office wanted it authenticated.

Her own institution had quietly edited it thousands of years before either of them arrived.

Pell waited near the door.

"What do we tell Logos?"

"The truth."

He blinked.

"Which one?"

Atrovald looked at him.

Pell looked mortified.

Then, despite herself, she laughed.

It was not a pleasant sound.

It was too tired for that.

But it was real.

"An excellent question," she said.

She dictated.

"Interlocutum acknowledges receipt of Concordance Petition Seven-Tau."

Pell began recording.

"Primary comparison confirms substantial textual agreement between the Logos witness and the Interlocutum Authorized Original, with significant divergence beginning at folio eighty-seven."

Pell's stylus moved.

"Interlocutum provenance review has identified evidence of an internal contextual harmonization affecting the disputed folia."

The stylus stopped.

Pell looked up.

"Internal?"

"Internal."

"Should we say suspected?"

"No."

"Probable?"

"No."

"Historic?"

"Obviously."

Pell resumed.

Atrovald continued.

"Pending reconstruction of the original custodial justification, the Interlocutum withdraws sole-authority designation from its present witness."

That made Pell stop again.

"Matron."

"Write it."

"That classification has been in place for nine thousand years."

"Then it has enjoyed a very long career."

"If we withdraw it, every secondary text derived from it becomes—"

"Disputed."

"Do you know how many?"

"No."

Pell looked physically ill.

"Millions."

"Then you had better request more clerks."

He stared at her.

She raised an eyebrow.

Pell wrote.

Atrovald turned to Predulum's destruction request.

She stamped it.

**DENIED.**

Then added a second stamp.

**SOURCE AUTHORITY DISPUTED — DESTRUCTION PROHIBITED PENDING CONCORDANCE.**

Pell made a small noise.

"Is he going to be angry?"

"Yes."

"Will he come back?"

"Yes."

"Will he remember where we are?"

"Probably not."

"That has not stopped him before."

"No."

There were advantages to consistency.

Atrovald transmitted the Logos response.

The machine accepted it.

A receipt printed.

Then another.

Then a third.

Pell gathered them.

"Done."

Atrovald sat back.

The chamber seemed quieter.

Across the spire-way, the Logos Historica Verita would be receiving her admission.

Some historian she did not know would read that the ancient secret archive beneath Spire Methelium Beta had discovered an authenticated act of its own historical editing.

There would be consequences.

Committees.

Audits.

Possibly executions.

Certainly forms.

Atrovald found, to her surprise, that the thought did not frighten her as much as it should have.

She had always believed truth required custody.

She still did.

But custody, she now understood, required witnesses.

Even custodians needed someone watching the shelves.

Her lectern chimed.

Incoming Logos reply.

Too fast.

Atrovald opened it.

One line appeared.

**CONCORDANCE ACKNOWLEDGED. YOUR DISCLOSURE MATCHES OUR EXPECTED RESULT.**

Atrovald's skin prickled.

A second line appeared.

**PLEASE DO NOT ALTER EITHER WITNESS.**

A third.

**A THIRD VERSION HAS BEEN LOCATED.**

Pell stopped breathing again.

Atrovald continued to find the habit inefficient.

The message expanded.

**PROVENANCE: PRE-UNIFICATION.**

Silence filled the chamber.

Pell stared at the words.

"That predates the Interlocutum."

"Yes."

"It predates the custodial charter."

"Yes."

"It might predate the office that supposedly wrote the text."

"Yes."

Pell sat down without permission.

Atrovald allowed it.

The final line appeared.

**DELIVERY REQUESTED. PHYSICAL TRANSFER ONLY. NO TRANSCRIPTION AUTHORIZED.**

Atrovald looked across the spire-way, though meters of stone and armored archive wall made the gesture entirely symbolic.

The Lord Regent's historians possessed a version of a dangerous philosophical text older than the institution that had spent nine thousand years declaring what its original said.

Predulum wanted it destroyed.

The Logos wanted it compared.

The Interlocutum had already altered it once.

And now the oldest surviving copy was coming to her office.

Atrovald reached for her cold recaf.

She drank it.

It was terrible.

That was almost reassuring.

"Pell."

"Matron?"

"Cancel my afternoon."

"You have six scheduled petitioners, a stasis failure review, two doctrinal drift hearings, and the quarterly mortality reconciliation."

"Move them."

"To when?"

Atrovald looked at the message.

"Later."

Pell glanced at the ancient text on her lectern.

"In archival terms?"

"No."

She stood.

"In human terms."

That frightened him more.

Outside, deep in the sealed vaults of the Interlocutum, millions of approved words rested in darkness beneath wax, iron, prayer, and authority.

Most were true.

Some were useful.

A few were both.

And somewhere across the spire-way, wrapped in whatever protections the Logos Historica Verita considered sufficient, a book older than Atrovald's certainty was being prepared for delivery.

The truth belonged to the Emperor.

Atrovald still believed that.

She was simply becoming less certain that anyone else could be trusted to label the box.
