# On the Fuzzy Edge

## Continuation VI — The Carriage Held in Readiness

**Based on the work of:** *On the Fuzzy Edge.*  
**Original story by:** Christopher Vardeman / Mrcalzon02

The transit registrar had been correct about the carrier's records.

They existed.

He had been less helpful about where.

The carrier had ceased carrying anything eleven years earlier. Its horses had been sold, its loading yard converted to a bonded grain store, and its surviving papers divided between the men who had purchased its debts and the municipal office that still disputed its final tariff assessment.

Serik found this out by spending half a morning talking to merchants who remembered being owed money.

Javard found him in a tea shop beside the old yard. Three account books lay on the table, none of them the one they wanted.

"Well?" Javard asked.

"The business is dead."

"That is an ordinary condition for a business of seventeen years' age."

"The debts aren't."

Serik moved one of the books aside. Beneath it lay a slip bearing the seal of the tariff office and an inventory number. The carrier's creditors had been required to lodge its dispatch books before the last assets could be sold. The tariff office had kept the books because nobody agreed how much the carrier had earned during its final year.

"Will they let us see them?"

"They will let a trade factor see the accounts of a failed competitor. They are less enthusiastic about an Inquisitorial officer seeing their own assessment."

"Then we shall be a factor first."

Serik considered the account books.

"Will you be paying for the tea?"

"I am already paying for your education."

"It has become expensive."

Javard left him to settle the bill. The tea was cold, but the books were genuine, and for once neither man had been required to descend into a condemned crypt to obtain them.

---

The tariff office occupied the second floor of a warehouse whose first floor was still used to weigh grain.

Every few minutes the floor trembled under a cart. Dust sifted from a ceiling beam and settled on the inkstand. The ledger clerk had placed a saucer over it.

She did not ask why a commercial factor wished to inspect a seventeen-year-old carriage account. She asked which quarter, which route and which disputed charge. Serik supplied the details from the transit reference.

The clerk brought out a book bound in cracked hide.

Its pages were ruled into three columns: reservations, releases and returns.

Javard turned to the week after Canon Sol's recorded death.

There was the cathedral reference.

One covered carriage had been reserved for a burial transfer. A team and driver were assigned a two-hour standby. The entry named the destination as a cathedral receiving point, not Lower Ossuary Nineteen. It did not name a corpse.

The reservation carried a tariff-office assessment mark. It also carried a charge for unused readiness.

The release column was empty.

"Does that mean it never left?" Serik asked.

"It means this book does not record a release," the clerk answered.

"Would a release always be recorded?"

"If the carrier wanted payment for the journey. A driver needed a movement chit to collect the road allowance, and the yard master needed it to account for the team."

"Could the driver have gone without one?"

"Of course. He would have had to explain the missing horse hours, the missing fodder and why the company owed him nothing."

Javard examined the next entry. The same team had been assigned to a grain delivery the following morning. The yard master's return count matched the allocation.

That was evidence against the reserved carriage making an unrecorded long journey. It was not evidence against every other possible movement.

"Who cancelled the reservation?"

The clerk turned to the final column.

"No cancellation is entered. The standby expired."

"Who paid?"

"The account was settled as an unused hire. The cathedral's office was charged the minimum."

"Who collected?"

"The carrier, eventually."

She pointed to a later settlement mark.

"The creditors had to fight for that one. The cathedral said it had requested a transfer, not ordered a journey."

Serik gave Javard a look.

There was an entire history of institutions refusing to pay for things they had never quite ordered. Javard did not need to hear it again.

"May we examine the supporting chits?" he asked.

The clerk hesitated.

"Some are missing."

"Which?"

"That is why we keep an inventory."

She fetched it. The movement-chit packet for that quarter was present. The particular numbered chit that would have authorized this carriage's departure had never been issued. Its unused stub remained bound to the pad.

Javard asked for the clerk's account of how the pad had been preserved. She showed him the tariff office's accession seal, applied when the carrier's creditors lodged the books eleven years earlier. She did not pretend it proved the papers had been untouched during the six preceding years.

That qualification made the record more useful, not less.

---

The transit registrar did not appreciate being told that another office possessed a better book.

"I did not say it was better," Javard replied.

"You came back carrying it."

"A copy."

"An authenticated copy?"

"With the tariff clerk's signature, the accession reference and the limits of her custody."

The registrar took the pages. He read them with his hand over his own register, as though the two documents might otherwise begin arguing.

His office's stamp had accepted the delivery request. The carrier's book had reserved a vehicle. Neither office recorded a loaded body leaving the cathedral.

"Then we can close it," he said.

"No."

"The carriage wasn't released."

"The reserved carriage wasn't recorded as released. Do you know whether another carrier moved the remains?"

"No."

"Whether the cathedral used its own conveyance?"

"No."

"Whether anyone moved something without entering your office?"

The registrar looked offended.

"That would be irregular."

"Yes."

Javard let the word remain between them.

The registrar returned to the blank departure field.

"An old request can be cancelled by the issuing authority."

"Is that authority still identifiable?"

"The office is. The person who placed the Inquisitorial restriction is not."

"Then you have not acquired a cancellation order."

The registrar pushed the book away.

"What have I acquired?"

"A reason not to certify delivery. You already had one. This is a second."

The man regarded the copy again. His anger had been directed at the prospect of another seventeen years of unresolved business. Javard understood it. An open entry cost time each time an auditor asked why it was open.

"Will you attach it?" Javard asked.

"With a note saying the carrier record is incomplete."

"Yes."

"And that no body has been accounted for."

"That is the question we are investigating. Write what the record establishes."

The registrar reached for a fresh sheet.

Serik waited until they were outside.

"You might have allowed him the pleasure of closing something."

"I should like to close it myself."

"Then why not?"

"Because a closed book can still contain an unburied man."

---

Caldris had located the predecessor-bursar's original instruction.

He had not found it in the burial office. That office possessed the working copy. The original had been filed with the first annual appropriation under a heading that concerned the upkeep of unused ecclesiastical property.

The folder was still in the cathedral's financial archive. It had been retained because each succeeding bursar had been required to justify the same expenditure against the previous year's allowance.

Merek, the burial clerk, stood beside Caldris when Javard arrived. The senior custodian had declined to attend. Someone had to supervise the living burial office.

The original instruction was short.

Prepare one destination for the pending transfer of Ephram Sol. Maintain it in serviceable condition. Record deficiencies. Do not issue a receipt of remains except upon witnessed delivery under competent authority.

Below the signature was an acknowledgment by the burial office. The receiving field was blank.

"Does this change anything?" Caldris asked.

"It tells us the bursar understood the difference between preparing a place and receiving a body."

"He may have been instructed to use those words."

"He may."

"By the prior Inquisitorial delegation?"

"We do not know."

Merek set down the working copy. Its text matched the original, apart from a later annotation directing the annual inspections to be filed after the Vigil of Saint Orphael.

"Who added the annual schedule?" Javard asked.

"The burial office," Merek said. "It was the existing inspection day for the condemned galleries. There was no reason to send a unit twice."

That was plausible. Javard had spent too long among false coincidences to treat every ordinary economy as a cipher.

"Can you establish when the annotation was made?"

"From the duty clerk's hand, probably the following year. Not from the ink alone."

"Then put that in your statement."

Caldris looked at the original signature.

"My predecessor would have known why the instruction was issued."

"He might have known. He might have known only that an authority had ordered a destination maintained."

"He's dead."

"That does make further questioning inconvenient."

Merek did not smile. He was reading the blank receiving field.

"People keep asking whether our office failed to bury Sol," he said. "We were ordered to keep a place ready. We did."

"Yes."

"Then why are we being investigated?"

"Because the record of a place and the custody of a person have been made to look like the same thing. That may be nobody's deliberate fault. It may be someone's. Your office has evidence about one part."

Merek placed his hand on the original.

"Then I would prefer it remain here."

"So would I."

Javard ordered a witnessed copy and left the original where its custodians could be held accountable for it.

---

The maintenance payments were not secret.

That was their most irritating feature.

For sixteen years the annual warrants had been absorbed into the ordinary allowance for unused burial spaces. The amounts were small. They paid for a servitor's route, replacement brushes, lamp oil and occasional wheel repairs. When the ossuary allocation came under review, the cathedral had defended it as the cost of maintaining closed places.

No one had needed to forge an extraordinary payment. No one had needed to mention Sol.

The latest account, however, had acquired an extraordinary cost.

Javard's inquiry required two clerks to reconcile the original warrants, a third to witness copies, and a maintenance supervisor to explain why the inspection servitor had not been retired when its wheel bearing began to fail. The burial office had postponed its regular inventory of common grave tablets.

Merek showed Javard the delay sheet.

"These names will not disappear," he said. "But their families will come tomorrow asking why the inscriptions have not been checked."

"How many?"

"Twenty-six."

"Can the work be done this evening?"

"With overtime."

"Paid from where?"

"The same allowance you are examining."

Caldris looked weary.

"We can transfer a clerk from the appointments office."

"Montpclair's recovery office?" Javard asked.

"No. I have learned that lesson. The ordinary appointments office. It has two clerks on the next watch."

"And its work?"

"Would be delayed."

Javard looked at the figures. There was no innocent extra clerk waiting in a cupboard. Every reassignment would transfer inconvenience to someone whose case did not concern Ephram Sol.

"Keep the grave tablets on schedule," he said. "One clerk on the historical reconciliation, not three. Have the tariff office's copies witnessed once, then stop making copies of copies."

"That will take longer," Caldris said.

"Yes."

"And the original records?"

"Remain where they are."

Caldris crossed out two proposed temporary assignments. Merek went back to the burial office with a signed schedule for the tablets.

The investigation would lose a day.

Twenty-six families would not.

Serik watched the last clerk leave.

"You have just delayed your own case."

"I noticed."

"I thought the Inquisition disliked delay."

"It dislikes being told that delay is free."

Above them the cathedral bell marked the afternoon office. The acting celebrant took the service under the standing delegation. Montpclair remained in the medicae ward, still prohibited from receiving visitors. Halva's note reported pain, rest and no clearance for administrative duties.

Javard did not send a question back.

---

Serik brought the last carrier statement after dark.

The tariff clerk had declined to certify that no journey took place. She would certify the narrower facts: the reserved vehicle had no issued movement chit, its standby charge was settled as unused hire, and the surviving yard accounts did not record its departure. Her office could establish custody of the books only from the creditors' deposit eleven years ago.

She had written the limitations in her own hand.

Serik placed the statement on Javard's desk.

"She thought you would be disappointed."

"I am."

"She said you would prefer a conclusion."

"I would."

"She refused to give you one."

"Then she has done her work."

Serik sat opposite him. The factor had carried a commercial identity through an investigation that increasingly involved old Inquisitorial authority, condemned burial galleries and a man who had been turned into a records servitor. Javard had never asked whether he wished to remain. The question was beginning to matter.

"You could go back to the trade house," Javard said.

Serik looked at him.

"Is that an order?"

"No."

"Have I become inconvenient?"

"Not yet."

"Then I'll stay."

Javard did not mistake the answer for limitless loyalty. Serik knew enough to be frightened, and enough about Javard to suspect that he would not be rewarded for inventing certainty. Those were practical reasons to continue. They were also reasons the man might leave later.

"Tomorrow," Serik said, "what are we looking for?"

"The authority that held the request open after the carriage reservation expired."

"Not the body?"

"Not until we can account for who was permitted to move it."

Serik glanced toward the old sealed cabinet beyond the adjoining chamber.

"And if that authority is in there?"

"Then we establish how to ask it a question without breaking its seal."

"That sounds expensive."

"It may be."

Serik rose. At the door he stopped.

"The tea this morning was also expensive."

Javard handed him a coin.

"An advance against tomorrow's complaints."

---

Javard arranged the evidence in three separate groups.

The burial office had prepared a destination and maintained it for seventeen years. The transit office had accepted a delivery request but recorded neither departure nor arrival. The carrier had reserved a carriage and charged for its unused readiness; its surviving papers showed no release of that particular vehicle.

None of those statements identified what lay behind Sol's clean plaque.

None established that Sol's body had left the cathedral, or that it had not.

The original bursar's instruction was especially plain about one thing: a prepared place was not a receipt. Someone had understood that distinction seventeen years ago, and the institution had managed to preserve the words while losing any account of who had been expected to deliver the man.

Javard wrote a request for the surviving authority record governing the transfer's release or suspension. It named the old reference, the carrier's reservation and the blank receiving field. It did not demand access to the sealed cabinet. It asked which office could authenticate an order to release, cancel or continue the transfer.

Caldris would send it through the cathedral's proper channel. Javard would send a separate authenticated request through his own.

Two questions, two chains of custody. If both returned silence, that silence would at least have a date and a witness.

He placed the new copies beside the printed warning from Archive Servitor Nine.

**DO NOT OPEN UNTIL HE ASKS.**

He still did not know who *he* was.

The cabinet remained sealed. The niche remained closed. Montpclair remained under Halva's care. Somewhere in the city's old accounts, a carriage had been paid to wait for a dead man it was never recorded as carrying.

Javard put away the coin receipt Serik had returned with his change.

The little piece of paper proved that a debt had been settled. It proved nothing about whether the tea had been worth drinking.

He filed it anyway.

In the morning he would have to explain to two authorities why an investigation had advanced by establishing precisely what nobody was entitled to certify.

That was not the answer he had wanted.

It was, for the moment, the answer he could defend.
