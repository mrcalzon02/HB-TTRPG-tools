VI. THE IMPERIAL ART OF SLEEPING BADLY

The damaged freighter was far enough away that Broussard could justify a short rest cycle.

Not a proper one.

Nothing involving the Imperial Guard, a Chimera, and several dozen kilometers of frozen ocean could reasonably be described as proper rest.

But enough.

His men needed it.

More importantly, he needed it.

There was a particular species of stupidity that came from exhaustion. Broussard had seen competent officers stare directly at obvious problems and fail to recognize them because they had been awake for twenty-eight hours and were surviving entirely on recaff, irritation, and institutional momentum.

He had no desire to become one of them.

They were currently moving through what should, by every available measure, be the safest portion of the operation.

The route had already been surveyed.

The vehicles were intact.

The convoy's departure corridor remained behind them.

Nothing large had yet attempted to eat anyone.

This was as close to security as High Presidio generally offered.

So Broussard made a decision that felt almost decadent.

He delegated.

His second would maintain command of the column while he got his head down for a while.

Broussard announced this with considerably more formality than the act deserved, partly because a commissar was sitting three meters away and partly because officers liked pretending that collapsing into an armored bench counted as doctrinal fatigue management rather than surrender.

He glanced toward Gabriel.

"If I can get an hour or two, I'll be worth considerably more when we reach the site."

The commissar nodded.

"Agreed."

That was it.

No lecture.

No speech about the Emperor granting strength beyond mortal limits.

No suggestion that sleep demonstrated insufficient zeal.

Just agreement.

Broussard found himself quietly relieved.

"I'd rather arrive able to make decisions."

Gabriel gave him a faint smile.

"A surprisingly controversial command philosophy."

"Don't spread it around."

"Your secret is safe."

Broussard handed over the final operational details, confirmed that the vox operator knew under what circumstances he was to be awakened, and then began the complicated military exercise known as trying to sleep in a Chimera.

The Chimera was many things.

Armored.

Reliable, by Imperial standards.

Capable of traversing terrain no sensible civilian vehicle would willingly approach.

Armed sufficiently to turn a disagreement into a crater.

It was not, however, designed around human comfort.

The seats seemed to have been constructed according to some ancient specification requiring them to support a guardsman efficiently during transport while ensuring that he never became comfortable enough to mistake military service for hospitality.

Broussard had occasionally wondered whether the original design team had been punished for excessive compassion and later forced to redesign the troop compartment under ecclesiastical supervision.

The benches were hard.

Not merely hard in the ordinary sense.

They possessed the particular unforgiving hardness of military equipment designed by people who would never personally sit upon it.

Every edge seemed positioned to discover a bone.

Every bracket located a joint.

Every bolt head found flesh.

The suspension did nothing to improve matters.

High Presidio's winter ice looked smooth from a distance.

It was not.

Pressure changes created long ridges beneath the snow. Frozen cracks lifted unevenly. Drifts concealed hard edges where ice plates had overlapped and fused.

The Chimera crossed all of them with admirable determination.

Its occupants crossed them somewhat less admirably.

Broussard rearranged himself along one section of seating.

Then again.

Then a third time.

There was a technique to sleeping inside an armored vehicle.

Veterans learned it eventually.

The important thing was to abandon the assumption that the human body had been intended to possess a spine.

He wedged his kit bag against one corner.

The bag contained several pieces of folded clothing, two ration packs, spare gloves, a cleaning roll, emergency gear and something hard whose identity he did not care enough to investigate.

Perfect.

Military bedding.

He punched it several times until the contents rearranged themselves into a configuration that was only mildly hostile to the human skull.

Then he curled sideways.

This brought him into conflict with the armrests.

The armrests were among the Chimera's more mysterious design decisions.

Nobody Broussard knew actually liked them.

When lowered, they sat at precisely the wrong angle for resting an arm comfortably.

When raised, they became vertical metal protrusions strategically positioned to punish anyone attempting to lean against them.

They had three primary configurations:

inconvenient,

painful,

and unexpectedly painful.

Broussard had ordered several removed from his vehicle years earlier.

This had improved things dramatically.

Unfortunately, not all of them.

One remained beside the section where he was trying to sleep.

Lowered, it dug into his thigh.

Raised, it pressed directly into his armpit.

Halfway between those positions, it became a mechanical device seemingly intended to ensure that whichever part of his body moved first would regret the decision.

He lowered it.

No.

Raised it.

Worse.

Lowered it again.

Hessel watched this process from across the compartment.

"Need assistance, sir?"

"No."

"You look comfortable."

"I will execute you."

Gabriel glanced over.

Hessel immediately pointed at Broussard.

"Threat witnessed by commissariat authority."

The commissar considered the situation.

"Justified."

Broussard stared at both of them.

"I regret encouraging morale."

Hessel grinned.

"You always say that."

Broussard turned away before either man could become more ambitious.

Eventually he discovered the correct arrangement.

Left shoulder against the hull.

Kit bag beneath his head.

One knee bent.

Other leg partially extended beneath the opposite bench.

Coat folded beneath his ribs.

Armrest lowered with his elbow wedged around it.

This produced something resembling comfort.

Not actual comfort.

But a state sufficiently distant from immediate suffering that sleep became theoretically possible.

The Chimera struck a ridge.

The entire compartment jumped.

Broussard's head bounced off the kit bag.

Something inside it stabbed him behind the ear.

He opened one eye.

Of course.

He reached into the bag and discovered a spare auspex battery.

He removed it.

Rearranged the clothing.

Closed his eyes again.

Another impact.

The seat came upward and struck him directly in the hip.

Sleep, he reflected, was easier in trenches.

At least trenches occasionally remained still.

Eventually, however, exhaustion won the argument.

Not completely.

A Chimera never permitted complete victory.

Broussard drifted instead into the peculiar half-sleep common to soldiers in moving vehicles.

Part of him slept.

Part of him remained aware of everything.

The engine's constant growl became the foundation of the dream.

Track links clattered.

Suspension arms groaned.

Somewhere forward, the driver and his relief quietly discussed route markers.

The vox hissed occasionally as the vehicles exchanged short position checks.

Someone opened a ration tin.

Someone else complained about the smell.

A lasgun shifted against a rack with a metallic click every time they crossed uneven ice.

Gabriel spoke softly with one of the crewmen about something Broussard could not quite follow.

Then the voices receded.

Snow hissed along the outer armor.

The Chimera rolled onward.

Broussard dreamed without dreaming.

Fragments.

Boot camp.

A parade ground.

The old instructor screaming because someone had misplaced an entrenching tool.

Then an ice field.

Then the freighter ahead of them somehow resting vertically upon its stern like a monument.

Then nothing.

Another jolt dragged him halfway awake.

He opened his eyes just enough to see the opposite wall.

Everything remained normal.

Hessel was asleep now, folded into a position that should have required surgical intervention.

Gabriel was reading something on a dataslate.

The commissar noticed Broussard looking.

"Everything's fine."

Broussard grunted.

"Time?"

"Forty-three minutes."

He closed his eyes.

"Wake me if the ocean attacks."

"Define attacks."

"Anything with tentacles."

"Very good."

Pause.

"Large tentacles?"

Broussard opened one eye again.

"Gabriel."

"Understood."

He went back under.

This time he managed something closer to genuine sleep.

Not deep.

Never deep.

The body refused to surrender fully while surrounded by engines, armor and armed men.

But enough for thought to loosen.

Enough for his muscles to stop quietly complaining.

Enough for the pressure behind his eyes to begin fading.

The unpleasant truth was that soldiers became very good at sleeping badly.

It was an acquired skill.

Given sufficient experience, a guardsman could sleep against ammunition crates, beneath artillery pieces, inside transport holds, beside burning machinery, during bombardments and occasionally while someone nearby was actively arguing.

Comfort was not necessary.

Only exhaustion.

Broussard had once watched a man sleep for three hours beneath a Basilisk while its crew performed barrel maintenance above him.

Another had slept through an air raid.

One particularly gifted trooper had fallen asleep standing against a wall during a regimental briefing and remained upright until everyone else left.

Humans were adaptable creatures.

The Imperium relied heavily upon this.

Design nothing for comfort.

Make nothing easy.

Then congratulate mankind for surviving it.

The Emperor protects.

The quartermaster economizes.

Another hour passed.

Perhaps more.

Time became difficult to judge.

At some point the motion changed.

Subtle.

The Chimera began slowing.

That reached Broussard faster than any alarm could have.

His eyes opened.

Fully this time.

The engine pitch dropped.

The suspension ceased its regular pounding.

Voices changed tone.

Not fear.

Attention.

That particular sharpening of speech that occurred when men began seeing something worth discussing.

Broussard pulled himself upright.

His spine objected immediately.

Every joint felt as though it had been replaced during his sleep with a cheaper component.

Gabriel looked over from the opposite bench.

"Morning."

Broussard rubbed his face.

"How long?"

"Two hours and twelve minutes."

That was better than expected.

He rolled his shoulders.

Something cracked.

Then something else.

He suspected neither sound was authorized.

"Any problems?"

"None."

"Wildlife?"

"Two thermal contacts beneath the ice. Both small. They moved away."

"Vehicles?"

"All operational."

"Recovery hauler?"

"Still ugly."

"Good."

He reached for his gloves.

The Chimera slowed further.

The command vox clicked.

"Lead to One. Visual contact."

Broussard froze.

There it was.

Sleep vanished.

Every part of him reorganized itself around the sentence.

He grabbed the handset.

"One receiving."

"Freighter sighted. Approximately three kilometers. Bearing zero-eight-seven."

Broussard moved toward the forward viewing position.

The driver shifted slightly to give him room.

Outside, the world remained an endless gray-white plain beneath a colorless sky.

At first he saw nothing.

Then movement resolved through the blowing snow.

A shape.

Dark.

Large.

Wrong.

The crippled freighter sat against a pressure formation ahead of them.

Even from kilometers away, the damage was obvious.

The vessel listed several degrees to port.

One side rode noticeably lower than the other.

Its bow had driven partially into a ridge of shattered ice while the stern remained elevated behind it.

Black scoring marked the lower hull.

A long tear ran through plating near the engineering section.

Emergency lights blinked weakly through the storm.

No engines.

No visible movement.

No reassuring glow from gravitic systems.

Just several thousand tonnes of stranded metal sitting alone on the frozen ocean.

Broussard stared.

Gabriel had moved beside him.

"Could be worse," the commissar said.

Broussard glanced sideways.

"You've been with us four hours and you're already saying things like that."

"I'm adapting."

The vox crackled again.

"Lead reports no visible personnel outside."

Broussard looked at the freighter.

Then at the surrounding ice.

Flat.

Empty.

Quiet.

Too quiet.

"All vehicles," he said into the command channel. "Tighten formation. Weapons live but safed. No one dismounts until I give the word."

Acknowledgments came back one by one.

Broussard continued watching the wreck.

His body still ached from sleeping.

His neck hurt.

His left arm had gone partially numb.

There was probably an armrest-shaped bruise forming beneath his ribs.

But his head was clearer.

The fog of exhaustion had gone.

That made the view ahead somehow worse.

A tired man could blame unease on fatigue.

A rested man had fewer excuses.

The freighter grew larger through the snow.

Broussard leaned closer to the vision slit.

Something about it bothered him.

Not the damage.

Not the angle.

Something else.

He could not yet identify it.

But the instinct that had kept his unit alive across years of service had begun whispering again.

Nothing had gone wrong during the drive.

They had rested.

The vehicles had behaved.

The weather remained manageable.

The route had been clear.

For several hours, Murphy had been exceptionally quiet.

Broussard had learned to distrust that most of all.
