Darcelle had kept the seminar's first-account procedure on a maintenance slate, including the times when the cadets sealed their accounts and when the machine recorded release of the opposing briefs.

He could prove the first sequence. He could not prove the overnight transfer.

The examining officer asked him to distinguish the two in his statement. He did, then read it again before signing.

"Do you want the technical logs?"

"The portions that establish the order of sealing and release. Not the private accounts."

"They also show the cabinet's access monitor."

"Does the monitor identify a person?"

"It records a door cycle and the cabinet lock state."

"Then call it that."

Darcelle looked at the monitor record. There had been no door cycle between the recorded closure and the morning inventory. The cabinet had remained locked. It was evidence against an ordinary physical opening during those hours, but not a witness to the transfer or proof against every possible manipulation of a slate before it entered the cabinet.

He marked the limitation.

When he brought the statement to Prite, Prite was examining the same gap from the opposite direction.

"Can we show that the accounts were sealed before the cadets learned the other briefs?"

"Yes."

"Can we show they stayed sealed overnight?"

"The seals are intact and the monitor records no opening."

"Then what are we missing?"

"The person who accepted them."

Prite stared at him.

"I thought the cabinet accepted them."

"Cabinets are not witnesses."

"Mine have testified against me."

"Yours usually have scorch marks."

Renault, reading the appeals warrant over their shoulders, did not smile. He was looking at the line that forbade either side from representing the review as a test of the teaching method.

"Even if the custody passes," he said, "we still haven't proved the seminar improved anyone's command."

Prite set down his slate.

He had spent weeks learning that distinction. It had not become pleasant through repetition.

---

The custodian who had closed the records room after the seminar remembered receiving two sealed slates in a carrying tray. He had checked their numbers against the dispatch page, taken them to the cabinet and locked it before leaving.

He had not signed the receiving box.

"The cabinet register records my shift," he told the examining officer. "I thought that was sufficient."

The clerk who had dispatched the tray had thought the same. Both procedures were printed on different sides of the same form. Neither required the other clerk to check for a name before ending the shift.

The officer compared the shift roster, cabinet lock record, seal numbers and the two statements. She found the records mutually consistent. She also found that the receiving signature had never existed.

"Was anybody else in the room?" the Caldrake observer asked.

"The night watch made its rounds," the custodian answered. "They did not take the tray."

"Did you see them?"

"At the outer door."

"Then you cannot testify to what happened inside the cabinet after you left."

"No."

The observer wrote that answer down.

The examining officer asked for the watch's corridor log. It recorded the rounds, not the cabinet's contents. She attached it without treating it as a missing witness miraculously recovered.
