# Scion of the Warp-Born Blood — The Weight of the Second Copy

**Based on the work of:** *Scion of the Warp-Born Blood*  
**Original story by:** Christopher Vardeman / Mrcalzon02  
**Continuation:** The Weight of the Second Copy

---

The physician permitted Mulvane to attend the drydock review for one watch, provided the room remained shielded and the House supplied a chair.

“The last condition was not mine,” Mulvane said.

“It was mine when you tried standing through the previous examination.”

“I stood.”

“Briefly. You have confused that with an argument.”

The physician turned the slate so he could read the figures. The tremor in Mulvane's left hand had diminished. His response to an ordinary unlit line of text was nearly normal. A bright bar still lingered across his vision after the test lamp was covered, and a second examination had produced the same result.

“May I work with the passage calculations?”

“You may study them. You may correct ordinary trajectories if another qualified officer reviews your corrections. You will not sit beneath a navigation lamp, open your eye, or undertake a simulation that reproduces the beacon's pressure.”

“The House has been asking for an estimate.”

“The House may continue asking.”

Mulvane signed the restriction. His signature had become legible again. That was something he could measure without being told that it was enough.

The physician looked at the drydock summons. “They want you there because the altered manifest began with you.”

“Not because I am fit to guide a ship.”

“Then you understand the distinction.”

Mulvane had understood it for three days. He had not yet grown accustomed to hearing another man make it on his behalf.

---

On the sixth day of the register trial, the two copies disagreed.

The allocation office had recorded a repair tender's revised departure at the end of second watch. The traffic desk showed the same movement at the beginning of third. Both carried proper seals. Both showed the vessel departing after a routine engine check. Neither clerk had invented a ship or concealed a passenger.

The senior allocation officer laid the sheets side by side.

“Which is right?”

The traffic supervisor considered the question. “Both, according to their clocks. The traffic bell is four minutes behind the allocation bell. We used the time of the bell that released the cradle. They used the time the revised priority was entered.”

“And the passage?”

“Delayed eleven minutes. No contract penalty. The tender's master acknowledged the revised slot.”

The officer wrote the difference in the margin. “Then the entries are not interchangeable.”

The supervisor did not look pleased. “We have been saying that since the first day.”

A junior clerk brought a third sheet, this one showing the tug crew's duty extension. Four men had remained on station to receive a clearance they expected before the watch changed. The extra minutes fell across the boundary of their paid shift. Their foreman had already entered the charge against the drydock.

“We could have kept the old arrangement,” the liaison said. “One desk's time would have been sufficient.”

“One desk's time was sufficient to lose a vessel from the working list,” the senior officer replied. “That was why we authorized a trial.”

“Which now costs us four men and a second clerk.”

“Yes.”

The officer did not pretend that the cost proved the trial worthwhile. He ordered the two offices to record both the authorization time and the physical movement time until the thirty days expired. The additional field would have to be entered by hand. If the clerks could not maintain it under a crowded watch, that failure would belong in the report.

Mulvane watched the supervisor mark the discrepancy. There had been no deception in the two clocks. That did not make their disagreement harmless. A future claimant could have chosen whichever time suited his case and presented it as the only one.

“Do you want the old sheet corrected?” the liaison asked.

“No,” said the senior officer. “I want it preserved, with the explanation attached.”

The junior clerk looked at the second copy as if it had become heavier.

---

The restricted entry arrived that afternoon.

The traffic sheet showed a reserved place without a vessel name. It carried the seal of the House's restricted-movement office and an accountable reference number. The allocation copy recorded the same reservation, but its authorization time was later by seven minutes. The two entries could not both describe the original decision.

The traffic supervisor requested a reconciliation. The restricted-movement clerk refused to disclose the client.

“I haven't asked for the client,” the supervisor said. “I have asked when the place was reserved.”

“The authority is sealed.”

“The name is sealed. The time is printed on your own acknowledgment.”

The clerk went away to consult his superior. Mulvane remained seated while the others waited. A name withheld from a public manifest could be a necessary precaution. An invisible change in the order of passage could also conceal a preference that nobody had been permitted to examine. The trial had been designed to keep those two matters apart. It had not yet proved that they could be kept apart cheaply.

The restricted office returned a certified timing extract without the vessel's identity or destination. It showed that the slot had been held earlier than either working sheet suggested. A duty scribe had copied the later release time into the authorization field.

“Was the vessel moved?” the senior officer asked.

“No,” said the clerk. “The reservation stood throughout.”

“Then no client's priority was lost.”

“Not in this case.”

The liaison tapped the unlabelled line. “Anyone who reads the two desks together can infer that a restricted movement took place.”

“They could infer that from the old blank,” Mulvane said.

The liaison turned toward him. Mulvane had not meant to speak. The afterimage from the physician's lamp had faded, but the habit of protecting himself from the House's attention remained.

“A blank could have been an error,” the liaison said.

“And this is an acknowledged reservation,” Mulvane replied. “Neither tells a reader whose vessel passed.”

“Some readers require no name to ask dangerous questions.”

“That is true.”

He had no answer that made the risk disappear. The senior officer required a sealed reconciliation held in the permanent archive, and a public copy showing only that the timing had been corrected under accountable authority. He forbade either desk from copying the restricted extract into its ordinary ledger.

The traffic supervisor protested the extra handling. The restricted clerk protested that the correction itself disclosed the existence of a discrepancy. Both objections were reasonable. Both went into the report.

The vessel passed without incident. The record of the reservation took longer to settle than the passage.

---

The carrier factor's representative came with a pump-service extract and without the original receiving-grade certificate.

“The certificate remains with the receiving factor,” he said. “Their seal clerk is absent. I can send a certified copy next watch.”

“Then the loss claim remains open,” the senior officer said.

“We have supplied what was asked.”

“Part of what was asked.”

The pump extract showed irregular cycling before the hospital lighter displaced the carrier's original approach. The earliest warming was not new evidence. It was the reason the House had requested the service record. The later entries were less accommodating. During the delay the auxiliary circuit had failed for a short interval; the log recorded a maintenance acknowledgment, but the pressure stylus had stopped marking before the repair team reached the pump.

“How long was the auxiliary unavailable?” the officer asked.

“The crew estimate is twelve minutes.”

“The maintenance acknowledgment says nine.”

“Two men were using different clocks.”

Mulvane looked at the drydock's two manifest sheets. He could see why the officer's mouth tightened.

The representative spread his hands. “My employers lost value on their cargo. The receiving factor paid a lower grade. The delay occurred. Are you suggesting that the House owes nothing because a pump also failed?”

“No,” said the senior officer. “I am saying that a real loss, a real delay and a real pump failure are not a calculation of which caused how much of the loss.”

“Will you at least pay the berth charge?”

“That is a separate head of claim. We can examine the contract and the acknowledged arrival time. We will not make payment on a line whose basis we have not yet verified merely to improve the appearance of the whole account.”

The representative was silent for a moment. Mulvane expected anger. Instead the man removed a smaller invoice from his folio.

“This is the holding service,” he said. “It was incurred even if the grain had arrived cold.”

The officer read it. The service was real, but the contract allowed a free interval after a documented mechanical interruption. Whether that interval applied to the carrier's own pump fault remained a question for the signed carriage terms.

“I will answer this separately,” he said.

“That is all I ask.”

It was not all the representative wanted. It was enough to keep him at the table.

Mulvane did not offer a theory of the damaged pump. He had never seen it. He knew the two navigation assignments and their times. He could not make the carrier's machinery obey his memory of the passage.

---

The drydock clerk who had preserved the superseded manifest was now assigned to reconciliation duty.

His old priority desk had not been restored. His new work consisted of comparing copies after movements were complete, a task requiring accuracy but granting little discretion. The senior officer had made certain that the reassignment notice carried no misconduct finding. He had not made certain that the clerk's next promotion would survive it.

The clerk brought Mulvane a sealed list of the week's differences.

“Seven entries,” he said. “Three clock distinctions, two corrected authorizations, one restricted timing extract and one transposition in a tender's berth number. No movement missing.”

“Did you find them all?”

“Five. The traffic office found two.”

“You sound disappointed.”

The clerk looked down. “I had hoped the trial would need less of us after the first watch.”

“Will it?”

“Not if it works.”

Mulvane almost answered with a consoling observation about necessary labor. He stopped. The man had been moved from a desk he knew to work that might vanish when the thirty days ended. The House would keep whatever it learned. The clerk might keep only the entry in his service record.

“Are the hours recorded?”

“Yes, lord. The supervisor signed them.”

“And the personnel review?”

“Still pending.”

Mulvane had no authority to restore his place or secure his advancement. The clerk knew that. Mulvane knew he knew.

“Then keep the signed copies,” Mulvane said.

“I have.”

The clerk waited, perhaps for a promise, perhaps only for permission to leave. Mulvane gave neither a false reassurance nor a new task. He thanked him for the work, and the man returned to his office with the sealed list.

It was a small exchange. Mulvane found it harder to put away than the factor's figures.

---

At the next physician's review, Mulvane could hold the cup in his left hand while reading a passage table with his right.

The physician covered the lamp. The bar remained in Mulvane's sight for three breaths. The previous examination had shown four. The one before that, six.

“An improvement,” Mulvane said.

“A trend,” the physician replied. “Three observations made under comparable conditions. Not a clearance.”

“Would you permit another examination sooner?”

“No. You would spend the interval preparing to pass it rather than recovering.”

Mulvane set the cup down. His hand did not tremble.

“The allocation office has given me routine trajectories.”

“I know. I approved them.”

“My replacement has taken another inward passage.”

“I know that too.”

Mulvane had once regarded every completed assignment by another Navigator as evidence that the House might learn to do without him. Now he tried to imagine the replacement in the chair, the unbearable light beyond the bindings, and the crew waiting for a course that did not kill them.

“Was the passage safe?” the physician asked.

“Yes.”

“Then there is one fewer reason to injure yourself proving that you remain useful.”

Mulvane looked toward the covered lamp. The physician had not promised he would recover fully. The House had not promised to retain him if he did not. He knew what happened to members of his bloodline who ceased to serve.

“I would like the next figures entered as carefully as these,” he said.

“They will be.”

The physician signed the restriction for another interval. No sight exercise. No shipboard assignment. Shielded study only.

Mulvane took the order without protest.

---

The seventh-day trial report filled six pages. Two described the movements. Four described the work required to keep the descriptions honest.

The senior allocation officer had expected an additional clerk for each busy watch. The trial had consumed more than that, especially when the restricted office insisted upon its own witness and seal. The traffic supervisor had submitted the overtime docket. The restricted office had submitted an objection to preserving a visible marker of its reservations. The House's liaison had submitted a warning that an untested procedure could become an unexamined custom if nobody named an end date.

“All valid,” the senior officer said.

Mulvane was permitted to hear the review because his passage had prompted it. He was not invited to decide.

“Then end it,” the liaison said. “We have found no further erased vessel.”

“Seven days are not thirty,” the officer replied. “We have found two authorizations copied incorrectly and a difference between movement and assignment times. We have also found that keeping the two records costs more than we estimated.”

“Which argues against continuation.”

“Which argues for an honest trial. I will not call it a success to protect my signature, nor a failure to avoid paying the clerks.”

He authorized the next watch's additional reconciliation hours, limited the restricted extracts to sealed custody, and required the trial to continue only at this drydock until the original end date. The personnel review would proceed separately. The carrier claim would remain disputed until the receiving-grade certificate and signed terms arrived. The lighter's corrected acknowledgment would not be converted into an admission of debt. The station conversion expense would remain against Mulvane's service allocation pending the final House accounting.

Mulvane listened to the decisions in the order they were made. None undid his uncontracted passage. None explained the three deaths. None restored the clerk's former desk or made his own third eye fit for service.

When the review ended, the senior officer returned the first week's report to its folder.

“You have been quiet,” he told Mulvane.

“I have nothing to correct.”

“Good. I would rather have a record that survives disagreement than a witness who approves of it.”

Mulvane rose slowly. The room remained shielded, and the light did not hurt. Beyond its door the drydock was preparing another passage, with another crew and another Navigator. Somewhere in the traffic office two clerks were beginning the second week's copies. They would have to be paid. The pages would have to be kept. The trial might yet fail.

The earlier manifest had lost a ship by making a line disappear. The new procedure had given that line a second witness. It had also given the House a new expense, and the men who carried it a new burden.

Mulvane went back to his shielded table and the ordinary trajectories he was allowed to read. On the first sheet he found two different times for the same movement.

He wrote both down.
