# Scion of the Warp-Born Blood — The Authority to Amend

**Based on the work of:** *Scion of the Warp-Born Blood*  
**Original story by:** Christopher Vardeman / Mrcalzon02  
**Continuation:** The Authority to Amend

---

The physician placed two cups on the table. Mulvane reached for the nearer one with his left hand.

The water moved against the rim, but none spilled.

"That is better," the physician said.

"It is."

"Now read the second line."

Mulvane looked at the slate. The letters were black on an unlit surface. He could read them until he reached the final word, when a pale bar passed across his sight. He waited for it to disappear.

"How long?" the physician asked.

"Four breaths."

"Yesterday?"

"Six."

The physician entered the figures. Mulvane had learned not to mistake improvement for recovery. He had once watched a cousin make the same error during the House's exercises. The boy had been permitted to try again before the weakness had left him. Mulvane did not know what became of him after the second attempt.

"The allocation office has requested an expected date," the physician said.

"Have you given one?"

"No. They asked for a date, not a guess. I have some professional pride."

The restriction remained in force. Mulvane could attend the next review in a shielded room, provided he was brought there without exposure to the navigation lamps. He could not take a ship, open his third eye, or submit to a test intended to imitate the Astronomican's pressure.

He accepted the conditions. The House would not be able to call his compliance insubordination.

The physician set the examination slate aside. "You are worried about the review."

"About the figures."

"Which figures?"

"The ones that were not taken until after the grain was unloaded."

"That sounds like the allocation office's worry."

"They may make it mine."

The physician took the untouched second cup. It contained a bitter restorative that Mulvane had been hoping to avoid. He drank it without further argument.

---

The carrier's factor had submitted a loss schedule with three columns. The first contained the value of grain allegedly spoiled. The second contained charges for holding the ship beyond its contracted unloading window. The third contained the sum the owners wished House Drenal to pay.

The senior allocation officer had requested the temperature record from the carrier's stores. Now it lay beside the factor's survey.

"The earliest rise was before the deviation," the officer said.

The factor's representative, a narrow man in a serviceable coat, inclined his head. "A small rise. Within the tolerance accepted by the receiving factor."

"And the later rise?"

"After the scheduled berth was lost."

"Was the hold opened between those readings?"

"Only for inspection."

"By whom?"

"Our cargo master."

The officer turned to Mulvane. "You have seen the record."

"Yes."

"Does it establish that your delay caused the later rise?"

"No."

"Does it exclude that possibility?"

"No."

The representative seemed disappointed by the second answer. He had come prepared to argue against a Navigator who would deny everything.

Mulvane studied the timestamps. A refrigeration pump had already been cycling irregularly before the lighter's request reached the drydock. The carrier had remained within its declared tolerance at that time. After the delayed arrival, the temperature climbed farther, but the cargo master had opened a hold for inspection and a local auxiliary supply had briefly failed. Neither event was explained by the navigation record.

"The cargo was sold at a reduced grade," the representative said. "That loss is real."

"Then the loss belongs in the account," the senior officer replied. "Its cause is a separate matter."

"The House undertook a timely passage."

"The House completed a passage whose safety assessment changed the sequence. We are examining whether that assessment was within the Navigator's authority, whether the delay breached the agreement, and whether the alleged loss follows from it. Those are three questions."

The representative set a finger on the contract. "My employers have paid for certainty."

Mulvane understood the man's irritation. A client who paid for priority expected more than a collection of reasons why priority had become difficult. The representative was defending an obligation he had been sent to enforce.

"Your captain received the revised time," Mulvane said. "He completed the approach without refusing it."

"He had a ship to deliver."

"Yes."

"His cooperation does not waive our claim."

"I did not say it did."

The senior officer requested the pump's maintenance entries, the cargo master's inspection order, and the receiving factor's original grade certificate. He marked the loss schedule as disputed, not rejected. The representative objected to another delay in payment, then agreed to send the records because a rejected claim would take longer.

When he left, the senior officer did not congratulate Mulvane.

"Do not become fond of the temperature record," he said. "The next document may make your position worse."

"I know."

"Good. It has made mine more complicated already."

---

The receiving station's superintendent appeared by secured connection from a room where someone was arguing about bed linen.

She did not apologize for the noise.

"The duplicate charges are gone," she said. "The rest of the conversion bill is certified. We have supplied every seal the House requested."

The senior officer confirmed receipt. The supported conversion costs remained against Mulvane's service allocation. They had not become a personal purse debt; the House had already paid the station and was now determining where, within its own accounts, the expense would finally rest.

"The quarantine hold is still occupied?" he asked.

"Partly. We have moved the stable patients to proper wards in stages. The isolation teams will remain until the medical superintendent clears the last compartment."

"Is that work in the original conversion bill?"

"No. It is our continuing medical duty. If you wish to fund it, I will not object, but I will not disguise tomorrow's wages as yesterday's filters."

Mulvane looked up. The distinction mattered. He had paid for an emergency receiving space, not purchased the care of seven hundred and fourteen people. Some of those patients would require treatment long after he was cleared to navigate again.

"The lighter's owners have been sent a provisional recovery notice," the superintendent added. "Their shipmaster is asking whether you intend to charge her for the hold."

The liaison, seated at the end of the table, answered before Mulvane could speak.

"The House reserves its rights against any party that accepted a benefit."

"Then send the reservation to the owners," the superintendent said. "Not to the wards."

"We have not invoiced a patient."

"Not yet. The lighter's master has been asked to acknowledge the service account as recoverable. She will not sign. I do not blame her."

The senior officer requested the wording. It was brought up on the screen. The notice combined an acknowledgment of the station's service with a clause accepting possible repayment.

"Separate them," he said.

The liaison's face tightened. "That is a standard form."

"Then the standard form is doing two jobs. The captain can acknowledge what was received without agreeing who owes for it."

"An acknowledgment without recourse is worth little."

"It is worth exactly what it proves."

The officer ordered a corrected receipt and a temporary hold on collection against the lighter pending the allocation review. The hold did not discharge the cost. It did not establish that House Drenal must bear it forever. It stopped a provisional document from becoming an apparent admission of debt by a shipmaster who had never signed a Drenal passage contract.

The superintendent accepted the correction. Then she asked when the House would settle the three patients' death certificates.

"That is not our authority," the senior officer said.

"Good," she replied. "The families are waiting on ours."

She ended the connection. Mulvane sat with the thought that a person could survive the passage and still spend weeks waiting for a document to tell a family where the body was.

He knew none of the three names. He had no right to request them for the purpose of making his own account feel more complete.

---

The drydock clerk arrived in the afternoon with the original authorization for the superseded manifest.

His thumb had healed enough to leave the dressing off. The scar was a small pale crescent. He placed the authorization on the table, followed by the request that had preceded it.

"These are my copies," he said.

The liaison examined the seals. "You were reassigned while the review proceeded."

"Yes, sir."

"Why have you brought these yourself?"

"The records office released them to the hearing. I was instructed to deliver them."

The senior officer checked the dispatch order. The clerk had obeyed it.

Mulvane could see the care in every movement. The man had learned that a proper authorization could protect him from one accusation without protecting his position. He no longer touched a document until he had made certain the receiving officer was watching.

"Your request was valid," the senior officer said.

"Thank you, sir."

"That does not mean your former desk will be restored."

"I understand."

"The priority desk now requires separate hands for requesting an earlier manifest and certifying its release. That separation should have existed before this dispute."

The clerk did not argue. The rule was sensible. It also meant the position he had occupied no longer existed in the same form.

"Will his service record show misconduct?" Mulvane asked.

The liaison gave him a look. "That is a personnel matter."

"It arose from this review."

"The question can be answered," the senior officer said. He read the reassignment notice. "There is no finding of misconduct. There is a temporary reassignment. Advancement consequences have not been decided."

The clerk's eyes remained on the edge of the table.

Mulvane wanted to ask for more. He knew the House could treat such a request as an attempt to buy loyalty from a clerk who had assisted him. He had no command over the man's employment. He could not promise him a future.

"Then please put the distinction in the record," Mulvane said.

"It already is."

The clerk collected his receipt. As he turned to leave, Mulvane said, "You kept the request."

"Yes, lord."

"I am glad."

The man bowed. He did not smile. Mulvane had not repaired what had happened to him, and the clerk had no reason to pretend otherwise.

---

The senior officer's proposed change to the priority register occupied a single page.

It did not grant Navigators permission to disregard contracts. It did not require the allocation office to publish a hospital's medical details or a client's protected cargo. It required that, when a scheduled vessel was removed or displaced, the current manifest retain a marked reference to the superseded entry, the time of the change, and the office that had authorized it.

The reason could remain sealed in the restricted file. The existence of a change could not be scraped out of the working sequence.

"Two copies?" Mulvane asked.

"One held by allocation, one by the traffic desk. They will disagree occasionally. When they do, someone must resolve it before the next passage is assigned."

"That will slow a busy watch."

"Yes."

The liaison studied the page. "A clerk could use the marked reference to infer the presence of a restricted vessel."

"Possibly," the senior officer said. "We have provided for an unlabelled restricted entry, not an empty space. The traffic desk will know a place has been held without learning for whom."

"That could be abused to reserve a place without a client."

"Which is why the restricted entry must carry an accountable seal. Abuse of an accountable procedure is not cured by making the procedure invisible."

Mulvane kept his hands folded. The officer had arrived at the arrangement by examining the House's needs, not by adopting a Navigator's suggestion as an act of generosity. That made it more likely to survive the first difficult watch.

"Will this govern every Drenal manifest?" the liaison asked.

"No. It will be trialled at this drydock for thirty days. If the traffic desk cannot administer it, we will amend it. If it leaks restricted movements, we will stop it. If it prevents a priority alteration from disappearing without record, we will have learned something."

"Who signs the trial?"

"I do. The House will review my authority afterward."

For the first time Mulvane understood that the senior officer had exposed himself to a kind of cost. Not the cost of opening an eye twice. The cost of putting his own name on a procedure that might fail in front of his superiors.

The liaison read the paper again. He suggested that the traffic desk retain the sealed reference only until the vessel had passed. The officer refused. A record destroyed after its immediate use could not answer a later claim.

They argued over the retention period and reached a compromise: the working desk would retain the reference through the next accounting review, while the sealed authority remained in the permanent House archive. It was not a perfect safeguard. It was one that two offices might actually obey.

The officer signed the trial order. Mulvane was not asked to sign.

---

The lighter's shipmaster sent her acknowledgment the following watch. She accepted the corrected receipt as evidence that her vessel had been received and that House Drenal's claim against its owners remained under review. She expressly declined to accept liability for the conversion.

Her message to Mulvane was shorter.

*The ward confirms the remaining transfers are proceeding. My injured Navigator is still under care. I have kept the papers. Thank you for sending the right ones.*

Mulvane read it twice, then had it filed. He did not reply with a promise he could not keep.

At his next examination, the tremor was nearly absent. The afterimage was not.

"Can I return to routine passage planning?" he asked.

"With the lamps shielded and no sight exercise. You may study ordinary trajectories."

"Can I take a commission?"

"No."

The physician wrote a new restriction rather than extending the old one without examination. The House would receive a current medical finding. Mulvane would remain unfit for the work that had made him valuable.

He asked whether the injury would last.

"I cannot tell you yet," the physician said. "I would prefer not to discover the answer by making it worse."

Mulvane accepted that too. He was becoming accustomed to a kind of obedience that did not begin with the allocation office.

---

The interim finding arrived at the end of the watch.

The House accepted that the original hazard examination fell within a Navigator's professional discretion. It did not approve the uncontracted passage retrospectively. The supported station conversion expense remained charged to Mulvane's service allocation pending final accounting. The carrier's damages claim remained open, with the pump and receiving-grade records outstanding. The clerk's personnel review was separate and unfinished. The thirty-day register trial was authorized for the drydock alone.

No patient death was attributed to Mulvane or to the delay. No finding declared that the deaths would have occurred regardless. The corrected times remained attached to the medical inquiry, where they belonged.

The liaison brought the order to Mulvane's recovery chamber.

"You have obtained a procedural concession," he said.

"The officer has authorized a trial."

"Do not mistake that for an acquittal."

"I have not."

The liaison looked at the medical restriction on the table. "Your replacement has completed two inward passages while you have been unavailable."

"Was the work done safely?"

"Yes."

"Then the assignments were met."

The liaison seemed almost offended by the absence of jealousy. He had expected Mulvane to defend his place. Mulvane had spent his life defending it, often without noticing. He could not make his hand steady by wanting the House to need him.

When the man left, Mulvane set the interim order beside the two manifests that had begun the matter. The first had contained an absence. The second had recorded a vessel and a delay. Now there were more papers: a disputed cargo claim, a supported hospital expense, a clerk's unfinished review, a physician's prohibition, a shipmaster's limited acknowledgment, and a thirty-day instruction bearing another man's signature.

The papers had not made the House kind. They had made several decisions harder to conceal inside one another.

From the passage chamber beyond the shielding came the low signal that warned of a Navigator preparing to work. Mulvane felt his trained attention move toward it. His third eye remained bound. The physician had told him not to open it, and he did not.

The next ship would leave without him. The cost of his earlier choice was still being counted. For the moment he had nothing further to add to the account.

He put the papers in their proper order and went back to the ordinary trajectories he had been cleared to read.
