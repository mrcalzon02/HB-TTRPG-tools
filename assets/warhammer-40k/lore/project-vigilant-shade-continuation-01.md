# Project VIGILANT SHADE

## Installment VII — The Line on the Map

General Snadler Helgren had been asking for a second camera angle when the iron pipe struck the Arbites trooper.

The image on the Highmount display jumped. The gunship's pilot swore over an open channel. For two seconds the command staff saw little but rain and a warehouse wall, then the camera recovered and showed a shield still pinned to the masonry. A man's boots were moving beneath it.

The general watched until they stopped.

"That recording," he said. "Keep the original."

His signals officer glanced at the authentication report beside the console. The old Commissar's codes had passed every check Highmount could perform without referring the matter beyond the planet. None of the officers present had been able to explain how a dead man possessed them.

Helgren had spent the last quarter-hour demanding an explanation.

He was finished demanding one.

"Major, what can we actually move?"

The operations officer brought up the garrison duty board. Two companies were at readiness, though neither was fully assembled. A third had been dispersed among the outer districts for transport security. The nearest armoured vehicles were at the eastern depot, where their crews were still waiting for a fuel allotment that had been promised before the storm began.

"First company can leave within twelve minutes. Second will take longer. The engineer section is already with the transport pool."

"Give the first company the eastern road. Engineers with them. No artillery into the estate until the civilians are clear."

"The estate's jurisdiction—"

"Can explain itself afterward."

Helgren looked back at the image. Figures were moving at the edge of the tunnel mouth. Some were too small for the scale of the warehouse doors. Others were not.

"Inform the planetary prefect that I am assuming military responsibility for the breach perimeter. The Arbites retain custody of their original investigation and every witness they can still keep alive. I want their captain to understand that."

The signals officer began transmitting.

"And the Commissar, sir?"

Helgren regarded the figure standing beside the map table on the distant feed. The man had the claw, the eye, the voice and the codes. The man also appeared younger than the official portrait hanging in Highmount's western hall.

"Do not certify him. Do not arrest him. Captain Trellis will take tactical advice from him until I have someone better placed to judge the ground. My officers retain their commissions and their orders."

The major made a notation.

Helgren caught his expression.

"Yes," the general said. "It is an untidy arrangement. Put it in writing."

---

Rain had found its way through the command tent's roof seam and was dripping onto a map of the northern warehouses.

Captain Trellis moved the map before the water reached the ink. Waldorf, who had been holding a receiver to one ear for so long that the cartilage hurt, watched the captain flatten the wet paper beneath a steel ruler.

"First company is coming," Trellis said.

"How many?"

"Enough to begin a proper line. Not enough to end this."

Yarrick had already turned toward the northern feed. "Where are the civilians?"

"Still moving from the outer workshops. The southern road is open, but half the vehicles are going the wrong way."

"Why?"

Waldorf knew that answer. "The estate traffic gates only accept registered delivery permits after curfew. The evacuation officers have been overriding them one at a time."

Trellis stared at him. "Can they be left open?"

"They can. The gate supervisor won't do it without a signed assumption of liability."

For the first time that night, the captain looked less astonished by the returned Commissar than by the perfectly ordinary workings of New Presidio.

"Bring me the supervisor."

Waldorf was already calling him. He had spent three years learning which officers could be persuaded to ignore a regulation, which ones required a written order and which ones were waiting for someone else to accept the blame. There was comfort, almost, in discovering that the world had not abandoned every familiar habit simply because a dead war hero had come back to life.

Outside, a burst of autogun fire rattled across the northern yard.

Yarrick pointed at the warehouse roofs. "Move the wounded station behind that second building. Their present route crosses the fire lane."

"We have a field dressing post there," Trellis said.

"Then it will have to move."

Trellis looked at the feed, saw the angle, and nodded. He gave the order himself.

Waldorf relayed it to the precinct captain's aide. He heard the aide repeat the location correctly, then request two additional stretchers. There were none to send. The medicae vehicle assigned to the evacuation was still trapped behind a freight hauler whose driver had abandoned it in the gate queue.

"One more thing," Waldorf said. "The officer struck by the pipe. We need his name recorded before the bodies start moving."

Trellis's eyes shifted toward him. For a moment Waldorf expected the old answer: later, when the shooting stops.

"Get it," the captain said. "But stay inside the cordon."

Waldorf put down the receiver and reached for his case slate.

He had not been ordered to abandon the investigation. Nobody had yet remembered to give him that order.

---

The northern warehouse had once received agricultural machinery. Its loading bays were deep enough to take three haulers abreast, and the concrete apron sloped toward a drainage trench now running full of black rainwater.

That trench was keeping the Arbites alive.

Orks attempting to cross it lost their footing in the flood and came under fire from the windows above. The defenders had learned to aim at the creatures climbing out rather than waste ammunition on those still struggling in the water.

The Orks had learned something too.

A pair of them emerged carrying a torn loading shutter between them. It was too heavy to be an ordinary shield, and too broad for the narrow gap. They drove it forward anyway, taking shots across its surface while smaller creatures crowded behind.

Yarrick saw the movement on the feed.

"They're testing the approach," he said.

Trellis gave a short order over the vox. The upper windows ceased fire for a moment. A gunner shifted position to the far loading gantry, where the shutter's side was exposed.

The next burst tore through the two carriers. The metal sheet fell into the drainage trench, forming an accidental bridge.

"Destroy that," Yarrick said.

A demolition charge was brought from the warehouse stores, passed hand to hand down the inner stairs, and thrown from a maintenance opening. It detonated in the water with a flat, brutal report. The shutter broke against the concrete bank. So did a section of the drain wall.

The flood changed direction.

Within a minute it was pouring into the lower warehouse floor, soaking ammunition crates that had been stacked beside the entrance.

"Move those rounds!" the local sergeant shouted.

A trooper slipped on the flooded ramp and struck his head against the railing. Two others pulled him clear. For several moments the men at the lower entrance were too occupied saving their supplies and their comrade to watch the breach.

A larger Ork came over the broken bank.

It had made a club from a length of hoist chain and a coupling the size of a man's skull. The first blow crushed the guard rail. The second caught a trooper across the shoulder and threw him into the doorway.

The sergeant fired until his magazine emptied. Then he dragged the wounded man inside and ordered the door shut.

The Ork hit it once, twice, three times.

Yarrick watched the camera shake with each impact. "Do not open it."

"The sergeant's men are still in there," Trellis said.

"They are behind a door that is holding. Give them another exit."

Trellis studied the floor plan and sent the order to cut through the service wall into the adjacent loading bay. A cutting torch was already in the engineers' truck; the truck was four minutes away.

Four minutes was an astonishing length of time to ask a door to survive.

The sergeant and his men held it for three.

Then the first military carrier came through the eastern gate and its pintle gun began firing across the yard.

The Ork turned toward the new sound. That was enough. The door stayed shut.

No one in the command tent cheered. Trellis was already asking for the sergeant's casualty count, and Yarrick was examining the next approach.

---

Waldorf found Henshaw at the temporary casualty station with a torn strip of cloth wrapped around his forearm.

"Yours?" Waldorf asked.

"Mostly the warehouse's."

Henshaw had been helping lift a wounded patrolman when a shard of glass from a shattered window cut him. The bandage was badly tied. Waldorf tightened it, receiving a grateful wince.

"Carver?"

"South gate. He got the supervisor to sign."

"Good."

Henshaw looked toward the warehouse. "They're bringing out the man from the shield."

The body came beneath a waterproof sheet. The two stretcher bearers were moving carefully, as though the man might still feel the jolts.

Waldorf stood aside and asked for the casualty slate. The name had been entered, along with the squad, time and place. He copied the identifying number into his own case record and noted which medic had received the body.

The sergeant who had witnessed the strike was still alive. He was not available to give a statement. Waldorf wrote that down too.

A field clerk was collecting loose equipment in a crate. The dead man's shield lay beside it, its central plate bent around the hole left by the pipe.

"That stays separate," Waldorf said.

The clerk looked up. "It was damaged in action."

"I can see that."

"All damaged equipment goes to salvage."

"Not that one."

The clerk's expression hardened. "Detective, we're short of space and I'm short of people."

Waldorf showed him the numbered evidence seal in his palm. "Give me thirty seconds to mark it. Then you can move it wherever the casualty office requires. I only need the chain."

The clerk considered him, then pushed the shield aside with his boot.

Waldorf photographed both faces of the plate and the deformed opening, recorded the clerk's name, and attached a duplicate seal to the casualty register. The pipe itself had been carried into the warehouse when the line was pulled back; its location remained unconfirmed. He wrote that down rather than invent a custody history.

A senior Arbites officer approached through the rain.

"The estate file is suspended. Military emergency."

"Yes, sir."

"Then why are you making an evidence inventory?"

"Because it's a military emergency."

The officer stared at him.

"Someone will have to account for where this came from," Waldorf said. "The first Ork, the tunnel, the dead Mechanicus representative, the estate's own workers. The army can kill what's in front of us. It can't give us yesterday back."

The officer looked at the shield, then at the growing row of stretchers.

"Use a duplicate ledger," he said at last. "The precinct original goes to the captain. No civilian estate clerk gets it."

Waldorf nodded. It was more cooperation than he had expected.

He made two copies of the first page.

---

The reinforcement column was not a parade.

Its leading carrier had lost a headlamp on the road. The second arrived with a track tension warning and a crewman holding the side hatch shut because its latch had failed. Infantry dismounted in the rain and immediately began asking for a dry place to stack ammunition.

There was none.

Captain Trellis met the company commander beside the eastern gate. The two officers bent over a map on the carrier's bonnet while water ran down their collars.

"Three positions," Trellis said. "North warehouse, east freight lane, south evacuation road. The Arbites are holding the first. Your men take the second and keep the third open."

"What's the enemy strength?"

"Unknown. More than the first reports. They have improvised heavy weapons."

The commander glanced toward Yarrick, who stood several paces away watching the warehouse roofs.

"Is that—"

"The identity question is being handled. The enemy question is not."

The commander accepted the distinction. He called his platoon leaders and began dividing the men.

Yarrick stepped forward only when they reached the eastern freight lane.

"Your reserve belongs behind that junction," he said. "Not beside the loading bays. The Orks will come toward the loudest guns. When they do, you'll want men who can still move."

The commander examined the map. "If they come through the drainage culvert, my reserve will be too far back."

"Then close the culvert."

"We have no charges left at this position."

"The engineers have them."

Trellis nodded to the company commander. "They do. I have the allocation."

The commander made the alteration without surrendering his authority. Yarrick did not ask him to. The difference mattered more than any salute.

At the southern road, civilian traffic finally began moving in two continuous columns. Carver stood at the gate with a rain-soaked permit book, waving through haulers that would ordinarily have been turned back for expired registration. Each vehicle carried more people than its manufacturer had intended. The passengers held children, blankets, toolboxes, food tins, and whatever else they had managed to lift before the evacuation order reached them.

A woman refused to leave because her husband was still on the late shift in a machine shop.

Carver took her name, sent a runner to the shop's muster point, and asked her to keep moving. She argued until a burst of gunfire came from the north. Then she climbed aboard the next hauler, staring backward until the rain swallowed the buildings.

The convoy moved.

It was not orderly. It was not complete. But the road was no longer blocked by a gate that demanded the correct paperwork from people fleeing for their lives.

---

In the command tent, Yarrick reached for a map weight and missed it.

Only by the width of a finger.

His hand corrected before the object moved. Nobody noticed. Trellis was speaking to the engineers, Waldorf had returned with the casualty slate, and the vox operator was trying to raise a patrol whose signal had vanished behind the freight sheds.

Yarrick remembered other maps, other rooms, other officers. He remembered the weight of a claw against a command table. He remembered it so clearly that for an instant the present metal felt wrong beneath his fingers.

He looked down.

The claw obeyed him. The servos answered without delay. Its polished joints were almost offensively free of the small hesitations and scars that had accompanied years of use.

He closed it slowly.

There was no time to consider the difference. The eastern culvert had become the next problem, and the first military company had only just taken its positions.

"Commissar," Trellis said, "Highmount is requesting your assessment of the next two hours."

Yarrick looked at the live feed. Orks were gathering in the mouth of the tunnel, shoving and fighting one another for space. A few carried pieces of machinery. One was dragging a length of cable that still sparked against the ground.

"Two hours?" he said. "I cannot give you two hours. I can give you the next approach."

Trellis's mouth tightened. "Then give me that."

"Their numbers will press against the northern mouth. Some will test the east, especially once they hear the carriers. The smaller ones will try the drains and service tunnels. You need men watching the places too narrow for the large ones. And you must keep the civilian road open even when every gunner in the estate wants to turn south."

Trellis repeated the orders in his own words over the military channel. He made no attempt to attribute them to a dead hero or a living one.

Yarrick watched him work.

It was not a glorious command. It was a wet, hurried defense built from incomplete maps, frightened policemen and soldiers who had been eating their evening meal when the order arrived.

It would have to do.

He was still looking at the map when a runner brought word that the culvert charges had been set. The engineers wanted permission to fire them before the Orks reached the junction. Trellis granted it after the last drainage crew reported clear.

The explosion lifted a dark column of water and masonry into the rain.

The eastern lane remained open.

For the moment.

---

Near dawn, Highmount's second company had reached the outer district but not the estate. Its transports were delayed by the same civilian movement they had been sent to protect. The medicae service had established a collection point outside the southern road, and the engineers had marked three structures unsafe for occupation.

The northern warehouse was still contested. The Orks had not been driven back into the depths. They had, however, been prevented from spreading beyond the first industrial perimeter.

General Helgren received a casualty return with blanks where the missing patrols ought to have been. He refused to sign a final count and ordered the incomplete return circulated as incomplete. The prefect's office sent a protest concerning the military occupation of noble property. He placed it beneath the ammunition report.

At the estate, Waldorf sat on an empty ration crate and made a third copy of the evidence register.

His prosthetic leg had begun to ache where the socket pressed against swollen skin. He loosened the fastening by one notch and kept writing.

The original estate investigation had not disappeared. It was buried beneath a battle, which was not the same thing. The death of the Mechanicus representative, the armed noble, the industrial site, the first Ork body and the tunnel mouth still belonged to a chain of events. Waldorf did not know what connected them. He had not been below the estate. He had not seen a cloning vat. He could not prove that the man in the greatcoat had been dead or restored to life.

He could preserve the order in which the evidence had reached him.

Trellis found him beside the ration crates.

"Highmount wants a witness statement."

"Which witness?"

"Yours."

"About the Commissar?"

"About what you saw."

Waldorf considered that distinction and closed the ledger.

"Then I can give them one."

He rose slowly, waited for the prosthetic to take his weight, and followed the captain into the tent.

Across the yard, Yarrick stood beneath the warehouse lamps and watched the soldiers change magazines. They were exhausted, wet and short of ammunition. They had held.

He did not know who had made him. He did not know whether the memories that came so easily belonged to the flesh that carried them. He knew only that the next Ork would not wait for those questions to be answered.

Behind the sealed depths of the estate, machinery continued its work without the permission of anyone on the surface.

And in New Presidio, a military line had finally been drawn on a map that the enemy had not yet agreed to respect.
