# Of Blood and Wires: The Litanies of Tenelja Station

## Chapter IV — The Price of a Morning

Sister Arbentia woke to the sound of a pipe knocking behind the wall.

It knocked three times, paused, and knocked twice more. She knew the sequence. The washroom supply had begun to draw against an air pocket again. Someone on the deck below would complain, a maintenance clerk would mark the complaint received, and a work crew would attend to it after the next cargo-cycle unless a pipe burst first.

For the moment, it was merely a noise.

She lay still beneath the linen and listened to the station. There was the thin electrical hiss from her vidscreen, which she had neglected to switch off. There was the gravity coil's familiar high whine. Beneath both lay the long, patient vibration of machinery that had outlived the men who understood its first design.

The room's chronometer said she had slept five hours and nineteen minutes.

She had arranged to meet Rix after six.

That gave her enough time to wash, dress, and be late in a manner that could plausibly be blamed upon duty. It was a habit of hers, though she had never decided whether it was vanity or caution.

At the warming plate she made recaf too thin to be respectable. Her ration allotment would not renew until the next station accounting period, and she had already used most of the stronger powder after the void-burned man's last night in the infirmaria. She ate a square of compressed grain standing up.

A small paper notice had been pushed beneath her door.

**WATER ALLOCATION: PERSONAL IMMERSION BASINS SUSPENDED DURING THIRD WATCH.**

She read it twice. The printed explanation referred to conservation, inspection and temporary equalization of pressure. There was no date for restoration. The station had not troubled to remove the word *temporary* from the previous notice, either.

Arbentia folded the paper and set it beside her three rejected petitions for scriptorum access. The stack was beginning to look like a devotional practice.

She put on her plain grey station clothes rather than the white of her order. Today she wanted to be seen by one man and, if the Emperor was feeling unusually generous, ignored by everybody else.

---

The lower refectory was half full of salvage crews finishing a night shift. Their work clothes bore the colours of companies that had changed owners so often the old insignia showed through the new paint. A damaged lumen panel blinked above the serving hatch. Nobody looked up at it.

Rix sat beneath a vent with two cups and a plate of something fried.

He was as she remembered: narrow-shouldered, restless, silver-eyed, his dark hair cut badly enough to suggest he had done it himself. A pale burn ran across the side of one hand. He kept that hand wrapped around the cup as if warmth could make the mark disappear.

"You came," he said.

"I said I would."

"You also said the same thing last time."

"Last time I had a man trying to die."

"Did he?"

"Not for want of effort."

Rix pushed one of the cups towards her. Real recaf, or something sufficiently close that she did not wish to investigate its origin.

She drank and closed her eyes for an instant.

"You've been robbing officers."

"Only the ones who can afford it."

"That narrows the list considerably."

He grinned. Then he reached across the table, took her free hand, and ran his thumb over the crease left by a glove cuff. It was a familiar gesture. He did it when he had been away long enough to worry that the next meeting might feel like the first.

Arbentia turned her hand beneath his and held on.

For several minutes they ate and spoke of things that did not matter very much: a cargo hauler whose captain had insisted that a crate of devotional statues be counted as fragile agricultural machinery; the latest ration inspection; a station singer whose performances were excellent until he began to explain the theology of his own lyrics.

Rix asked whether she still listened to the old low-g music.

"Whenever the receiver works."

"I could look at it."

"You said that three meetings ago."

"And I have thought about it very seriously."

She laughed. He looked pleased in a way that made her suddenly aware of how seldom she laughed at work.

Then she asked about the man from Xanadu.

Rix's hand stopped moving.

"Which one?"

"The one who lost most of his face to the void and spent half the last quarter-cycle costing us our remaining medical stock."

"Oh," Rix said. "Him."

It was not a promising answer.

---

His name, Rix told her, was Beren Holt.

He was not a gunner, not a pilot, and not a man accustomed to the dangerous work for which the company charged danger rates. He was a fitting assistant, hired to inventory salvaged clamps and make certain that the tools issued at the start of a watch returned at the end.

"He shouldn't have been outside the inner service hatch," Rix said.

"Was he?"

"I wasn't there. The work party says he was helping with a jammed release. The company says it was an authorized adjustment."

"Those aren't the same account."

"No."

Rix pushed the empty plate aside. His thumb had found the burn on his other hand and was worrying at it.

"His suit seal went during the release. Or just before it. They pulled him back through the lock, but by then—" He stopped. "You've seen him."

"Enough of him."

"The crew wants the fitting examined. Xanadu wants the equipment back in service. The dock office has placed a hold on the damaged assembly until somebody signs an incident declaration."

"Who has to sign?"

"That is what they are arguing about."

He knew the crew's version because he had heard it from the two men who carried Holt into the lock. He had not seen the fitting. He had not read the official declaration. Arbentia asked him which of those things he was certain of, and he answered without taking offence.

"I know he was hurt. I know the release was jammed. I know the men who pulled him in haven't been paid their last hazard allowance. Everything else is somebody's account."

She appreciated that. It would have been easy for him to give her a villain. She had spent enough time among the dying to distrust explanations that arrived before the blood was cleaned away.

"His discharge papers say he can walk," she said. "That doesn't mean he can work."

"Xanadu has him in a convalescent berth. They call it light duty because the company doesn't have a category for someone who can breathe for ten minutes and then needs to sit down."

"Who pays for the berth?"

"At present? The company. They'll try to move it against his compensation allotment if the claim is approved. If it isn't, they'll call it an advance."

She looked at him.

"Don't."

"I haven't said anything."

"You were about to tell me the rules are the rules."

"I was about to tell you the rules were written by someone who never had to use them."

He leaned back. His smile returned, but not quite all the way.

"I can show you where the fitting came from, if that's useful."

"Would you be allowed?"

"No."

"Then don't promise it."

He considered this, and nodded. It was one of the reasons she continued to see him. He was capable of hearing a boundary without treating it as a challenge.

---

They left the refectory by the long gallery overlooking the salvage berths.

Beyond the armourglass, Pelzane hung in a pale curve beneath the station. Cargo tenders crawled between docking pylons with their navigation lamps blinking against the black. A service arm stood idle beside an empty cradle, its warning stripe interrupted where an old repair plate had been fitted.

Rix pointed to a small tug moored beneath the arm.

"That's mine for the next three watches."

"You own a tug?"

"I have permission to be blamed for one."

He was assigned to a salvage convoy due to shift a damaged hull section from the outer moorings. A successful transfer would earn him enough additional credit to clear an old equipment charge. A missed watch would cost him the assignment.

Arbentia had been thinking of asking him to remain near the infirmaria if Holt needed help getting through the follow-up examination.

She did not ask.

Instead she said, "What happens if the dock office keeps the release assembly?"

"Then the next job uses the spare, if we have one. If we don't, they borrow from another berth. Every delay moves somebody else's cargo. The owners are very good at counting that part."

"And the man?"

Rix looked at her.

"The owners count him too."

He put an arm around her shoulders. She rested her head against him, just for a moment. The glass was cold through her sleeve. She could feel the tremor of the station's machinery through the deck.

"I can't get away every time someone from Xanadu comes into your ward," he said.

"I know."

"I can come when I can."

"I know that as well."

There was more they might have said, but a cargo alarm sounded twice and a crew began moving along the far gantry. Rix checked the watch dial on his wrist.

"Will you be here tomorrow?"

"After second watch. Unless someone tries to die."

"Could you ask them to wait?"

"I shall submit the request to the appropriate office."

He kissed her beside the armourglass. It was brief, unceremonious and wholly unlike the devotions she performed at the infirmaria. She had no desire to make it resemble them.

When he went, she watched until he was lost among the grey coats and hazard-striped helmets. Then she took the service lift up to the medical decks.

---

The scriptorum annex occupied a narrow passage between the infirmaria and the station's older administration spine. Arbentia had passed it hundreds of times. Its door was thick, its hinges maintained, and its reader plate brighter than any light in the ward.

She had come to ask for one thing rather than a fourth general petition.

The clerk behind the grille was a thin man with a swollen knuckle and a pencil worn almost to the metal ferrule. He recognized her before she spoke.

"Your previous determinations remain in force, Sister."

"I haven't come to appeal them."

He looked doubtful.

"I need the old post-void exposure rehabilitation tables. The printed ward summary stops at discharge. We have a survivor whose breathing tolerance varies between examinations. I want to know which observations the earlier cases recorded after they left the ward."

"That is advanced study material."

"I know."

"Your station has a qualified Magos."

"Who has his own patients."

"Then ask him."

"I intend to. I am asking whether there is a supervised reading period available to a nurse."

The clerk consulted the allocation ledger. It took him longer than she expected, and she felt a small, unreasonable hope.

"There are two supervised hours this cycle," he said. "Both assigned to certified trainees. Reallocation would require a sponsor and a replacement duty arrangement."

"Who approves a sponsor?"

"The infirmaria's medical authorities."

"And the duty arrangement?"

"Your order."

She almost smiled. A door guarded by two keys was at least a door. Until now she had been told it was a wall.

"Can I have the requirement in writing?"

He examined her as if this were the first suspicious thing she had said.

"Why?"

"Because I don't wish to ask the same question four times."

The clerk made a copy of the allocation rule and stamped it. He did not give her a reading period. He did not promise one. The stamp cost the annex nothing, which may have been why he was permitted to use it.

As she left, the corridor lumens dimmed together, recovered, and dimmed once more.

A maintenance servitor stood beneath the junction box with a panel removed. Its manipulator held a replacement contact, but its other hand was empty. A handwritten tag on the panel read **SECONDARY FEED—AWAITING MATCHED FUSE**.

Arbentia looked at the tag and went on. The infirmaria's emergency lamps had separate reserve cells. She had checked them last watch. The corridor did not.

---

Holt arrived for his scheduled review in a wheeled chair, accompanied by a Xanadu porter who had brought the wrong invoice.

He had recovered enough of his face to frown at the paper.

"That's for a man with two hands," he said through a voice roughened by injury.

Arbentia examined the number, compared it to his discharge record, and set it aside.

"Then it isn't yours."

The porter looked relieved. "Can I take him back when you're done?"

"When he's done," Arbentia replied.

Holt had walked twelve paces from his berth that morning before his chest tightened. The company had recorded that as an improvement over nine. His oxygen readings were acceptable while seated, less reassuring after movement. His right hand was still bandaged, but it was the breathing that concerned Arbentia.

Bethany came to inspect the grafts. She did not ask Holt to endure unnecessary pain as proof of fortitude. She warmed the dressing fluid between her palms, told him when the next movement would hurt, and waited for his consent.

Abraxas arrived with a portable analyzer and immediately disputed the station's decision to use a walking-distance measure without a recorded rest interval.

"Nine paces yesterday," he said through the augmitter, "twelve today. A numerical increase without comparable conditions is not a recovery trend."

Holt looked from the Magos to Bethany.

"Does that mean I can't walk?"

"It means," Bethany said, "you may walk when your body can bear it. We shall not make a wager of it."

"Resting respiratory stability does not establish exertional reserve," Abraxas added.

"I believe that is what I said."

"No. You described permission. I described measurement."

"Then we have both made ourselves useful."

Arbentia watched Holt's breathing settle. The familiar argument had not disappeared; it had become smaller, fitted around the man rather than laid across him.

She showed Abraxas the discharge record and the new measurements.

"Is there a follow-up schedule for cases with damaged pulmonary tissue after exposure? Not just the discharge threshold. The longer interval."

"There are archival cohorts."

"May I consult them?"

"Through my office."

"I have the annex rule. It permits a sponsored reading period."

His optical array paused over the stamped paper. Bethany glanced at it too.

"Two hours," Abraxas said. "Insufficient for a proper comparison."

"Two hours is more than none."

"You would require a supervising authority."

"Yes."

"And your absence would require coverage."

"Yes."

"Then you have identified the administrative variables. You have not solved them."

"No," Arbentia said. "But now I know what they are."

Bethany took the paper from her and read it without comment. She handed it back before the porter returned.

Holt was not discharged from the review with a miraculous cure. He received a revised exertion limit, a documented rest interval, and another appointment. The porter was instructed to take the corrected sheet to the company's duty clerk, not to interpret it as permission to return the man to a cargo watch.

Holt thanked Arbentia quietly.

She was not certain whether he thanked her for the medicine or for making the invoice go away. Both had been worth doing.

---

At the end of second watch, Arbentia found the corridor maintenance tag unchanged.

The servitor had gone. The empty fuse holder remained open beneath its warning cover. A clerk had added a second notation: **PART REQUEST CONFIRMED—ISSUE PENDING**.

She made a copy of the tag number for the ward's equipment book. She did not call it an imminent catastrophe. The lamps still worked. She knew the difference between a fault and the future she feared it might become.

Back in her quarters, the water notice still lay beside her petitions. She added the scriptorum rule to the stack, then moved it away from the refusals. It was not a refusal. It was a price.

Two supervised hours, a sponsor, a replacement nurse, and an institution willing to recognize the need.

She could ask Bethany for the duty arrangement. She could ask Abraxas for the sponsorship. Neither would give anything away merely because she wanted it. Both had patients, schedules and reasons to refuse. For the first time, however, she could ask a question to which the answer might be something other than *no functional need*.

Her receiver hissed when she turned it on. The music came through on the third attempt, a thin, imperfect viol line under the mutter of an ancient sermon.

A message waited on the small terminal beside it. Rix had sent it before taking his tug out.

**HOLT'S CREW HAVE BEEN ASKED FOR SEPARATE STATEMENTS. I HAVEN'T SEEN THE FITTING. STILL OWE YOU A LOOK AT THE RECEIVER.**

She read it once and put it away.

She would not use his affection to get documents he was not entitled to surrender. She would not pretend that her concern for Holt gave her authority over a salvage investigation. She would ask for the medical records she was entitled to seek, and she would do the work that followed if anyone granted her the time.

The pipes knocked again behind the wall.

Arbentia listened to the five familiar blows, finished the last of her recaf, and began writing a request for two hours of supervised study.

It was a very small demand to make of an ancient station.

She had learned not to confuse small with inexpensive.
