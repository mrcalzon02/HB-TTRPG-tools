# The Long Patrol of Broussard

## VIII. A CAPTAIN'S SHAME

**Author:** Mrcalzon02 / Christopher Vardeman  
**Based on the work of:** *The Long Patrol of Broussard*

*An original non-commercial Warhammer 40,000 fan chronicle. Warhammer 40,000 and associated marks and setting elements are the property of Games Workshop. This fan work is not endorsed by or affiliated with Games Workshop.*

Broussard knew enough about mariners to understand that almost every vessel on these waters carried secrets.


Not grand secrets.


Not necessarily heretical secrets.


Usually the small, practical sins accumulated by people who spent too many months operating expensive machinery beyond easy reach of inspectors.


A bypassed warning circuit because the replacement relay would not arrive until spring.


A pump technically overdue for overhaul but still making the correct noises.


A section of hull plating repaired with material that was almost the correct specification.


Cargo moved before the paperwork caught up with it.


A maintenance interval creatively interpreted.


A pressure valve that had been officially replaced three years ago and unofficially repaired seven times since.


Ships accumulated such things the way old soldiers accumulated scars.


Everyone knew about them.


Nobody discussed them unless something went wrong.


And something had gone very wrong aboard the Hesperant Vale.


Broussard could therefore forgive embarrassment.


The vessel had probably completed dozens of crossings like this.


Perhaps hundreds.


Its captain and crew knew these waters.


They knew the pressure ridges, the seasonal routes, the ice formations and the particular hazards of taking a multi-thousand-tonne freight skimmer across an ocean that periodically became a continent.


Getting caught by something as simple as an unexpected ice spur punching through the hull would be humiliating.


Especially for an experienced captain.


A professional mariner could survive storms, mechanical failures, incompetent dockmasters, unreliable cargo factors and Administratum inspectors without losing sleep.


But run one's ship onto a piece of ice?


That would linger.


Add to that the indignity of requiring rescue by the Imperial Guard and Broussard could understand why the crew looked uncomfortable.


The military had arrived.


The Mechanicus had arrived.


Soon there would be reports.


Inspections.


Questions.


Damage assessments.


Insurance claims.


Cargo audits.


Engineering reviews.


Someone somewhere would eventually ask whether this accident could have been prevented.


Someone else would ask whether procedures had been followed.


Then someone truly dangerous would ask whether those procedures had been properly documented.


That alone was enough to frighten honest men.


So Broussard could excuse shame.


He could excuse embarrassment.


He could excuse nervousness.


What bothered him was that the expressions aboard the Hesperant Vale did not quite fit any of those things.


There was something else.


A tension beneath the surface.


Not merely we have made a terrible mistake.


Something closer to we are worried about what you are going to discover now that you are here.


He could not identify it.


That irritated him.


Broussard preferred dangers with shapes.


An enemy position had a location.


A predator had teeth.


A damaged reactor had temperature readings.


Human fear was less cooperative.


It leaked through pauses.


Glances.


People becoming suddenly busy when officers approached.


Crewmen who answered questions correctly but too carefully.


He hated that kind of uncertainty.


It made him downright angry.


Not because he believed the freighter crew guilty of anything.


Because if there was something important they were concealing, then every minute spent discovering it the difficult way was another minute in which his own soldiers could die from somebody else's silence.


Second Officer Holt led them deeper through the vessel.


The interior matched what Broussard expected from an aging commercial freight skimmer.


Narrow corridors.


Exposed piping.


Bulkheads painted in industrial cream that had surrendered to decades of boots, grease and cargo impacts.


Emergency lumen strips threw harsh yellow light along the passageways while several sections remained on reduced power.


Every few meters another crewman passed them carrying tools, cables, medicae supplies or some other urgent burden.


Most saluted Gabriel.


Several nearly forgot to salute Broussard.


That amused him more than it offended him.


The black coat had advantages.


They descended toward engineering.


The air grew warmer.


Then louder.


Pumps.


Emergency generators.


Ventilation fans.


The distant grinding hum of machinery being forced to perform functions it had not expected to perform when it woke that morning.


A metallic smell thickened in the air.


Electrical smoke.


Hot insulation.


Burned lubricant.


Then came voices.


One human.


One unmistakably Mechanicus.


They were arguing.


Loudly.


Holt slowed.


Broussard stopped behind him.


The junior officer's expression contained the exhausted resignation of a man who had spent some time listening to this already.


"What?" Broussard asked.


Holt looked toward the engineering hatch.


"The captain, sir."


Broussard listened.


A human voice carried through the compartment.


"If you bridge that line directly you'll overload the entire main bus!"


A second voice answered in the layered mechanical resonance typical of a senior tech-priest.


"Your assessment is based upon manufacturer tolerances which have already been exceeded by thirty-seven percent."


"Yes, because I know this ship!"


"Familiarity does not alter electrical resistance."


"It does when half the resistance compensators were replaced with Meredian pattern regulators nine years ago!"


Silence.


Then the Mechanicus voice:


"That modification is not recorded."


The captain answered immediately.


"Of course it isn't recorded."


Broussard closed his eyes.


There it was.


Mariners.


Holt looked deeply embarrassed.


Gabriel's mouth twitched.


Broussard whispered:


"Feeling better."


"Why?" Gabriel asked.


"That sounds normal."


They entered engineering.


The main compartment was chaos.


Not uncontrolled chaos.


Professional chaos.


Which was entirely different.


Emergency cables crossed the deck.


Portable illumination rigs had been erected around open equipment cabinets.


Crewmen moved between stations carrying tools and replacement components.


Two enginseers were connected directly to diagnostic ports while servo-skulls drifted overhead recording thermal images.


The deck itself tilted noticeably toward starboard.


Near the forward bulkhead, part of the compartment had been sealed behind an emergency pressure partition.


Beyond the transparent armored panel, Broussard could see darkness.


And through that darkness, ice.


The hull breach.


The ocean's frozen surface sat frighteningly close beyond twisted structure and torn plating.


Captain Derrovan Kelm stood in front of a massive open electrical distribution cabinet arguing with the Magos.


Broussard recognized him immediately from the personnel file.


He looked older in person.


Gray hair.


Broad shoulders.


Heavy commercial naval coat with captain's insignia.


His left sleeve had been burned near the cuff.


There was dried blood along one side of his forehead.


He had apparently been injured and simply decided that this was administratively inconvenient.


One hand held a dataslate.


The other was pointing angrily toward the exposed bus assembly.


"If you energize Grid Three through that transfer bank, it will take the load for perhaps thirty seconds."


The Magos replied:


"Forty-eight."


"Then it will melt."


"Probability: sixty-three percent."


"And when it melts, it takes the auxiliary distribution spine with it."


"Probability: forty-one percent."


"And then we have no ship."


"Incorrect. You retain a ship."


Kelm stared.


"You retain a powerless hull."


The captain looked as though he might commit a minor religious offense.


The Magos continued.


"Structural identity remains vessel-consistent."


"Wonderful. I'll tell the crew we've been successfully converted into a building."


Gabriel coughed.


Not loudly.


Just enough.


Kelm turned.


For the first time since boarding, Broussard saw the captain's expression clearly.


Exhaustion.


Irritation.


Pain.


And yes—


Fear.


But not the fear of a man caught concealing himself in a cabin.


The fear of someone trying desperately to prevent the destruction of something he loved.


Kelm looked at Holt.


Then at Broussard.


Then at Gabriel.


His face changed instantly when he recognized the commissariat uniform.


"Captain Broussard."


Broussard nodded.


"Captain Kelm."


Kelm glanced around himself.


"I apologize for not meeting you personally."


Broussard looked toward the open distribution cabinet.


"I've gathered you were occupied."


Kelm gave a humorless laugh.


"That is one word."


The Magos rotated slightly toward Broussard.


"Military command presence acknowledged."


Broussard looked at him.


"Progress?"


"The vessel remains recoverable."


Kelm immediately interrupted.


"Depending upon the definition of recoverable."


The Magos ignored him.


"Primary propulsion restoration remains improbable without replacement components unavailable aboard the recovery unit."


"Which means?" Broussard asked.


"Partial restoration of suspension and maneuvering capability is feasible."


Kelm stepped forward.


"Provided he does not destroy the main power bus trying to force current through a system that cannot take it."


The Magos turned.


"Captain Kelm continues to rely upon experiential intuition rather than validated technical models."


Kelm looked at Broussard.


"I've commanded this ship for nineteen years."


"Twenty-one point four local standard years," the Magos corrected.


"Thank you."


"You are welcome."


Broussard rubbed the bridge of his nose.


This might take some time.


He walked closer to the exposed electrical bank.


Even without technical expertise, he could see the damage.


Some conductors were scorched black.


Others had been physically torn loose.


Emergency bypass lines had already been installed in several places.


A thick cable bundle disappeared into an access trench cut open in the deck.


"What happens if we do it the captain's way?"


The Magos answered.


"Restoration requires a slower power distribution sequence, replacement of six regulator assemblies, manual isolation of damaged grid infrastructure and fabrication of two unavailable coupling housings."


"Time?"


"Between thirty-six and fifty-four hours."


Kelm folded his arms.


"And if we do it your way?"


"Potential suspension restoration within eleven hours."


"With a forty-one percent chance of destroying the auxiliary spine."


"Approximately."


Kelm looked at Broussard.


"There."


The Magos continued.


"The risk is operationally acceptable given convoy separation."


Kelm snapped:


"Not to me."


For a moment nobody spoke.


That sentence carried more weight than its volume.


Broussard looked at the captain.


Kelm realized perhaps that he had said too much.


Or said it too honestly.


He exhaled.


"This isn't a Guard vehicle, Captain."


"I know."


"I don't have another one waiting in a depot."


"I know."


"There are eighty-seven crew aboard."


"I know."


"Some of them have served here fifteen years."


Broussard nodded.


Kelm looked toward the exposed systems.


"She has crossed this ocean more times than I can count."


His voice dropped slightly.


"I am not going to turn her into scrap because someone wants the convoy schedule preserved."


The Magos emitted a short burst of machine code.


Broussard suspected it was rude.


Gabriel remained silent.


Watching.


Always watching.


Broussard found himself understanding the tension aboard a little better.


This was not merely a commercial vessel.


To the crew, it was home.


And now strangers had arrived with the authority to decide whether home was worth repairing.


That explained some of the fear.


Not all of it.


But some.


Broussard turned toward the captain.


"How did you hit the ice?"


Kelm's expression tightened.


There.


Again.


That hesitation.


Broussard felt the unease return immediately.


"We lost steering response."


"Cause?"


"Still investigating."


"Before or after impact?"


"Before."


"How long before?"


Kelm looked toward one of the damaged control displays.


"Seconds."


"How many?"


"Perhaps six."


Broussard frowned.


Six seconds.


At convoy speed, six seconds mattered.


"Did you see the formation?"


"Yes."


"And couldn't avoid it?"


"The helm did not respond."


"Mechanical failure?"


Kelm glanced toward the Magos.


"We don't know."


The Magos answered:


"Primary steering control records are incomplete."


Broussard looked at him.


"Incomplete how?"


"Data loss occurred during electrical discharge."


Reasonable.


Perfectly reasonable.


Then the Magos added:


"However, approximately fourteen seconds of telemetry immediately preceding impact are absent."


Broussard went still.


Kelm's jaw tightened.


Gabriel's easy expression disappeared.


Fourteen seconds.


Not destroyed.


Not corrupted.


Absent.


Broussard looked around the engineering compartment.


Several nearby crewmen had stopped pretending not to listen.


There it was again.


The fear.


The shame.


The thing he could not identify.


"Captain," Broussard said quietly.


Kelm looked at him.


"You said the helm stopped responding six seconds before impact."


"Yes."


"And fourteen seconds of telemetry are missing."


"Apparently."


"That is inconvenient."


Kelm gave him a tired look.


"You think?"


Broussard stepped closer.


"I think eight seconds are currently unaccounted for."


No one spoke.


The Magos's optical implants clicked softly as they refocused.


Broussard's voice remained friendly.


That made it harder.


"What happened during those eight seconds?"


Kelm stared at him.


For just a moment Broussard thought the captain might refuse.


Then the older man looked away.


"I don't know."


Broussard watched him.


That might even have been true.


Which was somehow worse.


Behind them, the damaged ship groaned.


A deep metallic sound traveled through the hull as the surviving suspension grids compensated for another shift in load.


Everyone in engineering instinctively stopped.


The deck tilted perhaps half a degree.


Then stabilized.


The Magos turned back toward the electrical systems.


"Structural discussion may continue after prevention of structural collapse."


Kelm looked at Broussard.


"I need this ship alive first."


Broussard nodded.


"So do I."


He glanced toward Gabriel.


The commissar met his eyes.


No words were necessary.


Something had happened before the Hesperant Vale struck the ice.


Something the logs did not contain.


Something the crew either did not understand or did not want discussed.


But there would be time for that.


First they had to keep several thousand tonnes of damaged freighter from becoming part of the ocean.


Broussard pointed toward the Magos.


"We take the slower repair."


The Mechanicus priest rotated toward him.


"Clarify."


"No forty-one percent gambles with the auxiliary spine."


Kelm visibly relaxed.


"Captain—"


Broussard held up a hand.


"Don't thank me yet. Fifty-four hours means we fall a very long way behind."


"I understand."


"It also means my men spend two more nights sitting on open ice."


"I understand."


"And if this ship becomes indefensible, unstable, or impossible to recover, I will evacuate your crew and abandon it."


Kelm's face hardened.


Broussard stepped closer.


"That part is not negotiable."


The captain held his gaze.


Then slowly nodded.


"Understood."


"Good."


Broussard turned toward the Magos.


"Make it work."


The Magos gave something that might have been the Mechanicus equivalent of irritation.


"That was already the objective."


"Excellent. We're aligned."


Gabriel followed Broussard toward the compartment exit.


They were halfway to the hatch when the commissar spoke quietly.


"Fourteen seconds."


"Yes."


"You noticed the captain knew where you were going before you asked."


Broussard glanced at him.


"I noticed."


"So did I."


They continued walking.


Behind them, Captain Kelm and the Magos resumed arguing over electrical tolerances.


Ahead waited the crew.


The patrol perimeter.


The cold.


And somewhere beneath all of it, the ocean.


Broussard had come aboard expecting to find embarrassed mariners hiding maintenance violations.


He had found those.


That was comforting.


What was less comforting was the growing possibility that the hole torn through the Hesperant Vale's hull was not the first thing that had gone wrong.


It was merely the first thing large enough for everyone else to see.
