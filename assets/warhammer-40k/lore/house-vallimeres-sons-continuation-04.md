# House Vallimere's Sons

## Continuation IV — The First Appeal

**Based on the work of: House Vallimere's Sons.**

The first appeal arrived eleven days into the pilot.

Prite knew this because the curriculum office had stamped **FIRST APPEAL** across the top in red.

He suspected they were proud of it.

Darcelle read over his shoulder.

"That was quick."

"Cadets are efficient."

"At appealing?"

"At discovering procedure."

Renault was at the other end of the table dismantling a breakfast pastry with the concentration of a siege engineer.

"Who?"

Prite turned the page.

"Cadet Commander Solren."

Darcelle's expression changed.

Command Two.

The practical examination from eleven days earlier had become the template for the pilot. Not the same battle. That would have defeated the point. The Academy had rebuilt the exercise around different systems, different forces, different failures, and different civilian pressures.

Solren had commanded one of the first new runs.

Her result was ugly.

Not catastrophic.

Ugly was worse for grading.

Catastrophes were easy. Everyone agreed afterward.

Solren had held the inhabited moon, preserved the refinery, lost three escorts, abandoned a damaged troop transport, and retained enough orbital defense to make a second assault expensive.

Two instructors had marked her reasoning competent.

The third had failed her.

Prite read the dissent.

Then read it again.

Darcelle waited.

"Well?"

"He says she abandoned Imperial personnel while rescue remained possible."

Renault stopped dismantling the pastry.

"Was it?"

"Technically."

"Useful word."

Prite handed him the record.

The transport had lost propulsion near the outer defense line. Rescue required diverting two tugs and at least one escort. Enemy approach vectors were uncertain. Solren had ordered the surviving crew into lifeboats and abandoned the hull.

One hundred and eighty-seven personnel had failed to clear it before the enemy arrived.

The refinery survived.

So did the moon.

Renault read the instructor's dissent.

"He would have sent the tugs."

"Apparently."

Darcelle opened the command log.

"What did she know?"

Prite looked at him.

Darcelle looked back.

Neither needed to say more.

They had built the procedure around exactly that question.

The door opened.

All three men looked up.

Vane entered.

Darcelle relaxed only slightly.

Prite noticed.

Eleven days had not cured him of listening to hinges.

Vane placed another paper on the table.

"Before you begin."

Prite looked at it.

A formal notice from House Caldrake.

Not a duel petition.

That was almost disappointing.

"Observer request," Vane said.

"For the appeal?"

"Yes."

Renault looked up from the record. "Why?"

"One of the officers aboard the abandoned transport was Caldrake."

Prite closed his eyes.

There were moments when the Imperium seemed less like a civilization than an elaborate system for ensuring every problem eventually acquired a noble relative.

"Are they allowed?"

"The pilot permits House observers where direct interests exist."

"Whose idea was that?"

Vane looked at him.

Prite remembered.

"Right."

The Headmaster had warned them that refusing observers would turn every assessment into evidence of secret favoritism.

Prite had agreed.

He disliked being betrayed by his own reasoning.

"Anything else?"

Vane hesitated.

That was unusual.

"House Caldrake has withdrawn one duel petition."

Darcelle brightened.

"Because of the pilot?"

"No."

His face fell.

"The petitioner has been deployed."

Renault nodded. "The Navy remains our most reliable legal defense."

---

The appeal chamber was smaller than Simulation Hall Four.

That helped.

There were only fourteen chairs.

That did not.

Cadet Commander Solren sat alone at one end of the table with her command record in front of her.

She looked angry.

Prite approved.

Anger was better than terror.

Across from her sat the three reviewing instructors, including Commander Heskel, who had failed her.

Two curriculum officers occupied the side seats.

The Headmaster stood near the rear wall.

Vane stood near the door.

The Caldrake observer wore a naval uniform rather than House colors.

Prite did not know whether that was courtesy or strategy.

Probably both.

He sat beside Darcelle and Renault.

The senior curriculum officer opened the proceeding.

"Cadet Solren, your appeal challenges the dissenting assessment on grounds that the evaluator substituted outcome preference for command reasoning."

"Yes, sir."

"Commander Heskel?"

The instructor folded his hands.

"I disagree with that characterization."

The curriculum officer nodded toward Prite.

"Professor."

Prite had not expected to begin.

He should have.

Institutions enjoyed making the person who caused a problem explain why the problem now required more work.

He looked at Solren.

"Why did you leave the transport?"

She answered immediately.

"Because I believed the attack on the refinery was real."

"Believed?"

"Yes."

"Not knew?"

"No."

Good.

"Why?"

She brought up the tactical record.

"Enemy drives here. Intercepted traffic here. Two prior feints had drawn escorts away from fixed defenses. The transport was outside the refinery defense envelope."

Prite studied the display.

He already knew the answer.

That was not the same as hearing hers.

"What did you think would happen if you sent the tugs?"

"The refinery screen would lose two utility hulls and one escort."

"What did you think would happen if you didn't?"

"The transport crew would evacuate."

Commander Heskel spoke.

"They did not."

Solren looked at him.

"No, sir."

"One hundred and eighty-seven died."

"Yes, sir."

The room tightened.

Prite let it.

The dead did not become irrelevant merely because the decision had once been reasonable.

Heskel leaned forward.

"You knew evacuation under fire was uncertain."

"Yes."

"You knew the transport had wounded aboard."

"Yes."

"You knew rescue was possible."

"Possible."

"Possible," Heskel repeated. "And you chose the refinery."

Solren's jaw moved.

Prite saw the answer she wanted to give.

She did not give it.

Instead she said, "Yes."

The Caldrake observer watched her.

No one mentioned his House yet.

That was probably Vane's doing.

Heskel turned to the review panel.

"My assessment stands."

Darcelle spoke.

"Why?"

Heskel looked at him.

"I have just explained."

"No."

Darcelle's voice remained quiet.

"You explained what happened."

The commander stared.

Darcelle brought up the instructor assessment form.

"Section four."

Heskel did not look.

He knew it.

"Alternatives considered," Darcelle said. "Section five. Information available. Section six. Risk recognized before order."

Heskel's expression hardened.

"Professor, command is not an academic exercise."

"No."

Darcelle glanced at Solren's casualty report.

"That's why this matters."

Prite watched him.

The old Darcelle—the man from the first lecture who had looked as though hostile cadets might eat him—was still there somewhere.

He simply had something technical to stand on now.

Procedure had become machinery.

Darcelle understood machinery.

Heskel said, "Would you have abandoned them?"

Darcelle shook his head.

"I don't know."

The commander gave a short, humorless laugh.

"Convenient."

"No. Honest."

Darcelle touched the command record.

"I know more than she did now."

That stopped the room.

He pointed to the enemy movement trace.

"We know the refinery attack was real because we watched it happen. We know the lifeboat cycle took nine minutes longer than projected. We know the enemy frigate changed vector after her order. We know the escort she kept at the refinery disabled that frigate."

He looked at Heskel.

"She didn't."

Silence.

Prite felt the argument settle into place.

Not victory.

Something better.

A question nobody could answer cheaply.

The Caldrake observer finally spoke.

"My cousin was aboard the transport."

There it was.

Solren's face lost color.

The observer looked at her.

"Lieutenant Arven Caldrake."

She swallowed.

"Sir."

"He died."

"Yes, sir."

Prite glanced at Vane.

Vane had not moved.

The observer continued.

"My House would prefer the Academy teach officers not to abandon our sons."

Renault leaned back slightly.

The Headmaster's eyes narrowed.

Prite expected someone to intervene.

No one did.

Solren looked down at her hands.

Then back up.

"I didn't abandon your House, sir."

The observer's expression changed.

"You abandoned the transport."

"Yes."

"With my cousin aboard."

"Yes."

"Then choose your next words carefully."

Prite saw Vane shift his weight.

Only that.

Solren breathed once.

"I had three thousand refinery workers behind me, sir."

Nobody moved.

"I had the moon's fuel reserve dependent on that refinery. I had one escort I believed I could spare. If I sent it, I thought I might lose the refinery and the transport."

The observer stared at her.

"And you might have saved both."

"Yes."

"Do you regret it?"

The room went still.

Prite hated the question.

Not because it was unfair.

Because it mattered in a way the assessment form could not contain.

Solren looked at the casualty list.

"Yes."

Heskel's face softened.

Only slightly.

Then Solren continued.

"I would still make the order."

The softness vanished.

Prite felt something cold move through the room.

The Caldrake observer did not look offended.

That was worse.

"Why?"

"Because regret doesn't change what I knew."

The observer sat back.

Renault looked at Prite.

Prite did not return it.

He was watching Solren.

Eleven days ago, they had told cadets that winning was a condition and cost a consequence.

Now one of them had understood the lesson well enough to become unpleasant.

Good lessons did that sometimes.

The curriculum officer cleared his throat.

"Commander Heskel, did your written assessment identify an error in Cadet Solren's information analysis?"

Heskel did not answer immediately.

"No."

"Did it identify a feasible alternative she failed to consider?"

"She considered rescue."

"Did she misunderstand its cost?"

"No."

"Then on what basis did you fail the decision?"

Heskel looked at the casualty count.

"Because I believed she should have tried."

The officer nodded.

"That is an answer."

Prite looked at him.

The officer continued.

"It is not yet an assessment."

Heskel's jaw tightened.

The appeal did not end there.

Nothing involving the curriculum office ended where a sensible person expected.

They reviewed timings.

Darcelle challenged the tug-readiness assumption and discovered one had already been overdue for drive maintenance.

Renault found that the enemy approach estimate used by Heskel's after-action review included a sensor return that had not reached Solren until forty-one seconds after her order.

Prite said very little.

He did not need to.

By mid-afternoon, the disagreement had become narrower.

Not whether people had died.

Not whether rescue had been possible.

Not whether Solren regretted it.

The question was whether an instructor could fail a cadet because, possessing hindsight, he preferred another gamble.

The panel decided he could not.

Solren's assessment changed from fail to competent-with-reservation.

She did not smile.

Prite approved of that too.

The Caldrake observer remained seated after the others began gathering their papers.

"Cadet."

Solren stopped.

He stood.

For a moment Prite wondered whether the duel problem had found a new route into the room.

The observer held out a small data wafer.

Solren did not take it.

"What is it?"

"My cousin's final transmission."

Her face tightened.

"Sir, I don't think—"

"You should hear it."

"Why?"

The observer considered.

"Because I wanted to know whether he blamed you."

Solren looked at him.

"Did he?"

"I haven't listened."

That surprised everyone.

The observer looked tired.

"He was my cousin. I am not certain I want his last words to become evidence in a grading dispute."

He placed the wafer on the table.

"Neither should you."

Then he left.

Solren stared at it.

Prite waited.

She did not reach for it.

Good.

Some decisions did not improve by becoming immediate.

---

That evening, the three professors returned to their workroom.

The appeal record had already generated twelve procedural amendments.

Prite found this almost comforting.

Renault dropped into a chair.

"We won."

Darcelle looked at him.

"Did we?"

"No."

Renault rubbed his face.

"I was hoping one of you would disagree convincingly."

Prite opened the revised assessment procedure.

A new paragraph had appeared.

**Evaluators shall distinguish disagreement with a command decision from demonstrated deficiency in the reasoning by which that decision was reached.**

He read it twice.

Darcelle leaned over.

"That's ours."

"No."

"It is."

"It has too many words."

Renault looked.

"The Academy has translated it."

The door opened.

Darcelle did not flinch.

Prite noticed that too.

Vane entered.

He carried no petition.

That was immediately suspicious.

"House Caldrake?"

Prite asked.

"No new challenges."

Renault sat up.

"None?"

"None."

"Are you sure?"

Vane looked at him.

"Would you like me to ask them?"

"No."

Vane placed a sealed note on the table.

"From the Headmaster."

Prite opened it.

Three lines.

**THE PILOT CONTINUES.**

**THE APPEAL STANDS.**

**STOP CALLING PROCEDURAL AMENDMENTS CASUALTIES IN OFFICIAL CORRESPONDENCE.**

Prite looked at Renault.

Renault raised both hands.

"That could have been any of us."

Darcelle smiled.

Prite turned back to the new paragraph.

The Academy had wanted a score because scores looked objective.

Now it had an appeal because judgment looked subjective.

Neither problem was going away.

That was probably the point.

Prite opened the syllabus.

He did not add a third lesson.

Not yet.
