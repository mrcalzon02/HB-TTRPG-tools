# The Long Patrol of Broussard

## IX. REPAIRS

**Author:** Mrcalzon02 / Christopher Vardeman  
**Based on the work of:** *The Long Patrol of Broussard*

Broussard lingered near the hatch a moment longer, listening to the rhythm of the ship's wounded heart. Pumps thumped unevenly. Ventilation fans labored. Somewhere metal protested. Behind him, the argument resumed, softer now, but no less intense.


Gabriel finally spoke once they were out of earshot.


"You're thinking what I'm thinking."


Broussard did not slow.


"That the ice wasn't the first problem? Yes."


"Fourteen seconds missing from a ship's brain doesn't feel like accident to me."


"Could be damage."


"Could be," Gabriel agreed. "Could also be someone deciding certain data didn't need to survive."


Broussard exhaled slowly.


"Let's not hang anyone in our heads yet."


"Wasn't planning to. Just noting that frightened crews don't usually get quiet around officers unless there's consequence attached to words."


Broussard stopped at an intersection of corridors. Crew moved past them, giving Gabriel a little too much space.


"For now," he said, "we keep them alive. After that, we ask harder questions."


Gabriel nodded once.


"Agreed."


A shudder ran through the deck again, stronger this time. A light flickered, went out, then came back dimmer. Somewhere a crewman swore.


Broussard keyed his vox.


"Voss, status."


"Perimeter steady," came the reply through static. "Ice is shifting slightly. Nothing dramatic yet."


Yet.


That word settled uncomfortably.


Broussard looked down the corridor toward where engineering labored and argued and hoped.


Fifty hours, maybe more.


A lot could happen in fifty hours.


The first interruption was small enough to be almost reassuring.


When Broussard and Gabriel stepped back out onto the ice, the cold hit them like a reprimand. The wind had shifted slightly, carrying a low rolling groan through the frozen surface underfoot.


Voss's voice came over the vox.


"Surface movement northeast. Could be pressure. Could be wildlife."


Broussard lifted his magnoculars.


At first the blowing snow concealed everything. Then pale forms resolved along a pressure ridge: a small line of shaggy ice grazers moving slowly through the white. Low, broad-backed things, heavily furred and dusted with frost, they nosed at mineral algae exposed where wind had scoured the ice.


One lifted its head toward the clustered lights of the freighter and recovery vehicles.


For a moment it stared.


Then, displaying considerably better judgment than many humans Broussard had commanded, it turned away.


The others followed.


"No threat," Voss said.


"Let them pass."


The herd faded into the haze.


Gabriel watched them go.


"Even the wildlife knows to keep moving."


"They live here," Broussard said. "We don't."


The ice groaned again.


Then there was work.


The next several hours settled into a rhythm of machinery, cold, and human stubbornness.


Broussard moved through the Hesperant Vale rather than remaining in engineering. He had learned long ago that one could learn more about a unit by walking through it than by reading its reports.


He began to know the crew.


Sella Varn, a wiry cargo engineer with grease permanently worked into the lines of her hands, seemed to appear wherever something was about to fail.


A younger rating named Torren followed her everywhere and copied every movement with the intense seriousness of someone determined not to remain young for long.


Meren Holt, despite the nervousness of their first meeting, proved competent. Too competent, perhaps, for someone still carrying "Second" in his title. He kept appearing with corrected duty lists, updated power reports, or crew assignments before Broussard needed to ask.


Captain Kelm never stopped moving.


He checked on the wounded.


He argued with the Magos.


He examined load shifts.


He climbed through maintenance spaces that a man of his age had every right to delegate to younger backs.


Once Broussard found him standing alone beside a bulkhead with one hand pressed against the metal, eyes closed.


For a moment Broussard thought the captain was praying.


Then he understood.


Kelm was listening.


Not metaphorically.


Listening to the vibration of the vessel through his palm.


To the way the old freighter spoke through its bones.


Kelm opened his eyes and noticed Broussard.


"Grid Four is hunting."


Broussard had no idea what that meant.


"Bad?"


"Annoying."


"Good."


Kelm gave him a tired smile.


"You have low standards."


"They keep being achievable."


Elsewhere, crewmen offered fragments of information without realizing they were doing so.


One told Broussard that the forward suspension grid had been temperamental for months but had always remained inside tolerance.


Another complained that the emergency ration lockers had been loaded incorrectly at their last port.


A deckhand mentioned that everyone had been working double shifts since the accident.


A cargo rigger confessed that he had not slept more than two hours at a stretch.


Gabriel circulated as well.


His presence initially stiffened every conversation.


Then the effect changed.


He helped move a damaged locker without being asked.


He held a work lamp for an enginseer.


He listened to a crewman's story about his children without converting it into a sermon on duty.


Slowly, very slowly, the black coat became part of the scenery.


Not harmless.


Never harmless.


But understood.


The repairs wound on.


The Hesperant Vale groaned and rolled on its remaining suspension grids.


The smell changed from compartment to compartment.


Ozone near open electrical panels.


Stale coolant in the lower passages.


Burned insulation near the damaged distribution runs.


Hot oil around the auxiliary machinery.


Human sweat trapped beneath layers of cold-weather clothing.


Metal.


Grease.


Recaf.


Fear.


At intervals the entire hull would give a deep structural complaint as the load shifted across surviving grids. Every time it happened, conversation faltered for just a fraction of a second.


Then resumed.


That was how Broussard came to understand the crew's integrity.


Not through speeches.


Through small things.


A technician rechecking a seal that had already passed.


A cargo hand reporting his own mistake before anyone discovered it.


A rating returning a tool to the correct locker even though the compartment was half destroyed.


Sella tearing out a bypass because she did not trust the connector, then rebuilding the entire run herself rather than leave a questionable repair for the next watch.


People could hide sins and still be decent.


Broussard had learned that years ago.


The Imperium preferred simpler categories.


Loyal.


Disloyal.


Pure.


Corrupt.


Guilty.


Innocent.


Reality generally arrived with more paperwork.


These people were frightened of something.


He remained certain of that.


But they were also working themselves half dead to save their ship and one another.


For the moment, that counted for something.


Hours into the repair, one of the rerouted power trunks accepted load without an alarm.


No one cheered.


The whole compartment merely exhaled.


Sella wiped her hands on a rag that had ceased being clean sometime during the previous century.


"Still flying."


Captain Kelm heard her.


One corner of his mouth moved.


"Still flying," he agreed.


The ship groaned again.


Gabriel leaned toward Broussard.


"They haven't given up."


"No."


Broussard watched the crew return to work.


"That's the dangerous part. People like this don't quit easy."
