# Matron Lagas Atrovald

## Continuation VIII — The Witnesses of the Cage

The route steward arrived with two receipts and a complaint.

His complaint was that the receipts had been requested by three offices, each of which believed the other two had already received them. He had consequently spent the morning walking between counters with the same two pieces of paper.

Arthus Pell offered him a chair. The steward declined until Atrovald assured him that sitting would not constitute an admission of negligence.

"I collected the commander's chest from the lower cage," he said. "I took it to the southern circuit's baggage wagon. The wagon officer signed for it."

"Did you collect it from the man who received it from Reth Carrow?"

"No, Matron. From the cage attendant. The receiving desk is upstairs."

"Was the chest opened?"

"Not by me."

"Did you examine the seal?"

"I read the number against the release slip."

"Did you touch the seal?"

He looked at the receipt, as though the paper might improve his memory.

"No. The cage attendant held the chest while I read it."

Atrovald asked him to set down the sequence without reference to the other statements. The steward had arrived after the evening overlap, waited for a freight lift, received a chest under the commander's seal, and placed it in the baggage wagon. He remembered the wagon officer objecting that the chest had arrived too late for the first load. The officer nevertheless accepted it under the commander's traveling authority.

"Where did the wagon take it?"

"With the southern circuit."

"Did you accompany it?"

"Only to the first relay."

"Then your knowledge ends there."

"Yes, Matron."

Pell looked at the receipts. One was a cage release; the other, a wagon acceptance. The names were different. Neither was the receiving person's name concealed beneath the corrected junction entry.

"Did anyone tell you the attendance sheet was inside?" he asked.

"The adjutant's note called it a commander's chest. It didn't inventory the contents."

"Then we have not proved the sheet traveled with it," Atrovald said.

The steward looked relieved to be dismissed from responsibility for a piece of paper he had never seen.

"Am I finished?"

"With your account. The receipts will remain available for comparison."

"And the other offices?"

"Send them copies of the request numbers. They can ask us for the certified result."

He finally sat down, not from obedience but because his legs had become tired.

---

The first cage attendant had been responsible for accepting the chest into storage. Atrovald interviewed him without the steward or the second attendant present.

He brought a duty book with a broad thumbprint on the cover. It recorded the same seal number and the twenty-three-minute interval after Carrow's handoff. He remembered the lower cage was short of space because a broken freight trolley had been left inside the threshold. The chest had to be placed on the inner bench.

"Who brought it down?"

"The route desk sent it on the lift."

"Who operated the lift?"

"One of the junction hands."

"Which one?"

"I didn't see. The cage door was shut while the lift came down. I received it through the service hatch."

"From whom?"

"Someone in the route sleeve band."

"Did you see his face?"

"No."

The attendant was not embarrassed until Pell asked whether he had seen the seal.

"Of course."

"On the chest?"

"On the slip."

Atrovald waited.

"The chest was wrapped," the attendant explained. "The seal impression was inside the cord guard. I checked that the guard was tied and the number on the accompanying slip matched the receipt. I did not remove the guard."

"Was that the instruction?"

"Yes. The commander had ordered the seal left undisturbed. If I broke the guard to inspect it, I would have made the chest less secure."

Atrovald did not rebuke him. The instruction protected a real interest. A chest carrying military papers could not be opened by every transport clerk who wished to reassure himself that he was competent.

She asked him to demonstrate the check with an empty dispatch case. He showed them the tie, the guard, the receipt slot and the mark a clerk was expected to make. He had performed the prescribed inspection. It was not the inspection Pell had imagined when reading the register.

"So the entry 'seal checked' means the outer protection and paperwork were consistent," she said.

"Yes, Matron."

"Not that you saw the commander's impression."

"No, Matron."

Pell struck a phrase from his working abstract. He had written *seal visually verified*. The attendant watched the correction with interest.

"Does that make the receipt false?"

"No," Atrovald said. "It makes our description of your work more accurate."

The attendant left with his duty book intact.

---

The second attendant had released the chest to the steward.

He remembered the inner bench, the broken trolley and the steward's irritation at waiting for the lift. He did not remember the person who had brought the chest into the cage, because he had not been on that watch. His release entry used the same number as the admission entry.

"Did you inspect the cord guard?" Atrovald asked.

"Yes."

"Did you see the impression beneath it?"

"No. I checked the guard and the slip."

"Was the chest in your sight throughout its storage?"

"During my watch. Before that, it was under the previous attendant."

"Was there an unattended interval?"

"The cage was locked during the watch change."

"Who had the keys?"

"The duty pair. They signed the key book."

He offered the book. Atrovald requested a certified copy rather than taking it from the cage. Its two key signatures covered the change of watch, but the record did not claim that two people had watched the chest continuously. A locked door was a control, not an eyewitness.

The second attendant hesitated before signing his statement.

"Will the first man be punished?"

"For what?"

"For not looking under the guard."

"Did you look under it?"

"No."

"Then perhaps we should first decide whether either of you was required to."

The attendant signed. He had a family meal waiting at the end of his shift and seemed chiefly concerned that the inquiry might consume it.

When he left, Pell laid the two accounts beside Carrow's.

"The receiver was older, with a wrapped hand. The first attendant saw a sleeve band. The second never saw the receiver. The steward saw the chest after storage."

"Yes."

"And the seal number agrees."

"The recorded number agrees."

Pell looked at her.

"That is a smaller comfort."

"It is the comfort we possess."

They could reconstruct an authorized chain of offices. They could not reconstruct an unbroken chain of observed hands. Nor could they say the chest had been altered. The distinction was becoming less agreeable as the documents improved.

---

The commander's adjutant sent a reply during the next watch.

The commander was still on the southern circuit. His traveling chest remained under his authority, and his office would not surrender it on the strength of an archival request. The adjutant could provide a certified extract from the named attendance sheet after receiving the commander's approval. The reply gave no date for that approval.

Atrovald did not send wardens.

She drafted two lawful alternatives. The commander could authorize a supervised examination of the original sheet by his officer and an Interlocutum reviewer, with unrelated personnel entries screened from the latter. Or he could furnish a certified extract naming the person assigned to the fifty-third position, accompanied by a signed statement of whether the position was filled at dispatch and at return. If he could certify neither, he could say so under his own seal.

Pell read the alternatives.

"You've given him a way to refuse us."

"He already has a way to refuse us."

"Then why make it easier?"

"Because I would rather receive an honest refusal than a convenient certificate. And because his command has a legitimate interest in its roster."

She also asked the route office to preserve the key book, the release and admission slips, and the original correction packet. The lower cage remained in service. Closing it would strand every dispatch that passed through the southern junction, none of which had volunteered to become part of the Venn inquiry.

The adjutant's office acknowledged receipt. It did not provide the attendance sheet.

Pell looked at the acknowledgment as if he might wring a name from the ink.

"We have followed the chest as far as the commander."

"We have followed its recorded custody as far as the commander's traveling authority. We have not seen what is inside."

"And the man at the desk?"

"Still unnamed."

Pell closed the file without trying to turn those two absences into one answer.

---

The return-strength inquiry had advanced less visibly.

Seret Dain supplied the original batch schedule and a separate receipt from the escort office's return desk. The receipt recorded fifty-two as the number submitted to reconciliation. It carried a valid departmental stamp and the receiving clerk's acknowledgment. It did not carry the personal signature of the officer who had counted the returning escort.

Dain watched Atrovald read it.

"This is what I had when I signed the batch."

"I believe you."

"Does it establish who entered the number?"

"It establishes which desk sent it."

"That is what I said last watch."

"Then we need not make you say it again."

He appeared almost grateful.

The escort office's return clerk had been assigned to a different shift by the time Pell found him. He recognized the form and the office stamp. He could explain the procedure: a returning escort submitted a strength figure, the desk checked its authority code, and the clerk entered the figure into the reconciliation queue. If an exceptional loss had been declared, the form was diverted for individual examination.

"Was there an exceptional declaration that night?" Pell asked.

"Not in the packet I processed."

"Could one have been sent elsewhere?"

"An officer could submit it to Personnel or command. I wouldn't see it until someone linked it to the return."

Atrovald requested the declaration registers for the relevant watches, not a universal search of every casualty file in the spire. The surviving registers showed no linked exceptional-loss declaration for the escort in that interval. One register had a late-entry column, and its retention schedule required a separate check. She ordered that check rather than writing *none existed*.

"Who told you fifty-two?" she asked.

"The return desk received the figure with the command code. I copied it."

"From a man?"

"From a form."

"Who brought the form?"

"I don't remember. There were several returns."

"Then we shall not improve your memory for you."

The clerk's account was useful and unsatisfying. The number had passed through identifiable offices, each of which could truthfully describe its own part. The person who originated it remained beyond the visible paper.

Dain asked whether the new review rule would now force a named officer to certify the number.

"For future disputed custody cases," Atrovald said. "It cannot manufacture yesterday's signature."

"And the old form?"

"Will remain evidence of what your office received."

Dain signed the corrected abstract. It named his batch responsibility and preserved the unanswered question of origin. He no longer asked for his signature to be removed.

---

The new human-review queue arrived on Atrovald's desk in three bundles.

The first contained cases in which an original physical receipt had already been located. Three could be returned for ordinary closure once the reviewing officers signed their findings. The second contained cases whose records were incomplete but whose claimants could still be contacted. The third contained two disputed-custody cases, including one in which the sending and receiving offices disagreed about which name belonged to the same prisoner.

Nineteen cases had entered the queue. None was being discarded merely because its arithmetic now balanced.

The custody chief brought the staffing report. Four historical-index clerks per watch and two supervisors were still assigned to the temporary review. Their first day's output was lower than the estimate. The supervisors had spent much of it learning which records they were permitted to see.

"The Logos concordance office has sent a complaint," he said.

"About the staffing figures?"

"About the comparisons we have delayed."

The complaint was legitimate. A set of older derivative witnesses awaiting historical collation would now remain unexamined beyond its ordinary turn. The Logos office had not asked to see the missing-person files; it had asked when its own work would resume.

Atrovald dictated a reply giving the diversion's duration, the number of staff withdrawn, and the next review date. It disclosed no prisoner, witness or custody identity. She offered a limited priority hearing for any historical comparison whose delay created an independently documented preservation risk.

Pell raised an eyebrow.

"Will that not bring every comparison to our door?"

"Probably."

"And will you approve them?"

"Not without evidence of risk."

The chief turned the staffing table toward her.

"We could return one clerk to indexing if we let the machine close the sixteen ordinary-looking cases."

"No. We can return staff when the reviewing officers have established which cases are ordinary."

"Then the historical queue grows."

"Record its growth."

He did. There was no comforting number that made both obligations disappear.

Before the chief left, Atrovald asked whether the quartermaster's allowance had cleared.

"Approved for the next accounting cycle. No disbursement entry yet."

"Do not mark it paid."

"Of course not, Matron."

She gave him a look.

He amended the status in front of her.

---

Pell found her late beside the two clocks.

The old mechanical face had gained another minute on the newer instrument. The tomorrow-dated instruction remained sealed, its two original readings undisturbed. No clerk had been authorized to execute it. Mortuary-Seventeen's ceramic container remained in place under Sorn's physical custody objection. Predulum and Witness Four remained in Null-Seven; the confinement log showed no change of order.

Pell carried the separate statements in their separate folders.

"The first attendant followed his instructions. So did the second. The steward carried the chest. Dain signed the batch. The return clerk copied the number."

"And none of those acts establishes who occupied the fifty-third position."

"No."

He hesitated.

"I thought finding more witnesses would make the story simpler."

"Sometimes it makes the story smaller. That is not the same thing."

"The commander may refuse the roster."

"Then we shall have a refusal with an accountable signature."

"And the exceptional declaration?"

"We have found none in the registers examined. The late-entry check remains."

Pell set the files down. His recollection of a cold escort with a scar had not changed. He had learned how little of that recollection could be given to someone else as proof.

Atrovald signed the preservation orders for the cage records and the return-office packet. She placed the request to the commander beside them, not on top of them. A man could be absent from a list without having vanished from the world. A list could be corrected without a man ever having been present. A valid seal could pass between offices without anyone seeing the hand that carried it.

The Interlocutum had procedures for every one of those circumstances. It had been less successful at distinguishing them.

"Shall I mark the investigation advanced?" Pell asked.

"Yes."

"By how much?"

"Three witnesses heard separately. One steward heard. Two receipts preserved. One unanswered request carried forward."

"That will make an ugly summary."

"Then at least it will be recognizable as our work."

Beyond the office the historical-index clerks were leaving their usual shelves to read custody disputes that could not be settled by arithmetic. The shelves would wait. The people in the disputed records might not have that luxury.

Atrovald closed the day's book with the named attendance sheet still unseen, the source of fifty-two still unidentified, and Hesk Venn still neither found nor disproved.

For once the archive had not mistaken a completed form for a completed inquiry.
