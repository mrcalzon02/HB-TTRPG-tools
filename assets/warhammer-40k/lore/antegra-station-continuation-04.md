# Antegra Station

## Continuation IV — The Inheritance Tax

*An original Cafarron Corridor chronicle continuation. Warhammer 40,000 and associated marks and setting elements are the property of Games Workshop. This non-commercial fan work is not endorsed by or affiliated with Games Workshop.*

Nobody spoke for eleven seconds.

On Antegra Station, eleven seconds was long enough for a pressure door to close, a coolant line to rupture, or a clerk to misfile a citizen into a penal levy.

Governor Talbor Varik spent them remembering a sky he had never seen.

It was violet at the horizon and black overhead. Seven pale moons hung above a city of low white towers. The nearest tower had no windows. Something enormous moved behind it, slow enough to be mistaken for geology.

Varik knew the city's name.

He refused to think it.

Senior Reconciliator Halbrecht Senn watched him across the interview table.

Captain Ardan Krell watched everyone else.

Commissar-Keeper Dren Solvik watched the door.

The door, having recently demonstrated that it possessed opinions, remained locked.

At last Krell said, “My lord?”

Varik looked at him.

Krell's face was careful.

That worried Varik more than panic would have.

“What did you remember?” Solvik asked.

“A place.”

“What place?”

“I don't know.”

Senn made a small sound.

Varik turned on him.

“You disagree.”

“I believe you know exactly where it is.”

“I have never been there.”

“That has never prevented the memory.”

The ceiling vox clicked.

Custodian Magos Hester Vhal's voice arrived with the faint metallic doubling produced by a damaged vocal augmetic or an excellent sense of theatre.

“Governor Varik. Do not articulate the recovered designation.”

Krell glanced upward.

“Useful warning.”

“Captain?”

“It would have been more useful before he remembered it.”

Vhal ignored him.

“Reconciliator Senn, initiate mnemonic containment protocol.”

Senn remained seated.

“No.”

There was a pause.

Varik had known several senior servants of the Adeptus Mechanicus. Most reacted to refusal as though reality had committed a procedural error.

Vhal recovered quickly.

“Clarify.”

“I am ninety-three percent prosthetic support, Magos. My liver is borrowed, my spine is a contractual dispute, and I have been waiting sixty-eight years for the governor to remember why he ordered this department built. I am exercising the final privilege of age.”

“What privilege?” Krell asked.

“Poor judgment.”

Varik almost approved.

Senn opened the red file again.

“Governor, the memories do not merely reproduce information. They recognize associations.”

“Meaning?”

“The infected do not all remember the same things. They remember different pieces of the same civilization.”

Krell frowned.

“Civilization.”

“That was the term used in the first Ordo assessment.”

“Human?”

Senn hesitated.

Solvik noticed.

“Answer.”

“Possibly.”

“That is not an answer.”

“It is the only one that survived review.”

Varik leaned back.

The chair creaked beneath him.

He remembered a woman standing beneath the seven moons.

He knew she was a mathematician.

He knew she hated pears.

He knew she had three daughters.

He knew she had died eleven thousand years before Talbor Varik was born.

He did not know her name.

That omission felt deliberate.

“Why does it spread?” he asked.

Senn folded his hands.

“We never established a mechanism.”

“Conversation.”

“Sometimes.”

“Family contact.”

“Sometimes.”

“Proximity?”

“Sometimes.”

Krell sighed.

“A remarkably disciplined contagion.”

“It is not a disease.”

“Diseases are usually less bureaucratic.”

Senn gave him a tired look.

“The best working theory was informational resonance.”

Solvik's expression hardened.

“Psyker contamination.”

“No measurable warp signature.”

“That does not comfort me.”

“It did not comfort the Ordo.”

Varik tapped the red file.

“Which Ordo?”

Senn looked toward the ceiling.

Vhal answered first.

“That designation remains sealed.”

“On whose authority?”

“Yours.”

Varik closed his eyes.

Of course it was.

There were few experiences more irritating than discovering that one's younger self had possessed excellent reasons and terrible documentation.

A tremor passed through the room.

Dust fell from the ceiling.

Krell was on his feet before the vibration ended.

“That wasn't the lift.”

“No,” Senn said.

The lights dimmed.

A red lumen began to pulse above the door.

The station vox sounded three descending tones.

Varik recognized them immediately.

Thermal emergency.

Then Master Auditor Selene Marr's voice came through every grille in Substructure Eighteen.

“Governor. Central Audit has lost heat feed from manifolds three through seven. Habitat Twelve reports ice formation in potable lines. Habitat Nine has begun emergency congregation.”

Krell swore.

Varik stood.

“Cause?”

“Someone redirected the southern trunk.”

“To where?”

Marr paused.

“Here.”

Senn's face changed.

Not fear.

Recognition.

Varik saw it.

“What is happening?”

Senn looked at the wall clock.

“Interview protocol.”

“You are freezing the station for an interview?”

“I am not.”

“Who is?”

The pneumatic tubes overhead began to clatter.

One capsule.

Then another.

Then twenty.

They struck baskets, desks, and the floor outside the chamber in a metallic rain.

Senn rose with difficulty.

“Governor, you should not have remembered this quickly.”

Varik crossed the room and struck the door control.

Locked.

Krell produced his laspistol.

“I have been waiting all afternoon for a door to become the enemy.”

“Do not shoot it.”

“My lord, with respect, your administration has had three continuations to improve its doors.”

Solvik looked at him.

“Three what?”

“Nothing.”

Krell holstered the pistol and drew a utility blade.

Varik decided not to inquire.

Above them, Marr continued.

“Governor, the Mechanicus party has reached Central Audit. Magos Vhal is attempting to override the thermal diversion.”

Vhal cut across her channel.

“Attempting is an imprecise characterization.”

“Is the heat restored?” Marr asked.

“No.”

“Then I stand by it.”

Varik smiled despite himself.

He had always liked auditors more when they became angry enough to forget hierarchy.

The red lumen accelerated.

Senn opened a cabinet beneath the interview table.

Inside were four respirators, four sidearms, and a brass key.

Krell stared at him.

“You had a key.”

“Yes.”

“To the locked door.”

“Yes.”

“And you waited until now.”

“It was filed under emergency equipment.”

Krell looked at Varik.

“My lord, when this is over I would like permission to reorganize the Administratum.”

“Denied. I still need you sane.”

Senn unlocked the door.

The great records hall beyond had changed.

Every desk lamp was burning.

Cogitator screens glowed green in the dimness.

Printers hammered.

Pneumatic capsules continued to fall.

The empty office was working.

Varik stepped into the aisle.

A sheet of paper slid from the nearest printer.

He tore it free.

POPULATION RECONCILIATION EVENT

SUBJECT: VARIK, TALBOR

STATUS: RECOGNIZED

MNEMONIC DEPTH: ESCALATING

STATION EXPOSURE: THIRTY-EIGHT POINT FOUR PERCENT

RECOMMENDED ACTION: SEVERANCE

Krell read over his shoulder.

“What does severance mean?”

Senn did not answer.

Varik turned.

“Senn.”

The old reconciliator looked exhausted.

“Originally? Evacuation of the unexposed population and destruction of Antegra.”

Silence returned.

It did not last eleven seconds this time.

Krell said, “No.”

Solvik looked at him.

The captain's face had gone hard.

“No,” Krell repeated. “We are not discussing that as though it were a maintenance option.”

Senn nodded.

“Governor Varik said much the same thing sixty-eight years ago.”

Varik stared at the recommendation.

“And that is why I built this department.”

“Yes.”

“To avoid the extermination order.”

“Yes.”

“By hiding the infected population from the Imperium.”

“Yes.”

Solvik's bolt pistol lowered slightly.

The Commissar-Keeper looked older than he had upstairs.

“The tithe fraud was not merely quarantine.”

Senn shook his head.

“It was an argument.”

“With whom?”

“The people who wanted Antegra sterilized.”

Varik looked at the active cogitators.

“Did we win?”

Senn laughed.

It became a cough.

When it ended, there was blood on his hand.

“No, Governor.”

He wiped his palm on his robe.

“We delayed the meeting.”

The southern trunk groaned through the walls.

A temperature gauge beside the records hall climbed another degree.

Habitat Twelve, Varik knew, would be falling below survivable levels.

He could picture the emergency chapels opening, families packing beneath heat lamps, station priests telling frightened citizens that suffering was evidence of the Emperor's attention.

The Emperor, in Varik's experience, had many demands upon His attention.

He doubted frozen plumbing ranked highly.

“Vhal,” he said.

“I hear you.”

“Restore the heat.”

“The substructure is drawing it autonomously.”

“Disconnect it.”

“That may terminate mnemonic containment.”

“And leaving it connected may terminate Habitat Twelve.”

“Acceptable losses depend upon—”

“No.”

The word carried through the records hall.

Even the printers seemed to hesitate.

Varik continued.

“No equations. No percentages. No acceptable losses. You will restore the heat.”

“Governor—”

“I have apparently spent sixty-eight years lying to the Imperium in order to keep these people alive. It would be embarrassing to lose them now to a radiator.”

Marr's voice entered the channel.

“I can isolate the audit trunk manually.”

Vhal said, “You do not possess the necessary access.”

“I possess a crowbar.”

Krell looked delighted.

Varik closed his eyes briefly.

Civilization endured.

“Do it,” he said.

The vox went dead.

Senn approached the nearest cogitator.

Lines of text streamed across its display.

Varik joined him.

“What is it doing?”

“Comparing your recovered memory against the station cohort.”

“Why?”

“To determine whether the recognition is local.”

“And if it is not?”

Senn's mouth tightened.

“Then the memories have begun converging.”

Krell said, “Meaning?”

Senn pointed toward the screen.

Names appeared.

Thousands of them.

Citizens from every habitation level.

Miners.

Clerks.

Dockhands.

Children.

Adeptus personnel.

Each name carried a mnemonic correlation percentage.

The numbers were rising.

Varik watched one child go from twelve percent to forty-one in less than a minute.

“What happens at one hundred?”

“We never observed it.”

“That is becoming a tiresome phrase.”

“We deliberately prevented convergence.”

“How?”

Senn looked at him.

“By keeping the infected population socially fragmented.”

Varik understood.

The false census.

The labor rotations.

The inexplicable housing allocations.

The endless permits that kept families in different sectors.

The transit restrictions everyone blamed on incompetence.

The station's bureaucracy had not merely hidden people from the Imperium.

It had hidden them from one another.

A civilization of queues, forms, arbitrary assignments, and contradictory work orders had been engineered as a mnemonic firewall.

Krell understood a moment later.

“You made the station inefficient on purpose.”

Senn nodded.

Krell looked genuinely offended.

Varik could sympathize.

There were crimes, and then there was deliberate paperwork.

“Why is it failing now?” Solvik asked.

Senn looked toward Varik.

“Because the governor remembered.”

“That cannot affect millions of people.”

“No. But his return to Substructure Eighteen triggered the reconciliation system. The department is attempting to rebuild the cohort map.”

“And in doing so?”

“It is telling the memories where everyone is.”

The implication settled over them.

Varik looked at the rising percentages.

The quarantine machinery had become the vector.

A printer began hammering beside them.

Krell tore off the page.

He read it.

His expression changed.

“My lord.”

Varik took the sheet.

The heading was not a population report.

It was a transmission log.

OUTBOUND ASTROPATHIC BURST

SOURCE: SUBSTRUCTURE XVIII

DESTINATION: SEALED

AUTHORITY: VARIK-OMEGA

STATUS: DELIVERED

TIME SINCE TRANSMISSION: 00:04:17

Varik read it twice.

“I did not authorize this.”

Senn leaned against the desk.

“No.”

“What is Varik-Omega?”

The old man's eyes closed.

“Your dead-man authority.”

Solvik raised his pistol again.

“Who received the message?”

Senn opened his eyes.

“The people we delayed.”

The records hall lights turned white.

Every cogitator stopped.

Every printer fell silent.

Then one machine at the center of the hall began printing.

Slowly.

One line at a time.

Varik walked toward it.

The paper emerged warm beneath his fingers.

ACKNOWLEDGEMENT RECEIVED.

SEVERANCE REVIEW REOPENED.

AUTHORIZED OBSERVERS IN TRANSIT.

Krell stared at the message.

“How long?”

The printer answered.

ESTIMATED ARRIVAL: NINETEEN STANDARD HOURS.

Varik looked at Senn.

“Who are the observers?”

Senn sank into the nearest chair.

For the first time since they had found him, the old reconciliator looked frightened.

“I was hoping,” he said, “that you remembered.”

Varik looked down at the message.

Somewhere above them, metal screamed.

Then the station heating pumps restarted.

Marr's voice came through the vox, breathless and triumphant.

“Southern trunk restored.”

Krell smiled.

“Crowbar?”

“Crowbar.”

Varik folded the severance notice into his coat.

Nineteen hours.

After sixty-eight years of successful administrative delay, the bill had finally arrived.

Naturally, it had been marked urgent.
