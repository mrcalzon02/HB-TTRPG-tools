# Matron Lagas Atrovald

## Continuation V — The Counterfoil

By morning, the search for Hesk Venn had produced its first living casualty.

The quartermaster had been suspended.

Arthus Pell brought the order to Atrovald with the expression he reserved for documents whose existence he considered a personal insult.

"On what grounds?" she asked.

"Improper issue of stores to an unregistered recipient."

"She issued a blanket to an escort."

"That is her defence."

"It is also what her ledger says."

"Personnel has decided the distinction requires investigation."

Atrovald held out her hand.

The suspension carried three signatures. None belonged to the quartermaster's commanding officer. A fourth space, for an emergency replacement, was blank.

"Who is issuing blankets now?"

"No one. Stores has sealed the issue cage pending reconciliation."

"How long?"

"Forty-eight hours."

"And the transfer crews?"

"They have been instructed to retain existing equipment."

"Of course. Winter is famous for respecting instructions."

She wrote a stay of suspension limited to ordinary stores duties. The disputed requisition would remain under seal. The quartermaster would remain available for interview. No one would be permitted to use a missing-person inquiry as an excuse to stop issuing gloves to people who demonstrably had hands.

Pell accepted the order.

"Will Personnel object?"

"Almost certainly."

"Shall I prepare a response?"

"Let them produce an objection first. We need not manufacture their work for them."

He turned toward the door, then stopped.

"I found the chit."

Atrovald set down her pen.

"Which chit?"

"The one the reporting escort took away from the inquiry clerk."

"Where?"

"In the custody duty office."

"How?"

"That is the part I have not yet understood."

She stood.

"Then we shall enjoy understanding it together."

---

The custody duty office occupied a chamber so narrow that two clerks could not pass each other without temporarily suspending the Imperial hierarchy.

Its chief registrar had brought out three books.

One recorded authority to issue custody chits. One recorded their physical removal. The third recorded their return, destruction, or transfer to a successor officer.

Atrovald approved of this arrangement. Three separate opportunities for human error were preferable to one invisible opportunity for automatic correction.

The registrar pointed to the issue book.

"Fifty-three positions authorized for the witness escort."

"People?"

"Positions, Matron."

"Named?"

"Not in this register."

The line had been written before the transfer. It carried the appropriate custody-office seal. The next book recorded fifty-three numbered chits detached from their binding, including the final one.

The last chit was present on the desk.

A narrow piece of stiff paper, folded twice, with a torn edge that matched the surviving stub in the book.

Pell placed the two edges beside one another without touching them together.

"That is the one from the inquiry?"

"According to the receiving entry," the registrar said.

"According to whom?"

"The officer who returned it."

"Name?"

The registrar looked at the third book.

"Not entered."

Atrovald waited.

"He had the custody-office seal."

"A seal is a tool. A person uses it."

"Yes, Matron."

"Did you see him?"

"No. I was on the next watch."

"Who received it?"

"Night relief."

"Where is night relief?"

"On compulsory rest after a thirty-hour shift."

"Leave them there until their rest is complete."

The registrar looked surprised. People expected a Matron investigating a vanished guard to begin by depriving every other guard of sleep.

"Show me the return book."

Fifty-two chits were accounted for by ordinary return marks. The fifty-third had been entered separately as a disputed inquiry item, then marked returned to custody after the complaint was withdrawn.

"Withdrawn by whom?" Atrovald asked.

"That is the same difficulty."

"No. The first difficulty is whether this piece of paper is the one issued. The second is who brought it back. The third is who closed the inquiry. Do not combine them merely because the same man might answer all three."

Pell wrote the distinctions in his manual book.

The registrar measured the perforation against the stub. They matched. So did the serial number, the edge watermark and a tiny crescent of blue ink left by the duty officer's pen.

A genuine chit.

Not a genuine man.

The reverse carried a single stamped word.

**EXCEPTION.**

The stamp had been applied crookedly. Its ink matched the ordinary return-book exception stamp, chained to the desk for eleven years.

"That could have been stamped here," Pell said.

"Yes."

"Or before it came back."

"Yes."

"Then it tells us very little."

"It tells us someone wanted the exception recorded. We may eventually learn who."

Atrovald left the chit on the table. The registrar looked relieved that she had not taken it away.

He was beginning to understand her.

---

The inquiry had not been closed by the clerk who opened it.

That much was established.

The reason it appeared closed was less interesting and considerably more dangerous.

A second registrar, from the reconciliation office, demonstrated the procedure with an unrelated case. At the end of each watch, an authorized count adjustment passed through the custody balance ledger. When the adjusted count matched the recorded return, the ledger generated a closure notice for any open inquiry whose only stated cause was a numerical discrepancy.

The procedure did not require a complainant's signature.

"It should," Atrovald said.

The registrar stiffened.

"I did not design it, Matron."

"I am aware. Show me the governing instruction."

He did.

It had been approved sixty-three years earlier to prevent clerks from leaving thousands of resolved numerical discrepancies open indefinitely. A reasonable solution to a real problem. A person had once spent four years investigating a shipment counted twice because two departments used different definitions of a crate.

The solution had acquired authority far beyond its purpose.

Atrovald read the operative line.

**BALANCE RESTORED: INQUIRY MAY BE CLOSED WITHOUT FURTHER COMPLAINANT ACTION.**

"May," she said.

"Yes, Matron."

"Not must."

"The batch procedure treats it as must."

"Who authorized that interpretation?"

The registrar produced a supplementary schedule. It bore a departmental seal, not an individual's signature. Its approval date fell well before the present transfer.

"Then the machine has been faithfully carrying out a bad interpretation for some time."

"Is it to be shut down?"

"No. How many unrelated inquiries does it process?"

"Several hundred each watch."

"And how many require the same correction?"

"We do not know."

Atrovald considered the consequence. Turning off the process would create a backlog across the spire. Leaving it untouched would continue to convert corrected arithmetic into the appearance of testimony withdrawn.

"Isolate inquiries involving a missing person or disputed physical custody. Those require human review. Leave the ordinary crate disputes alone."

"That requires an amended instruction."

"Prepare one for my signature and the custody office's. Until both offices agree, restore the Venn inquiry by hand and append the reason. Do not erase its closure."

The registrar frowned.

"Then it will show both open and closed."

"It was closed. Improperly. Those are two facts, not an inconvenience."

Pell made another note.

The registrar looked at the closure entry again.

"Someone still submitted the adjusted count."

"Yes," Atrovald said. "Find the request."

It was not there.

There was a valid office authorization, an adjusted total of fifty-two, and a machine receipt recording acceptance. The initiating officer's personal sign was absent from the surviving record.

The registrar offered three possible explanations, all ordinary: an older shared seal, a damaged authentication strip, or a manual transfer from a paper count sheet since misfiled.

Atrovald wrote down all three.

"Do you prefer one?" Pell asked.

"No."

"Not even privately?"

"Especially not privately."

---

The night-relief officer came to her after six hours of sleep and a meal.

He remembered the chit.

He remembered the man who returned it less well.

"He wore an escort coat," the officer said. "He said the count had been corrected. I put the chit with the inquiry returns."

"Did you know the inquiry clerk had refused to withdraw it?"

"No."

"Did he tell you who was missing?"

"No."

"Did he use the name Hesk Venn?"

"No."

"Was he cold?"

The officer considered.

"Everyone was cold that night."

Atrovald made no mark.

"Do you remember his face?"

"Only that he was tired. I could describe a dozen men who looked the same."

"Then don't select one."

After he left, Pell placed the maintenance slip for the transit lift beside the interview record.

"The refrigeration baffle," he said.

"Yes."

"The inquiry clerk remembered a cold man. Night relief remembers a cold night."

"Neither identifies Venn."

"And Predulum remembered an empty place."

"Predulum has occupied the same institution as all of them. We cannot certify independence by placing people in separate rooms after they have had years to hear one another."

Pell looked at the statements.

"How do we establish independence?"

"We may not be able to. Seek evidence that existed before the interviews."

"Such as the issue book."

"Such as the issue book."

He hesitated.

"Does that mean you believe there were fifty-three?"

"I believe fifty-three chits were issued. I remember fifty-three guards. I have not proved those are the same count."

Pell returned the lift slip to its folder.

He did not ask her to correct the memory.

For that she was grateful.

---

The quartermaster returned to work before second watch.

Atrovald found her in the issue cage, distributing insulated gloves to a maintenance crew whose supervisor had been forced to sign three emergency exceptions while Stores was closed.

The woman was angry.

She had every reason to be.

"Matron. I gave equipment to the escort officer. I did not invent a guard."

"I know what your ledger establishes."

"Then why was I suspended?"

"Because an office confused a disputed recipient with a fraudulent issue."

"Will they say so?"

"They will be instructed to amend the personnel action. Your suspension remains part of the record, with the stay and reason beside it."

The quartermaster's expression did not soften.

"So I still have a suspension."

"You have a record that one was issued and overturned. Erasing it would make the next mistake easier."

"That is not much comfort."

"No."

The woman set a pair of gloves on the counter.

"Two sizes," she told the waiting crewman. "Try both before you sign. I am finished being blamed for people whose hands I have not seen."

The crewman tried them.

Atrovald watched the small exchange with more attention than she had given the S-Rank register that morning.

There was a human cost to every abstract category. Sometimes it was a shift without pay. Sometimes it was a mechanic sent into a refrigerated lift without gloves because a committee wished to reconcile a number.

Sometimes, if Pell's memory was true, it was a man whose disappearance had been processed as an improvement in arithmetic.

The quartermaster caught her looking.

"Is there anything else?"

"Yes. You are entitled to your missed shift allowance. I will have Personnel calculate it."

"They'll object."

"I expect so."

For the first time the woman almost smiled.

---

Atrovald returned to the custody office after the final watch change.

The registrar had found the dispatch counterfoil.

Not a new record. An older sheet kept beneath the issue book because the office had never been granted enough shelf space to store both properly.

The paper had been written before the escort departed. Its ink had dried beneath the pressure mark of the following day's entries. The line was unremarkable.

**ESCORT COMPLEMENT AT DISPATCH: 53.**

Below it, in a different hand:

**RETURNED TO CUSTODY: 52.**

The second line carried no date and no personal signature. A departmental seal had been applied beside it.

"Can you authenticate the first line?" Atrovald asked.

"The dispatch officer's handwriting is known. The seal impression matches the day's other entries. The paper remained bound under the following sheet."

"And the second?"

"Not yet. It could have been entered on return or later."

Pell leaned over the page.

"That is another fifty-three."

"Another record of fifty-three," she corrected.

"But it predates the inquiry."

"Yes."

For the first time, the discrepancy existed in a surviving physical record made before anyone had opened a missing-person case. It did not depend solely on Pell's memory, Predulum's uncertain recollection or the inquiry clerk's report of what another man had said.

It still did not prove that Hesk Venn had been present.

Atrovald ordered the sheet preserved in its binding. A physical concordance would be made in the presence of the dispatch registrar, Pell and a custody officer who had not participated in the original transfer. No copy would be silently substituted into the ordinary personnel roll.

"Can we now ask the escort commander for his roster?" Pell said.

"We have asked."

"He has not answered."

"Then ask his office for the duty assignment and the identity of whoever submitted the count adjustment. Two requests. Two chains. They may not lead to the same person."

"And the missing-person notice?"

"Remains open."

"The future order?"

"Remains sealed."

"Mortuary-Seventeen?"

"Unopened."

"Predulum?"

"With Witness Four, in Null-Seven."

Pell closed his notebook.

"What have we accomplished?"

They had proved that a discrepancy had been written down before it was made to disappear. They had discovered how an unsigned closure could be produced without anyone openly taking responsibility for it. They had restored a clerk's complaint, a quartermaster's livelihood and a missing-person inquiry to their proper places.

They had not found a man.

"Enough to know which questions we are permitted to ask next," she said.

Pell regarded the books.

"That sounds very little."

"Then consider what it would cost to ask the wrong ones."

Outside the duty office, the Interlocutum continued its work. Clerks moved paper. Wardens changed watch. The mechanical clock completed another minute without seeking permission from its electronic counterpart.

Fifty-three at dispatch.

Fifty-two at return.

Between the two numbers lay a place for a person.

Whether anyone had occupied it remained the only question she would not allow the archive to answer for her.
