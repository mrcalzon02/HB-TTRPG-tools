# House Vallimere's Sons

## Continuation I — The First Lecture

**Based on the work of: House Vallimere's Sons.**

*An original Cafarron Corridor chronicle continuation. Warhammer 40,000 and associated marks and setting elements are the property of Games Workshop. This non-commercial fan work is not endorsed by or affiliated with Games Workshop.*

Professor Prite had been a professor for eleven hours when somebody tried to kill him with a syllabus.

The weapon was four hundred and eighty-seven pages long.

It arrived at breakfast.

Prite stared at the bound volume on the table between the recaf pot and a plate of eggs which House Vallimere's kitchen staff had somehow contrived to make square.

Darcelle leaned over his shoulder.

"What's that?"

"Our approved introductory curriculum."

Renault looked up from the eggs.

"We have one?"

"Apparently we have had one for six years."

Darcelle went pale.

This was not unusual. Since their promotion, induction into House Vallimere, discovery that several noble families wished to murder them legally, and acquisition by an Inquisitorial protection detail, Darcelle had developed a broad and flexible relationship with pallor.

Prite opened the book.

The first chapter was entitled *The Proper Moral Geometry of the Line of Battle*.

He closed it.

"No."

Renault chewed thoughtfully.

"Strong opening."

"No."

"You said that."

"I wanted to be certain the machine-spirit heard me."

At the far end of the room, their new security officer did not react.

The man's Academy armour still identified him as Edward.

Prite knew better now.

Interrogator-Senior Iral Vane had a gift for standing so still that furniture gradually accepted him as one of its own. The real Edward, according to information Prite had decided not to request formally, was alive, furious, and recovering from an involuntary nap in a linen closet.

Prite opened the syllabus again.

By page thirty-two, it had explained that battleships possessed spiritual primacy over cruisers.

By page seventy, it had established that retreat represented a moral failure except where ordered by someone sufficiently senior, at which point it became strategic repositioning.

By page one hundred and six, it had used the phrase *honourable concentration of force* nine times without once discussing fuel.

Prite put his forehead on the table.

"They want us dead," he said.

Darcelle glanced toward Vane.

"The nobles?"

"The curriculum committee."

Renault took the book.

"Same thing with stationery."

That earned the smallest movement from Vane. Not a smile. An acknowledgement that smiling had once existed.

The Headmaster had ordered them to write their core doctrine before political interests could reshape it.

Political interests, apparently, had arrived early and brought pagination.

Their first lecture was scheduled for ten hundred.

At nine forty-seven, Prite discovered that the lecture hall contained one hundred and twenty cadets, seventeen serving officers, six noble observers, three Mechanicus simulation adepts, two representatives of the curriculum office, and one man whose uniform contained so much gold braid that Prite initially mistook him for a curtain.

Darcelle stopped in the doorway.

"We can leave."

"No," Prite said.

"We absolutely can. The door is behind us."

Renault peered into the hall.

"If we run now, they'll call it maneuver warfare."

Vane stepped aside to let them pass.

"Priority Omega does not require dignity," he murmured.

Prite looked at him.

It was the longest sentence Vane had spoken to them all morning.

"Useful," Prite said.

They entered.

The applause was thin.

Most of the cadets looked curious. The officers looked offended. The nobles looked as though three maintenance workers had climbed onto the altar during Mass and announced improvements to the Emperor.

Prite understood the objection.

Two weeks ago, he would have objected too.

He placed the approved syllabus on the lectern.

Then he placed a wrench on top of it.

The hall became quiet.

"Good morning," he said. "I am Professor Prite. These are Professors Darcelle and Renault. You may have heard other names associated with us. Those names will not be discussed because half the Navy wants to promote them and the other half wants to duel them."

A few cadets laughed.

The gold curtain did not.

Prite touched the lectern control.

A void-battle simulation appeared above them.

Two fleets.

The blue fleet was larger, newer, and arranged in a textbook battle line.

The red fleet was smaller and scattered around a gas giant's outer moons.

"Blue has a twenty-eight percent advantage in effective broadside mass," Prite said. "Better crews. Better admirals. Better augur resolution. Red has older ships, less ammunition, and one escort squadron whose maintenance report contains the phrase 'reactor noise within devotional tolerance.'"

Renault leaned toward his lectern.

"Which means loud."

"Very loud," Prite agreed.

One of the curriculum representatives raised a hand.

Prite ignored him.

"Who wins?"

Hands rose.

Blue.

Of course blue.

A cadet in the second row cited tonnage.

Another cited range.

A lieutenant cited the moral advantage of concentrated Imperial firepower.

Prite nodded.

"Excellent. Blue loses in nineteen minutes."

Silence.

He started the simulation.

The red fleet did not attack.

It ran.

The gold curtain made a disgusted sound.

Blue pursued.

Red split around the moons.

Blue maintained formation.

Red dropped mines from cargo lighters hidden behind a refinery hulk.

Blue altered course.

One cruiser clipped the gravity well too deeply and burned fuel correcting.

A red escort squadron appeared from behind the moon, fired one torpedo spread, missed completely, and fled.

Several cadets laughed.

Prite froze the simulation.

"Why did they miss?"

A cadet answered, "Poor firing solution."

"No. They weren't shooting at the cruiser."

He advanced the display.

The torpedoes struck a navigation beacon.

Blue's formation adjusted automatically around the loss of the trusted reference point.

The lead battleship corrected twelve degrees.

The following cruiser corrected eleven.

The third vessel corrected thirteen.

The formation widened.

Red's hidden batteries fired through the gap.

The laughter stopped.

"The beacon," Prite said, "cost less than one of your dress uniforms. Blue trusted it because nobody receives medals for distrusting navigation infrastructure."

The gold curtain stood.

He was an admiral.

Prite realized this because everyone else became afraid before the man spoke.

"This is trickery."

Renault nodded pleasantly.

"Yes, Admiral."

The man blinked.

Renault continued.

"War is full of it. Very inconsiderate."

Darcelle made a strangled noise that might have been a cough or prayer.

The Admiral's face darkened.

"An Imperial officer does not base doctrine upon accidents, maintenance failures and dishonourable deception."

Prite looked at the simulation.

Then at the Admiral.

"Why not?"

The question landed harder than any insult.

Prite continued before survival instinct could intervene.

"The enemy does."

Nobody moved.

At the rear of the hall, Vane shifted his weight.

Prite wondered whether Priority Omega covered professors who deliberately antagonized admirals.

Probably.

There had been paperwork.

He restarted the battle.

Blue died by degrees.

Not gloriously.

That was the lesson.

A battleship lost drive efficiency because it had burned too hard correcting around the moon. A cruiser withdrew to protect a fuel tender. Red refused every engagement that favored Blue and accepted every ugly little exchange that did not. The weaker fleet attacked tankers, beacons, repair craft, vox relays and finally the wounded ships left behind by the pursuit.

At nineteen minutes, Blue still possessed more guns.

It simply possessed them in the wrong places, without fuel, ammunition, or reliable orders.

Prite ended the simulation.

"The Academy taught you to destroy fleets," he said. "We are going to teach you to make fleets stop being fleets."

This time nobody laughed.

Darcelle took over.

His voice shook for the first thirty seconds.

Then the simulation controls appeared under his hands.

The fear went away.

He explained how sensor assumptions hardened into doctrine. How officers learned what a simulator permitted and mistook those permissions for laws of nature. How maintenance crews knew where machines lied because maintenance crews spent their lives listening to lies made of metal.

Renault followed with a demonstration in which a numerically inferior squadron won by deliberately presenting a damaged cruiser as bait.

A cadet objected that no noble captain would tolerate being used that way.

"Correct," Renault said. "That is why it works better on nobles."

The hall laughed.

Even some officers.

Not the Admiral.

At the end of two hours, the curriculum representatives approached the lectern.

The senior one looked ill.

"Professors, your presentation did not correspond to the approved syllabus."

Prite looked down at the four-hundred-and-eighty-seven-page volume beneath his wrench.

"No."

"You are required to submit deviations for review."

"How long does review take?"

"Approximately fourteen months."

"The Headmaster ordered our doctrine recorded immediately."

"Then you should have submitted it fourteen months ago."

Prite stared at him.

The man stared back.

For one radiant moment, Prite understood the Imperium completely.

Then the feeling passed, leaving only a headache.

The Headmaster arrived before the argument became theological.

He surveyed the emptying hall, the angry Admiral, the delighted cadets, the curriculum officers, and the wrench.

"How did it go?"

Darcelle answered first.

"I think we're being challenged to three more duels."

"Four," Vane said from the wall.

Everyone looked at him.

He touched his vox-bead.

"One arrived during the fuel-tender section."

The Headmaster sighed.

"House Vallimere accepts none of them."

"Can Houses do that?" Prite asked.

"Old Houses can do almost anything if they possess sufficiently tedious precedent."

He picked up the approved syllabus.

"Did you use this?"

"As ballast," Renault said.

The Headmaster nodded.

"Good."

He handed it to the curriculum representative.

"Withdraw it."

The man went pale.

"Headmaster, this curriculum has been certified by the Academy Board for six years."

"Then it has had a long and honorable service. Give it a pension."

"And replace it with what?"

The Headmaster looked at the three technicians who were no longer technicians.

Prite understood.

He did not like understanding.

"Ours," he said.

"Yours."

The Headmaster turned toward the door.

"You wanted to know why I told you to write quickly. This is why. The Imperium can survive a new idea. What it cannot resist is improving it until it resembles the old one."

He left.

For several seconds, none of the Three Admirals spoke.

Then Darcelle sat heavily on the edge of the lectern.

"We have to write a syllabus."

Prite looked at the abandoned simulation.

"Yes."

"A real one."

"Yes."

"With chapters."

Renault picked up the wrench.

"We could put diagrams in it."

Darcelle looked hopeful.

"Mostly diagrams?"

"No," Prite said.

Darcelle's hope died.

Outside the lecture hall, cadets were already arguing about the battle.

Not about who had been right.

About why.

Prite listened.

That frightened him more than the duel petitions.

Winning a simulation was easy. You learned the machine, found the assumptions, and broke them before the other player did.

Teaching was different.

Teaching meant putting the exploit into someone else's head and hoping they understood why it worked instead of merely memorizing where to click.

For years, the Three Admirals had fought the Imperial Navy as a game.

Now the Imperial Navy was listening.

Prite opened a blank slate.

At the top he wrote:

**LESSON ONE: THE ENEMY IS NOT OBLIGED TO RESPECT YOUR DOCTRINE.**

Renault read it over his shoulder.

"Bit obvious."

Prite looked at the four duel petitions now blinking on Vane's slate.

"Apparently not."
