# The Long Patrol of Broussard

## XIV. THE PRICE OF MOVEMENT

**Author:** Mrcalzon02 / Christopher Vardeman  
**Based on the work of:** *The Long Patrol of Broussard*

The dead predator froze before the tow frame did.

By morning, High Presidio had welded the two together.

Captain Broussard stood beside Chimera One and watched six recovery men argue with several tonnes of dead animal from a respectful distance.

Nobody had approached the carcass yet.

That was why they were still allowed to argue.

The wind had dropped during the night. Without it, the cold felt less violent and more deliberate. The *Hesperant Vale* stood in a bowl of work lights, its damaged quarter webbed in temporary cabling and scaffold. Steam feathered from vents along the hull. Every few minutes a tool struck metal somewhere inside, the sound travelling through the freighter and out across the ice.

The carcass lay thirty metres beyond the contracted perimeter.

Most of it had frozen into the hole it had made. The head was ruined. One forelimb remained above the surface with the recovery frame hooked across it. Chain and tackle disappeared beneath black ice around the body.

The recovery chief lowered his field glass.

"We can get the frame."

Broussard looked at him.

"You can see the frame."

"Same beginning."

"Different ending."

The chief handed him the glass.

"We need it."

That was true.

The hauler still had power. The *Hesperant Vale* still had attachment points. What they did not have was another complete heavy alignment assembly. Without the frame, Sella's repair crews could restore the ship and still leave it sitting on High Presidio until convoy command sent another recovery package.

That would take longer than thirty-seven hours.

Possibly much longer.

The convoy did not possess infinite patience, infinite fuel, or infinite escorts.

Nothing Imperial possessed infinite anything except paperwork and distance.

"Ice?" Broussard asked.

"Bad around the carcass. Better from the bow side."

"Better isn't a measurement."

"Two-point-four metres at the nearest safe drill. One-point-one near the break. Less where it refroze."

"Load?"

"Men and hand gear, yes. Hauler, no. Chimera, no."

Broussard looked toward Chimera Two.

The damaged vision block had been plated over during the night. Its track was still missing two outer pads.

Mobile.

Not something he intended to test on fractured ice.

"Cable recovery only," he said. "Nobody crosses the red markers. Anchor from intact ice. If the frame doesn't come, it stays eaten."

The chief grimaced.

"Captain—"

"You asked for the frame. I gave you a method."

"A slow one."

"You have thirty-six hours."

The chief looked up at the freighter.

"Thirty-seven."

"An hour passed."

That ended the negotiation.

---

Sella found him under the starboard quarter twenty minutes later.

She had grease on one cheek and a slate under her arm.

"You stole an hour."

"Time passed."

"Not in engineering."

"How does it work there?"

"We become angry until the clock apologizes."

Broussard looked at the slate.

"Did it?"

"No. Thirty-nine."

He stopped.

Sella held his gaze.

"Before you ask, nothing new hit us."

"Then why?"

She turned the slate around.

A structural diagram showed the freighter's damaged quarter in layers. One support line had changed from amber to red.

"The last impact shifted the outer brace. Not enough to fail. Enough that I won't certify thrust against it."

"Repairable?"

"Yes."

"Thirty-nine hours?"

"If the replacement member seats cleanly."

"And if it doesn't?"

"You already know how this works."

He did.

Broussard studied the diagram.

"Can you test propulsion before the tow frame is recovered?"

"No."

"Can you test the drive plant?"

"Already have."

"Result?"

"It remains a drive plant."

He looked at her.

She sighed.

"Stable at idle. Stable under partial load. We haven't pushed it against the hull because I enjoy having a hull."

"Reasonable."

"Thank you. I was hoping for professional recognition."

A worker above them shouted for a torque setting.

Sella shouted one back without looking up.

Then she lowered her voice.

"The frame matters."

"I know."

"If they can't recover it—"

"I know."

She nodded.

Shared history saved them both several sentences.

"All right," she said. "Then I need the forward utility winch released from cargo handling."

"Kelm?"

"Already objected."

"Why?"

"Because cargo handling is using it."

"For what?"

"Cargo."

Broussard waited.

Sella smiled without warmth.

"He has a point."

"I'll talk to him."

"Good. He likes you."

"Does he?"

"No."

She walked away.

---

Kelm was in the forward holds.

Broussard had not been back there since they found Noll, Vale and Dorn.

The service junction had been isolated properly now. New warning plates covered the damaged decking. A temporary bridge crossed the section where the intermittent cross-feed had killed three men in sequence.

Nobody lingered there.

There was nothing mysterious about the junction anymore.

That did not make it friendly.

Kelm stood beyond it beside the loose produce bins, arguing with a cargo master over a stack of pallets that had shifted during the predator strike.

The bins rose around them in long industrial ranks.

Broussard stepped off the bridge.

Kelm saw him.

"No."

"I haven't asked."

"Sella has."

"Then this may be efficient."

"It won't be."

The cargo master wisely found another pallet to inspect.

Broussard looked toward the overhead utility winch track.

"She needs it."

"So do I."

"For shifted cargo."

"For cargo that becomes lethal if it shifts again under thrust."

Broussard nodded.

That was also true.

Kelm folded his arms.

"If I release the winch now, we stop securing the forward hold. If we stop securing the hold, Sella can repair the ship and I still won't move it."

"How long?"

"Six hours."

"She needs it in four."

"Then one of us is disappointed."

Broussard looked at the pallets.

"What can my people do?"

Kelm's expression changed.

Not much.

Enough.

"Your people don't know my cargo plan."

"They can carry things."

"That is not the same skill."

"No."

Broussard waited.

Kelm glanced toward the cargo master.

Then back at him.

"Twenty soldiers. Under my master. Nobody improvises. Nobody decides a restraint looks unnecessary. Nobody opens anything."

"Done."

"And the winch stays for two hours."

"One."

"Two."

"Ninety minutes."

Kelm stared at him.

"You bargain like a dock thief."

"I have been promoted since."

"Ninety minutes."

They shook on it.

Broussard crossed back over the isolated junction.

He did not look at the produce bins again.

There was work in front of him.

Behind him, something knocked softly from inside one of the bins.

The cargo master looked up.

A second knock came from farther down the row.

He listened.

Then swore at the refrigeration system and went back to the shifted pallets.

---

The frame moved at 1430.

Not much.

Half a metre.

It was enough to make twenty exhausted recovery personnel cheer before remembering where they were and reducing the cheer to several ugly noises.

Broussard watched from behind the anchor vehicle.

The retrieval cable ran from the intact ice through two ground pulleys and across the red marker line to the frozen frame. Nobody stood beside it. Nobody needed to.

The first pull had snapped a hook.

The second had torn frozen tissue away from the predator's forelimb.

The third moved the frame.

"Again," the recovery chief said.

The winch operator eased power on.

Cable tightened.

The frame scraped across the ice.

A crack ran outward from the carcass.

"Stop."

The winch stopped.

The crack stopped.

Everyone watched it.

Nothing happened.

The chief exhaled.

"Different angle."

They moved the ground pulley three metres.

Nobody complained about the delay.

High Presidio had become persuasive.

The fourth pull brought the frame free.

It came suddenly, tearing out of frozen flesh and sliding across the ice with a shower of black fragments. The crew let it travel until it crossed the red markers.

Only then did anyone approach.

The chief crouched beside it.

"Bent."

Broussard joined him.

One arm of the frame had twisted nearly ten degrees. A shackle mount was gone. Two chain runs remained somewhere beneath the predator.

"Usable?"

The chief ran a glove along the metal.

"Not like this."

"Repairable?"

"Everything is repairable if you don't care how long it takes."

Broussard looked at him.

"You have thirty-eight hours."

The chief frowned.

"Thought it was thirty-nine."

"An hour passed."

The chief stared at him.

"I liked you better yesterday."

"Yesterday you had a complete tow frame."

That was difficult to argue with.

---

By the second night, the recovery operation had become two operations pretending to be one.

Sella's crews rebuilt the outer brace and restored the starboard systems in stages.

Kelm's people secured cargo with soldiers doing exactly what the cargo master told them and, after one early mistake involving a restraint ratchet, nothing else.

The recovery crew heated the bent frame, pressed it against a portable jig, replaced the missing shackle mount with a reinforced field assembly and submitted every weld to inspection.

Broussard's detachment watched the ice.

Nothing attacked.

That did not make the watch easier.

At 0310, marker twenty-one vanished.

No crack.

No ridge.

It simply leaned and dropped out of sight.

The nearest squad reported it.

Broussard came out of the command shelter already fastening his coat.

Gabriel met him halfway to the perimeter.

"Twenty-one."

"Anything else?"

"No."

They stopped behind Chimera Four.

The night beyond the lamps was absolute.

Broussard looked at the marker line through the field glass.

Twenty.

Empty space.

Twenty-two.

No movement.

"Could be ice settling," Gabriel said.

"Yes."

"Could be another one."

"Yes."

Gabriel waited.

"I preferred you when you had opinions."

"I have one."

"Which is?"

"Nobody goes out to retrieve the pole."

Gabriel nodded.

That was enough opinion for the night.

They doubled the watch on the intact eastern approach and left marker twenty-one to whatever had taken it.

Nothing else happened.

Sometimes fear earned nothing for its trouble.

That did not make it wasted.

---

Thirty-four hours after the attack, Sella called Broussard to the bridge.

Kelm was already there.

So was the recovery chief.

The status board had changed.

The red structural line was amber.

Then green.

Broussard looked at Sella.

"Certified?"

"For controlled thrust."

"Not full?"

"Do you want to leave today?"

"Yes."

"Then controlled thrust."

The recovery chief put a hand on the back of a chair.

"Frame is ready."

Kelm said, "Cargo is secured."

Nobody smiled.

Broussard looked at the board.

The *Hesperant Vale* was no longer merely a casualty.

It was a ship that might move.

That distinction had killed people before.

"Test sequence?"

Sella brought it up.

"Idle plant. Internal distribution. Steering auxiliaries. Controlled drive load against tow restraint. Then we move her three metres. Stop. Inspect. If nothing complains, ten metres. Stop. Inspect."

"And after that?"

"After that we find out whether High Presidio intends to keep us."

Kelm looked toward the bridge windows.

"It has been possessive."

The recovery chief tapped the board.

"Tow alignment will hold."

Sella looked at him.

"You hope."

"I welded it."

"That is not a rebuttal."

Broussard let them argue for three seconds.

Then he said, "Do it."

The bridge changed.

Not dramatically.

People simply stopped being tired in public.

Stations filled.

Checks moved down lists.

Orders went out to the perimeter.

All four Chimeras repositioned onto the route already drilled and marked across the intact ice. Chimera Two took the inner position, where its damaged vision block mattered least. Infantry withdrew from the ship's immediate sides and formed moving security farther out.

Nobody approached marker twenty-one.

The recovery hauler took tension.

The repaired frame held.

Sella watched her instruments.

"Drive plant idle stable."

Kelm answered from command.

"Distribution stable."

"Auxiliaries."

A vibration moved through the deck.

Broussard felt it through his boots.

The *Hesperant Vale* had been making noises for days.

This one was different.

Intent had entered the machinery.

"Tow restraint loaded," the recovery chief reported.

Sella's eyes stayed on the board.

"Controlled thrust. Five percent."

The deck trembled.

Something deep in the ship wound upward.

Five seconds.

Ten.

No alarms.

"Seven."

The hauler engine changed note.

The tow cable tightened.

The repaired frame held.

A warning rune flashed amber.

Sella's hand moved.

It cleared.

"Thermal transient. Expected."

Broussard did not ask whether expected meant welcome.

"Ten percent."

The *Hesperant Vale* moved.

Three metres was almost nothing for a voidship.

On High Presidio it felt like a campaign.

The hull shifted forward.

Ice groaned beneath it.

The hauler pulled.

The drive pushed.

Then Sella cut thrust.

"Stop."

Everything stopped.

Nobody spoke for several seconds.

Teams went out.

They inspected the brace.

The frame.

The hull contact points.

The ice.

The temporary feeds.

Broussard waited on the bridge.

The fourteen-second telemetry gap remained on the secondary station.

Still unexplained.

Still not today's problem.

Reports returned.

One by one.

Green.

Green.

Green.

The recovery chief came over the circuit last.

"Frame held."

Sella sat back.

Kelm closed his eyes for one second.

Broussard looked through the forward glass at the marked route across the ice.

Ten metres next.

Then inspection.

Then fifty.

Then the long crawl back toward ground that could take the freighter's weight without trying to swallow the recovery vehicles.

Beyond that waited the convoy.

They were not there yet.

But for the first time since the *Hesperant Vale* had gone down, distance had begun decreasing.

Broussard keyed the circuit.

"Prepare the ten-metre test."

Outside, marker twenty-two leaned.

Only slightly.

Nobody on the bridge saw it.
