IV. THE COMMISSAR ARRIVES

The equipment deck had become a cathedral of complaints.

Not loud complaints, naturally.

The Imperial Guard had developed many sophisticated methods of maintaining morale, most of which involved making certain nobody within earshot of an officer could be proven to have said anything punishable.

Instead there was sighing.

There was equipment being moved with unnecessary force.

There were muttered observations concerning the parentage of convoy command.

There were repeated questions about why ammunition boxes always seemed to become heavier precisely when a rest period had been canceled.

And there was, from somewhere beneath the hull of Chimera Four-Two, the unmistakable sound of a wrench being thrown.

Broussard allowed it.

Within reason.

The men had been scheduled for rest.

They had been looking forward to rest.

Rest was not merely sleep. Rest meant hot food, proper maintenance, dry socks, washing oneself somewhere not violently vibrating, writing letters, gambling away one's pay, and spending several precious hours pretending the universe had forgotten you existed.

Instead, they were rearming for an independent recovery mission.

The four Chimeras sat nose-to-tail across one section of the Saint Malderson's lower equipment deck while loaders, crewmen, mechanics, quartermasters and one increasingly irritated servitor moved supplies around them.

Fresh lasgun charge packs were being stacked into internal lockers.

Emergency ration tins were loaded into every unused compartment.

Promethium reserves were checked.

Medical kits were replaced.

Cold-weather equipment was inspected.

Additional ammunition crates were strapped along exterior stowage racks.

Cargo netting had been bundled onto the recovery hauler in quantities sufficient to restrain either a freighter's cargo or, Broussard suspected, a moderately sized city district.

The men groused throughout.

This too he allowed.

A soldier prevented from complaining would eventually discover some other form of recreation.

Historically, those alternatives included drinking, fighting, gambling, theft, unauthorized explosives, religious enthusiasm, and marriage.

Of the available options, Broussard preferred complaining.

He had summoned the commanders of the other three Chimeras to meet him beside his own vehicle.

Lieutenant Dalen Voss commanded Chimera Two.

Sergeant-Major Kell Orro had Chimera Three, having acquired field command after the death of his original lieutenant two deployments before and somehow never relinquished it.

Chimera Four belonged to Acting Lieutenant Vella Marr, who had technically been acting lieutenant for eighteen months and had reached the stage where everybody involved had quietly agreed the word acting could probably be ignored.

Their vehicle detachments gathered around them.

Drivers.

Gunners.

Vox operators.

Squad leaders.

Medicae personnel.

A pair of combat engineers.

The recovery hauler's crew.

And the Mechanicus contingent, whose Magos had not so much joined the meeting as positioned himself nearby while giving the impression that the conversation was an atmospheric phenomenon beneath his notice.

Broussard climbed onto a maintenance crate.

It had become his unofficial podium.

He disliked podiums.

Podiums encouraged speeches.

Speeches encouraged officers to hear themselves speaking.

This was how military disasters acquired introductions.

He unfolded the order packet.

The talking subsided.

Not instantly.

That would have been unnatural.

But gradually enough that Broussard did not need to shout.

"Right," he said. "You've all heard some version of this already, and most of those versions are probably wrong."

A few smiles.

Good.

"We have a disabled commercial freighter approximately forty-seven kilometers west-northwest of the current route. Engine compartment breach. Main propulsion compromised. Emergency power operational. Crew alive. Cargo presently intact."

Someone near the rear murmured, "Presently."

Broussard pointed toward the voice without looking.

"Exactly. Presently."

That earned a few more smiles.

"We are being detached to secure the site, assist repairs, protect the crew, and return the vessel to convoy operation if possible."

He glanced around the gathered soldiers.

"If it cannot be repaired, we evacuate personnel and recover whatever cargo, fuel, equipment and supplies we can carry."

He raised the bundle of orders.

"The important portion is that we have been authorized independent operational command until rejoining the convoy."

That got their attention.

Independent command could mean freedom.

It could also mean being sufficiently far away from everyone else that there would be nobody nearby when things went wrong.

The Guard generally managed to combine both interpretations.

Broussard continued.

"The convoy will not stop for us."

A few expressions hardened.

"We should expect to lose ground steadily while repairs are underway. Best estimate is thirty-six to seventy-two hours."

"Best estimate from who?" Voss asked.

"Mechanicus."

Voss looked toward the Magos.

The Magos did not visibly acknowledge that anyone organic had spoken.

"Then seventy-two," Voss muttered.

"That's the spirit."

A few men chuckled.

Broussard folded his arms.

"Now, I know what you're all thinking."

Hessel called from near his Chimera.

"That we were supposed to be asleep?"

"Among other things."

"Because I was definitely thinking that."

"Thank you, Hessel."

"Always happy to contribute, sir."

"Your dedication has been recorded."

That shut him up.

Mostly.

Broussard allowed himself a moment before continuing.

"Yes. Your rest cycle has been canceled."

The groaning became audible.

He lifted one hand.

"I'm not going to stand here and pretend you should be happy about it."

That quieted them faster than a reprimand would have.

"You were promised rest. You earned it. It's been taken away because convoy command needs a recovery detachment and apparently the Emperor has decided we looked underworked."

Someone muttered something theological.

Broussard ignored it.

"We will rest when we can. We rotate sentries. We sleep in shifts. Nobody attempts to prove they're immortal by staying awake for three days. If I find anyone deliberately skipping their assigned sleep period, I will personally remove them from duty and make them explain to me why exhaustion is somehow a useful contribution to combat readiness."

This was part of the method he had developed over the years.

Friendly hard-ass.

The Guard produced plenty of officers who believed discipline meant constant shouting.

Broussard had found that shouting was like artillery.

Useful occasionally.

If used continuously, people simply learned to live around it.

Instead he preferred making expectations painfully clear.

Then enforcing them.

"You work with me," he continued. "I work with you. We get the freighter moving, we rejoin the convoy, and we come home with the same number of people we left with."

There was no joking in his voice now.

His men knew this part.

They had heard versions of it before every serious deployment.

"We do it cleanly. We do it efficiently. If fortune is feeling uncharacteristically charitable, we do it under schedule and under budget."

Orro raised a hand.

"Under budget?"

Broussard nodded solemnly.

"I have ambitions."

"You feeling all right, sir?"

"Not particularly."

That drew a genuine laugh.

Good.

Then Broussard's expression settled.

"We have a reputation."

Most of the men knew exactly what he meant.

His command had come astonishingly close to surviving its service intact.

The core of the unit had been together since training.

There had been injuries.

Broken bones.

Burns.

Shrapnel.

A handful of ugly close calls.

A particularly regrettable incident involving a cargo crane.

Two men had spent nearly six months in rehabilitation after an armored personnel carrier decided to reverse while they were standing somewhere armor personnel should not stand.

But deployment after deployment, they had come back.

Not every Imperial Guard officer could say that.

Very few could.

Some would consider it evidence their unit had never fought hard enough.

Broussard considered those people idiots.

"The regiment likes to tell everyone we're reliable," he said. "Command likes to assign us jobs because we get them done. The Munitorum likes us because we usually return their equipment in recognizable condition."

A voice called, "Usually?"

Broussard looked toward Chimera Three.

"Do you want to discuss what happened to the left side of your turret on Brendis?"

Silence.

"Thought not."

He continued.

"So we're going to preserve that reputation. We recover the ship if we can. We recover the people regardless. We do not lose vehicles because someone was lazy with a maintenance check. We do not get personnel killed because somebody thought cold-weather procedure was optional. And nobody wanders away from the group to investigate strange noises under the ice."

Hessel raised his hand.

"No?"

"No."

"What if it's interesting?"

"Especially then."
