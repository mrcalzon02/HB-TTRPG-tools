# The Long Patrol of Broussard

## XV. THE GROUND THAT MOVES

**Author:** Mrcalzon02 / Christopher Vardeman

The warning came from a man assigned to watch a pole. Captain Broussard had given him no more glamorous work.

The recovery hauler held tension on its repaired frame. Sella had certified the three-metre movement test. Then the forward observer reported marker twenty-two leaning.

"How much?" Broussard asked.

"Ten degrees. It was vertical at the last survey."

"Did you see it move?"

"No, captain."

Broussard looked through the bridge glass. The marker was too far away to judge.

"Hold the test. No thrust."

Sella studied the load clock. The repaired brace had already warmed under tension.

"We don't get another thermal cycle for free," she said. "If I cool the brace, I inspect it again. If you leave us loaded too long, I inspect it anyway."

"How long?" Broussard asked.

"Twenty minutes before I want the load off."

"Then I have twenty minutes."

"That is not what I said."

Kelm reached for the observer's circuit. "Was anyone working near marker twenty-two?"

"Negative, captain."

"Did it lean when the hauler took tension?"

"I didn't see it happen."

"Good answer," Kelm said. He turned to Broussard. "Settling ice, another predator, or a fool with a vehicle. Which answer are you using?"

"The one we can measure."

Broussard sent a two-person survey team from the eastern drilled line, tethered and carrying a sounder. Gabriel pulled on his gloves.

"I'll go."

"You don't know how to drill ice."

"No. I know how to keep curiosity from becoming an expedition."

At seventeen minutes, the team chief called.

"Captain, the old drill hole is oval. The ice has moved sideways."

"Thickness?"

"One-point-six metres. Old reading was two-point-three."

"Same position?"

"As close as we can safely get."

Sella tapped the load clock. "Nineteen minutes."

Broussard keyed the hauler. "Reduce tension in stages. Engineering, inspect the brace again."

The recovery chief swore. The *Hesperant Vale* did not move.

For the moment, that was the correct result.

---

The survey chief returned with three samples and a complaint. The complaint concerned the time required to find another route. The samples explained why the old one could not be trusted.

He spread his notes across the command-shelter table.

The survey chief drew a line across the map. The ice had shifted laterally since the previous inspection.

"Twenty-two centimetres between the old reference holes," he said. "The survey measured thickness, not sideways movement. The straight route is no longer safe."

Kelm studied the new figures.

"Can we go around?"

"The eastern shelf may support us. I need more holes."

Sella looked at the map. "May is a terrible word to put beneath a ship."

"It's a useful word before the measurements exist."

Gabriel nodded toward the marker. "So nothing needed to eat it."

"No," said the chief.

"That will disappoint the men who want to shoot it."

Broussard studied the eastern shelf. The route curved away from the damaged ice before returning toward the convoy road.

The hauler would have to turn under load, and the freighter's outer brace had only recently been repaired.

"How much time?" he asked.

The survey chief estimated another five hours of work.

Kelm objected to the delay because the convoy had already spent days waiting. Fuel, escort crews, cargo refrigeration and the timetable were all real expenses. Broussard accepted them as expenses, not as reasons to put the ship across a moving pressure seam. Sella confirmed that the temporary environmental bypass would require another service interval. Kelm reassigned two cargo workers to help maintain it, and Broussard offered soldiers for supervised cargo handling.

Kelm signed the delay report himself. He did not want convoy command to believe that the soldiers had kept his vessel stranded against its master's judgment. The choice was shared, and so was the cost.

Broussard appreciated the distinction. It was easier to work with a captain who disputed a decision openly than one who agreed in person and protested in writing afterward.

---

The eastern survey took six hours. The first went into repositioning the Chimeras without crossing the old pressure line.

Chimera Two, with its broken vision block plated over and two outer track pads missing, took the protected inner station. Its commander objected to being treated like a fragile heirloom. Broussard reminded him that fragile heirlooms usually possessed greater resale value. The commander remained at his assigned post. The other vehicles covered the surveyed approaches without entering the fractured field. No one went to retrieve the old marker.

The next two hours went into drilling. Each new point was reached from ground already tested. The chief measured ice thickness and lateral displacement, recording both beside each marker.

The fourth hour went into rejecting a route that looked excellent on the map. It crossed thick ice, but the uneven surface would lift one side of the repaired tow frame. The recovery chief refused to certify that load, and Broussard accepted the refusal.

During the fifth hour, a crack closed on a sounding probe. The team lost the instrument but recovered its tether. Its chief reported the loss with the embarrassment of a man who expected to be charged for it.

Broussard asked whether any person had been injured. When told no, he ordered the probe recorded as lost and the survey continued. Supply could argue about the instrument once there was a supply officer close enough to hear them.

Gabriel helped the team reel in the remaining line. He checked the wrist of the man who had held the tether and did not offer a sermon.

By the sixth hour, they had a route. Not a beautiful one. A usable one. It curved east of the fracture field, passed between pressure ridges and returned toward the convoy approach over tested ice. The hauler could turn in stages. The freighter could follow under restricted thrust.

Broussard took the signed survey to the bridge.

Holt placed it beside the movement record.

"Does it replace the old route?"

"For movement. Keep the old readings."

"Even the wrong ones?"

"They weren't wrong when they were taken. We need to know what changed."

Holt entered both surveys under separate times. He had learned to preserve an uncomfortable record without treating it as an accusation. The skill had not made him any more comfortable under Broussard's attention.

"And the impact recorder?" Broussard asked.

"Current recording is complete."

"The old interval?"

"Still missing."

"Keep it preserved."

Fourteen seconds remained absent. They had not caused the moving ice. They had not been explained by it.

Broussard left them alone.

---

The second movement began beneath a darker sky. Sella inspected the outer brace again and delayed everyone while a fitter replaced a heat-stressed clamp that had passed the morning inspection but no longer satisfied her.

The recovery chief stood beside the hauler with his hands in his pockets.

"We'll run out of clamps," he said.

"Then we'll have fewer reasons to trust the old ones," Sella replied.

"That isn't how stores work."

"It's how hulls work."

She tested the fastening, signed the work order and returned to the bridge.

Kelm's cargo teams had secured the forward hold, but the refrigeration plant required a service interval after the first leg. Two cargo hands were assigned to the bypass while soldiers carried restraints under the cargo master's orders. Nobody improvised a shortcut through the damaged service junction. The men who had died there were not an excuse for careless work, and their absence had left fewer experienced hands to perform it.

The compromise was written into the movement plan.

Broussard liked seeing compromises written down. It meant someone would have to admit they existed later.

The recovery chief reported that the first three-metre test had already passed.

"Not on this line," Sella said.

The hauler drew tension. The frame held. The drive plant rose through its restricted load sequence.

The *Hesperant Vale* moved three metres.

Stopped.

Inspection.

The freighter rested on the new line, still so close to its starting position that a man could have walked the distance in a few steps. The inspection reports came back green.

"Ten," Sella said.

The hauler's tracks turned slowly. The repaired frame took the load. Ten metres passed beneath the ship.

Stopped.

Another inspection.

One report took longer. A starboard auxiliary mount had developed a new vibration. Sella sent a fitter to check its fastening before she would allow the next stage.

Broussard watched the work lights move beneath the hull.

Kelm did not ask how long it would take. Neither did Broussard.

The fitter found a loosened fastening and corrected it. No structural movement was observed. Sella inspected the report herself.

"Fifty metres next," she said. "Two pauses if I ask for them."

The recovery chief acknowledged.

"Not after something breaks. When I ask."

The ship moved. At twenty metres, the hauler slowed for the eastern turn. The tow line changed angle a fraction. The recovery chief called for reduced thrust and Sella gave it. At thirty, the ship crossed the point where the abandoned straight route would have led them toward marker twenty-two. The old marker was still leaning. They passed well east of it.

At fifty metres, Sella ordered another stop and inspection. The frame held; the convoy was still out of sight. Broussard approved the next stage only after the bypass service. The ship had begun to move. It still had to survive the journey.
