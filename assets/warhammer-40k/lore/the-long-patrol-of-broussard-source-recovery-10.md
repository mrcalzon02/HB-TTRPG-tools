# The Long Patrol of Broussard

## X. THE FORWARD HOLDS

**Author:** Mrcalzon02 / Christopher Vardeman  
**Based on the work of:** *The Long Patrol of Broussard*

The problem began as a name.


Then another.


Then a third.


Sella was speaking with Holt near one of the cargo-control stations when Broussard saw the change in her face.


Not panic.


Inventory.


The expression of someone mentally counting people and discovering that the number refused to balance.


"Where's Jerrik?"


Holt looked up from the slate in his hands.


"What?"


"Jerrik. And the two with him. They went forward."


Holt frowned.


"Inspection sweep."


"Hours ago."


That was enough.


Broussard crossed the compartment.


"Who?"


Holt looked toward him.


"Three cargo crew. Jerrik Noll, Edren Vale, Pallas Dorn. They were sent to inspect the forward bulk holds for secondary damage."


"When did they check in last?"


Holt looked at the chronometer.


His face tightened.


"They haven't."


"How long?"


"Two hours. Maybe more."


Gabriel appeared beside Broussard without seeming to have crossed the intervening distance.


"Vox?"


"Unreliable forward," Holt said. "The surge burned out repeaters in two sections."


"Unreliable vox explains silence," Broussard said. "It doesn't explain feet failing to come back."


Holt swallowed.


"No, sir."


"Route."


Holt brought up the cargo schematic.


The forward holds occupied an enormous portion of the Hesperant Vale's bow.


Not containerized freight.


Not crates.


Loose agricultural bins.


The scale took Broussard a moment to understand.


Each was closer to a grain silo laid on its side than an ordinary cargo compartment: cavernous partitioned holds designed to receive bulk produce directly from loading conveyors.


Potatoes.


Root vegetables.


Hard winter fruit.


Cabbages.


Dense squash.


Onions.


Feed stock.


Produce by the hundreds of tonnes, loaded loose into enormous bins and held by internal baffles and restraint systems.


Under normal operation the system was efficient.


Open the lower gates at destination.


Run the conveyors.


Empty the ship.


After a collision, it became something else.


"Cargo shift?" Broussard asked.


Sella nodded.


"Possible. The bins are baffled, but if any internal partitions failed..."


She did not finish.


She did not need to.


A mass of loose produce that size did not behave like cargo.


It behaved like terrain.


A wall of root vegetables several meters deep could crush a man.


A shifting slope could bury him.


A breached lower gate could empty hundreds of tonnes into a passageway with all the dignity and mercy of a landslide.


And because the forward hull had taken the initial impact, every one of those compartments had experienced the full violence of the collision.


Broussard studied the schematic.


"Any of these bins punctured to exterior?"


"Not according to pressure readings."


"Internal breach?"


"Unknown."


"Temperature?"


"Falling. Forward environmental control is intermittent."


"So best case, they're stuck."


Holt nodded.


"And worst?"


No one answered.


Broussard looked at Gabriel.


The commissar's earlier affability was gone.


Not replaced by zeal.


By focus.


"How many access routes?"


Sella pointed.


"Three from the mid-cargo spine. One maintenance crawl. One lower conveyor service way, but I wouldn't use it."


"Why?"


"If a bin gate failed, that's where the contents would go."


Broussard nodded.


"Then we're not crawling under several hundred tonnes of vegetables to prove we're brave."


Hessel's voice came over the squad vox from outside.


"Sir, did you just say vegetables?"


"Yes."


A pause.


"Do we have hostile vegetables now?"


Broussard closed his eyes.


"Not yet, Hessel."


"Understood."


Gabriel's mouth twitched.


Broussard pointed at the schematic.


"Get me two ship crew who know those holds. My men provide the recovery team and security. Nobody goes alone. We mark every junction, keep hard-line vox relays behind us, and if we see evidence of shifting cargo we stop before entering."


Sella was already gathering tools.


"I'm coming."


Kelm's voice came from behind them.


"So am I."


Broussard turned.


The captain stood in the hatch.


"No."


Kelm's expression hardened.


"They're my crew."


"And this is my rescue operation."


"They know me."


"They need someone who knows where not to stand under a damaged bulkhead."


"I know my ship better than anyone here."


Broussard considered him for a moment.


That, unfortunately, was true.


"Fine. You come as far as the cargo spine. You do not enter a damaged bin without my order."


Kelm opened his mouth.


Broussard raised one finger.


"That was not the opening bid."


Kelm shut it.


Gabriel looked faintly pleased.


"Captain," he said, "I believe that was the friendly version."


"It was."


Broussard looked again at the map.


Three missing crew.


Several immense loose-bin holds.


Damaged forward structure.


Unreliable communications.


A ship with fourteen seconds missing from its memory.


He felt the same quiet instinct waking again.


Perhaps the men were trapped.


Perhaps a cargo wall had collapsed.


Perhaps a hatch had jammed.


Perhaps they had simply lost the route in a darkened section and were currently swearing at one another somewhere forward.


He would have preferred any of those explanations.


Because beneath them all sat the one fact he could not make comfortable:


Three experienced crewmen had gone into the forward holds.


None had come back.


Broussard folded the schematic.


"Let's go find them."
