# Antegra Station

## Continuation VI — The Knock

The third knock was quieter.

That frightened Governor Talbor Varik more than the first two.

The first had shaken dust from Docking Reliquary Three. The second had made ancient clamps groan in the walls. Those could be understood as force. Force was reassuring. Force had measurements, tolerances, repair estimates and, in the better parts of the Imperium, invoices.

The third knock sounded almost polite.

Legate-Inquisitor Aveline Rhyse looked at the deck.

“Do not answer that.”

Varik glanced at her.

“I had not intended to.”

The blindfolded girl beside Rhyse tilted her head.

“It knows you are here.”

Captain Ardan Krell kept his pistol trained on the floor, which was tactically useless but emotionally satisfying.

“What knows?”

The child smiled.

“The city.”

Krell looked at Varik.

“I preferred the extermination hearing.”

“We have not cancelled it.”

“Excellent. I was concerned the day lacked structure.”

Rhyse struck her cane against the deck once.

The girl flinched.

“Name,” Rhyse said.

“Lysa.”

“Full designation.”

“Witness Lysa Nineteen.”

Varik's expression hardened.

“Nineteen?”

Rhyse ignored him.

“Lysa. Tell me precisely what you perceive.”

The child's smile disappeared.

“They are standing at the door.”

“How many?”

“All of them.”

Nobody spoke for several seconds.

Krell finally cleared his throat.

“That is an inconvenient number.”

The chained adept opened his case.

Inside were twelve parchment rolls, three black data-slates, a silver hammer, and a pistol with a barrel wide enough to make the distinction between execution and excavation largely academic.

Varik nodded toward it.

“Administrative equipment?”

“Appeals procedure,” Rhyse said.

Varik liked her less now.

That was reassuring too.

---

Commissar-Keeper Dren Solvik met them outside Central Audit with his sidearm still attached.

Rhyse looked at the holster.

“You were instructed to surrender that.”

“I declined.”

“I recorded your refusal.”

“I assumed so.”

“You understand I could have you shot.”

Solvik considered this.

“Yes.”

“And?”

“You would need someone else to shoot me.”

Krell looked away before anyone noticed him smiling.

Rhyse studied Solvik for a moment, then continued walking.

“Keep it.”

Solvik fell into step beside Varik.

“I believe that went well.”

“By Antegra standards, exceptionally.”

Central Audit had become a war room for a conflict no one could identify. Thermal maps covered one wall. Population reconciliations covered another. The honest census occupied six cogitator banks and had already produced seventeen petitions from departments objecting to citizens they had successfully failed to notice for decades.

Selene Marr stood among the cables with her sleeves rolled up.

She saw the blindfolded child and stopped.

“No.”

Rhyse raised an eyebrow.

Marr pointed at the girl.

“No children in Central Audit.”

Varik stared at her.

“Selene.”

“We have exposed power trunks, unsecured archival stacks, three malfunctioning servitors and a ceiling panel that has been waiting nine years for a replacement fastener.”

Rhyse said, “She has survived direct proximity to a daemonic manifestation.”

Marr folded her arms.

“The ceiling panel does not care.”

Varik gestured toward Marr.

“Legate-Inquisitor, Senior Auditor Selene Marr.”

“I gathered.”

Lysa whispered, “She is right about the ceiling.”

A panel fell behind them.

Nobody moved.

Marr looked vindicated.

Rhyse looked irritated.

Varik felt, for the first time since the knocking began, that the universe remained comprehensible.

---

Halbrecht Senn was waiting beside the sealed access to Substructure XVIII.

He had aged visibly in twelve hours.

At his age this required effort.

When he saw Rhyse, he bowed his head.

“Aveline.”

“Halbrecht.”

“You survived.”

“So did you.”

“Debatable.”

Rhyse's gaze moved over his artificial limbs.

“You always were dramatic.”

“I have less original material with which to express myself.”

The exchange was too familiar for strangers and too cold for friends.

Varik watched them carefully.

“You were both at the first review.”

Rhyse nodded.

“Along with Interrogator Pell, Magos Yevan, Confessor Thale, and your predecessor in this room.”

“My predecessor?”

Senn closed his eyes.

Rhyse looked directly at Varik.

“You.”

The word settled into the corridor.

Varik's recovered memories stirred.

Violet sky.

Seven moons.

White towers.

People waiting beneath them.

A black doorway.

His own hand on a lever.

He leaned against the wall.

Krell moved closer but did not touch him.

Rhyse continued.

“Sixty-eight years ago you were not merely governor of Antegra. You were the principal civilian investigator attached to the Severance commission.”

“I founded the Department of Necessary Omissions.”

“Yes.”

“I ordered my memories excised.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

Rhyse looked at Lysa.

The girl was crying beneath the blindfold.

“Because you discovered the contamination was not spreading from the vault.”

Senn whispered, “Aveline.”

“He needs to know.”

“He decided he did not.”

“And his decision failed.”

Varik straightened.

“From where was it spreading?”

Rhyse's cane tapped once against the floor.

“From you.”

---

Nobody shot Varik.

He considered this an encouraging opening to the discussion.

Solvik did unfasten the retaining strap on his pistol.

Varik considered that reasonable.

Krell did not move at all.

That was less reasonable and considerably more dangerous.

Rhyse placed one of the black data-slates on the table.

“Your first exposure occurred before the vault was opened. Three months before.”

“That is impossible.”

“Yes.”

Varik waited.

Rhyse did not elaborate.

He frowned.

“You cannot simply agree.”

“I am an Inquisitor. It is one of the few luxuries of the office.”

Marr leaned over the slate.

The record was old. Varik recognized his own face, younger only in the subtle ways rejuvenat treatments could not quite erase. He was standing in a survey chamber beneath Antegra.

Beside him was a wall.

Not a door.

Not machinery.

A blank wall.

The recording showed Varik turn toward it.

He spoke.

The audio had been removed.

Then he stepped backward and ordered the chamber evacuated.

“What did I say?”

Rhyse's expression tightened.

“We destroyed the sound.”

“Why?”

“Because everyone who heard it dreamed of the city.”

Marr stopped touching the slate.

Krell swore softly.

Solvik drew his pistol.

He did not point it at Varik.

Not yet.

Varik stared at the silent image of himself.

“What was behind the wall?”

“Nothing.”

“That answer has become unpopular today.”

Rhyse nodded.

“We dismantled it. Solid structural composite. No cavity. No mechanism. No anomalous material.”

“Then why did I speak to it?”

Lysa answered.

“Because it spoke first.”

The lights dimmed.

Far below, the fourth knock sounded.

This time Central Audit answered.

Every data-slate in the chamber illuminated at once.

The honest census vanished.

Eight million four hundred twelve thousand six hundred nine citizens disappeared from the screens.

In their place appeared a single line of text.

WELCOME BACK, TALBOR.

Marr stared at it.

“Well.”

Varik looked at her.

“Well?”

“I was going to complain about unauthorized access.”

“And?”

“I am revising the wording.”

A second line appeared.

YOU LEFT US WAITING.

Krell raised his pistol toward the nearest cogitator.

Rhyse said, “Do not damage the terminals.”

“It is talking through them.”

“Yes.”

“That seems an excellent reason to damage them.”

“We tried that sixty-eight years ago.”

“And?”

“It continued through the station clocks.”

Krell lowered the weapon slightly.

“I hate this place.”

Varik almost smiled.

“You requested the posting.”

“I was younger.”

“You arrived nine years ago.”

“I was much younger.”

A third line appeared.

OPEN THE DOOR.

Rhyse looked at Varik.

“Do you remember the door?”

He did.

That was the problem.

He remembered white stone beneath his hands.

He remembered seven moons reflected in black glass.

He remembered people waiting in absolute silence.

And behind the memory, older than the city and somehow closer, he remembered Antegra before Antegra had been built.

A bare asteroid.

Survey lights.

A wall where no wall belonged.

His own voice saying words nobody had permitted him to retain.

Varik gripped the table until his fingers hurt.

“Yes.”

Lysa began to scream.

Not loudly.

That made it worse.

She screamed like someone trying not to wake a sleeping parent.

Rhyse seized her shoulders.

“What do you see?”

“The people.”

“What are they doing?”

“They stopped waiting.”

Deep beneath them came a sound of stone moving against stone.

Substructure XVIII reported a pressure loss.

Then another.

Then seventeen compartments simultaneously changed status from SEALED to OPEN.

Marr went white.

“There are no seventeen compartments.”

Senn laughed.

It was not a pleasant sound.

“There are now.”

Varik looked at the access map.

A new corridor was drawing itself beneath the station.

It extended downward beyond the surveyed foundations, beyond the old mining shafts, beyond the physical dimensions of the asteroid.

At its end was a single chamber.

The system labeled it GOVERNOR'S OFFICE.

Krell stared at Varik.

“My lord.”

“Yes.”

“I believe someone is mocking you.”

Varik drew his father's saber.

The old blade caught the red emergency light.

“Then at least we have established it possesses intelligence.”

Rhyse opened the chained adept's case and took out the silver hammer.

Solvik checked his pistol.

Marr collected three data-slates and, after consideration, a maintenance pry-bar.

Krell noticed.

“You are coming?”

“Someone has to document why this was a terrible idea.”

Varik looked at the impossible corridor forming beneath Antegra.

For sixty-eight years he had governed a station designed to forget.

Now something remembered him.

And, with the impeccable timing characteristic of all truly malignant institutions, it wanted an appointment.

“Very well,” he said. “Let us not keep the public waiting.”
