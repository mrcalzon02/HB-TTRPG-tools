## Chapter V — The Hours Between

The infirmaria's morning roster had six names on it and work for eight.

Arbentia counted the names again, as if one of them might have appeared between the lines while she was looking elsewhere. One sister had been sent to the lower docks with the emergency dressing cart. Another had finished a double watch and was sleeping under an order Bethany herself had signed. The porter who normally moved patients between the observation beds and the testing alcove was helping with a lift whose doors had stopped agreeing with the deck indicator.

The list was accurate. That was its most discouraging quality.

Bethany found Arbentia with the stamped scriptorum rule and the duty slate laid side by side.

"You have made a petition," she said.

"I have made the beginning of one."

"The difference?"

"Two signatures and a woman to do my work."

Bethany looked at the slate. She had already seen the scriptorum rule. Arbentia was grateful that she did not ask to have it read aloud again.

"You can have a signature for the work you want to do," Bethany said. "You cannot have a woman who does not exist."

"There is an overlap at the change of watch."

"There is an overlap on paper. The outgoing staff are still dressing the men the incoming staff must examine."

Arbentia knew that. She had counted the dressings as well as the names. She was not asking Bethany to pretend otherwise.

"Could we move the routine graft review to the following watch?"

"Which review?"

"Three stable patients. Their last dressings were clean."

"And the patient with the exposed tendon?"

"Not his."

Bethany put a finger on the slate, moved two appointments, and stopped.

"That buys forty minutes. Not two hours."

"It buys forty minutes without leaving anybody alone."

"It does," Bethany said. "And if I put you in the archive, someone else must answer the alarm in the interval. I will not borrow the Emperor's mercy against an empty bed."

She returned the slate. The revised appointments remained on it. Arbentia had been refused the larger request, but not the smaller improvement. It was an inconvenient distinction to carry away.

---

Beren Holt came in before the revised graft round, moving on his own feet because the chair had been sent to a patient who could not use his legs at all.

He reached the observation bench, sat, and spent the next several minutes trying to make his breathing sound ordinary.

Arbentia did not congratulate him on walking. She brought him water and waited for the tremor in his hands to ease.

"Xanadu says I'm to be examined for light duty," he said.

"Who said that?"

"The berth clerk. He has a paper."

"Did he bring it?"

"He said it was for the company."

"Then it is not an examination order for this ward."

Holt gave a small, tired laugh. The sound hurt him.

He had brought his own copy of the previous medical restriction, folded so often that the creases crossed the seal. He had been told the convalescent berth would continue while the compensation claim was examined. He had also been told its cost might be entered against his future allowance. He could not tell whether the two statements came from the same office.

Arbentia took his exertion readings with the same rest interval Abraxas had insisted upon. The first figures were better than the previous review. The next were not. Holt's breathing recovered slowly after the short walk, and the improvement vanished when the test was repeated before he had fully rested.

"Have I got worse?" he asked.

"We have learned that you need longer between efforts."

"That is a polite way of saying I am no use to them."

"That is a medical finding about your lungs. What your company thinks you are worth is a different question."

He looked at the folded restriction in his hand.

"If they stop paying for the berth, I cannot pay for it."

She had no lawful means to promise otherwise. She could keep him under observation until his breathing steadied, record the measurements, and make certain the next person who assessed his fitness did not mistake a walk between two rooms for a shift beside a salvage lock.

The ward's printed follow-up table ended at discharge. Arbentia wanted the older cohorts precisely because the questions now facing Holt began where that table stopped. She had no figures with which to say how often a patient with his pattern recovered sufficient reserve, or what interval between assessments had been found safe. She refused to invent them.

"Will you let us send the company the restriction, without your private examination notes?" she asked.

"Will it say I can work?"

"No."

"Then yes."

He signed. She recorded what he had consented to and what he had not.

The company would receive a limited fitness notice. It would not receive a convenient medical opinion about the cause of the failed fitting, which the ward had never examined.

---

Abraxas read Arbentia's sponsorship request beside the analyzer while its cooling fan rattled against a worn bearing.

He did not immediately refuse it. This was more unnerving than a refusal.

"Your question is narrow," he said.

"I thought you would prefer that."

"I do. The material is not narrow. The cohort includes failed grafts, late pulmonary scarring, incomplete exposure histories and observations made under several incompatible measurement standards."

"Then I need to know which measurements are comparable."

"Precisely."

He turned the paper over and marked three categories in a small, angular hand. Rest interval. Exertion load. Exposure class. He drew a line beneath them.

"Two hours will not make you an authority on the entire archive."

"I did not ask to become one."

"You asked for a reading period. That is different from asking to remove copies."

"I know."

"You may record anonymous comparative criteria. No patient names, no sealed company returns, no original charts taken from the annex. Your findings return to the ward for review before they alter treatment."

He paused. Arbentia waited.

"I will sponsor that request."

For a moment she could only look at him. His optical array had already returned to the analyzer.

"Thank you, Magos."

"I have not granted access."

"I know."

"And I will not surrender my scheduled clinical review to supervise you. The annex must supply its own custodian."

"That was in the rule."

"It remains in the rule."

He applied his medical seal. It was not a reward for aptitude, nor a concession to her impatience. It was permission to ask a better question at someone else's expense.

When Bethany saw the seal, she studied the three marked categories and nodded once.

"Now we must find the time," she said.

Abraxas made a sound through his augmitter that might have been agreement or a complaint about the fan.

---

The corridor lamps failed for eleven seconds while a patient was being brought back from imaging.

They did not fail entirely. The primary circuit dimmed, the emergency strips took the load, and the orderly pushing the bed stopped at the junction because the crossing doors had been left in their manual position. When the normal lights returned, the doors remained where they were.

The orderly waited for a maintenance worker. There was no worker at the junction. There was only the warning cover, the open secondary-feed holder and the tag Arbentia had copied the previous watch.

By the time the bed reached the infirmaria, the patient had missed the first place in the dressing queue. Nothing dramatic had happened to him. Another patient had been seen first, his dressing was late, and the orderly had lost time he was supposed to spend fetching sterilized trays.

Bethany signed the delay into the ward book.

"Did the emergency strips hold?" she asked.

"Yes," Arbentia said.

"Then write that as well."

The maintenance clerk arrived before the next meal bell. He had come because the ward had filed a service interruption against the tag, not because anyone had discovered a way to manufacture the missing part.

"The matched fuse is on a supply issue," he said. "The bin has contacts. It does not have the rated insert."

"Can you fit one from the other corridor?" Arbentia asked.

"I could fit something the same shape. I cannot certify it for this feed."

He was plainly tired of being asked to turn a dangerous substitution into a repair.

"Then do not," Bethany said.

He showed them the issue docket. A supply tender was due in the next service window; no one had certified that its manifest included the insert. He could assign a manual watch to the crossing doors during patient movements, but that worker would be taken from another queue.

Bethany authorized the watch for transfers involving beds or oxygen carts, not a permanent guard at an empty corridor. Arbentia marked the ward schedule so that routine imaging journeys would not be booked across the junction during the worst shift overlap.

The measures were imperfect. They were also measures the ward could take without pretending to own the station's maintenance stores.

The clerk copied the new delay report beneath the older tag. It had become more difficult to describe the fault as a harmless flicker. It had not become a catastrophe.

---

The scriptorum clerk examined the sponsorship seal, the revised duty slate and the request for a supervised reading period. He laid the three documents in a row and then found a fourth document that Arbentia had never seen.

"One trainee's allocation can be exchanged," he said. "Not cancelled. Exchanged."

"With whom?"

"With the trainee, with the training supervisor's approval. The displaced hours must be restored in the next cycle. I cannot simply give you time that belongs to somebody else."

Arbentia had imagined a clerk protecting his office for the pleasure of it. The young trainee who arrived half an hour later made the rule harder to despise.

He had waited two cycles for his own scheduled reading. He was learning to prepare surgical instruments for procedures he had never been allowed to observe. His supervisor had offered to exchange one session for an assisted dressing watch under Bethany, which would count toward a practical requirement he otherwise could not complete.

"I want the practical watch," he told Arbentia. "I don't want my other hour to disappear."

"It won't," the clerk said, with a sharpness suggesting that the point had been made to him more than once.

Bethany reviewed the proposed exchange. The trainee could assist with clean dressings under supervision; he could not replace an experienced sister on emergency response. That distinction ruined the original two-hour ward-coverage plan.

Arbentia looked again at the slate. The forty minutes they had saved in the morning could be joined to a shorter interval at the next change of watch, but only if Bethany remained available for the emergency bell and one of the outgoing sisters took a delayed meal.

"Ask her," Bethany said.

Arbentia did. The sister agreed to delay her meal once, not to make a habit of it. She wanted the arrangement written down because unpaid favors had a way of becoming permanent staffing assumptions.

They signed the arrangement in the ward book. Bethany signed for the clinical risk she was accepting. The trainee's supervisor signed for the restored study hour. The clerk signed for a conditional two-hour reading period in the next cycle, with the annex custodian present and the roster subject to confirmation before the door opened.

It was the first affirmative scriptorum allocation Arbentia had held in her hands.

It was not access yet.

The clerk returned the papers to her in a folder and asked whether she wanted a receipt.

"Very much," she said.

He gave her two.

---

Holt's limited fitness notice went out by ordinary ward dispatch. The Xanadu clerk who collected it asked whether it could include a statement that the original accident was work-related.

"No," Arbentia said. "That belongs to the incident inquiry."

"Then compensation will wait."

"It may."

The clerk frowned at the form. He was not a villain. He was a man required to close an account without being given the authority to determine what had happened.

"Can you at least say when he will be fit?"

"I can give you the date of his next review."

"That isn't the same thing."

"No."

He took the notice and signed for it. The convalescent berth remained authorized for the present review interval; the question of charging its cost against Holt's allowance remained unresolved. Arbentia made the clerk repeat that distinction before she let him go. She did not ask him to promise a favorable compensation decision.

Holt was asleep when she returned to the observation bay. His breathing was steadier. His hand lay open on the blanket, the damaged fingers stiff beneath their dressing. A porter had left a small paper packet of biscuits beside the bed. Holt had not opened it.

Abraxas reviewed the new exertion measurements and accepted the restricted notice. He did not claim the historical cohorts would settle Holt's case. Bethany checked the grafts and asked whether the patient had been told the compensation question remained open.

"He has," Arbentia said.

"Good. Then do not let anyone tell him we have decided it for him."

At the crossing doors the manual watch helped another patient through without delay. The warning cover was still open. The matched fuse was still absent.

Arbentia entered both facts in the same book.

---

Her quarters were warm when she returned, though the washroom pipes had resumed their five-beat knocking.

There was a message from Rix. His tug had completed the first transfer without losing its cargo. The spare release equipment had been borrowed from another berth, and the resulting charge would be entered against the job's allowance until the companies settled who had ordered it. He had not seen Holt's fitting. The separate crew statements were still with the dock office.

**I DIDN'T BRING YOU A SECRET PAPER,** he had written. **I MAY HAVE FOUND A REPLACEMENT KNOB FOR THE RECEIVER. THAT IS MUCH LESS LIKELY TO GET US BOTH FIRED.**

Arbentia smiled, then found she was too tired to laugh.

She wrote that Holt was alive, still restricted, and not yet certain who would pay for his berth. She told Rix nothing from the private examination that Holt had not consented to share. She asked whether he had eaten and whether the equipment charge would leave him enough credit to do so tomorrow.

Then she set the terminal aside.

The conditional archive allocation lay beneath the water notice and above the three rejected petitions. It had cost a trainee a postponed hour, a sister a delayed meal, Bethany a narrow assumption of emergency coverage, Abraxas a professional seal, and the clerk the trouble of protecting everyone's claim in writing. It had not given Arbentia a single page of the old pulmonary tables.

Tomorrow she would check the roster again. If the ward became too busy, the allocation could still be deferred. If the annex custodian were called away, the door would remain shut. There were a dozen ordinary ways to lose two hours that no one had stolen.

She switched on the receiver. The viol came through beneath a hiss of static. This time the music held.

On the desk beside her, the stamped folder waited. The station had made room for her question, provisionally, by moving other people's hours around it.

It was a small opening. She intended to be ready when it came.
