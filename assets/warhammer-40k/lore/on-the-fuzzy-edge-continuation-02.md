# On the Fuzzy Edge

## Continuation II — The Unbroken Seal

*An original Cafarron Corridor chronicle continuation. Warhammer 40,000 and associated marks and setting elements are the property of Games Workshop. This non-commercial fan work is not endorsed by or affiliated with Games Workshop.*

Javard had been taught that an Inquisitorial seal possessed three useful properties.

It frightened the innocent.

It frightened the guilty.

And, in circumstances where nobody could decide which category they occupied, it frightened everyone equally.

The red wax across the cabinet lock performed admirably.

Sub-Dean Caldris had stopped breathing loudly.

The El Shaban factor behind Javard had developed the rigid expression of a man attempting to become part of the furniture.

Even the records servitor beneath the green lumen seemed to pull its parchment feeder more quietly.

Javard leaned closer.

The seal was old enough that the wax had darkened almost to brown. Dust had settled into the stamped skull-and-sunburst. Fine cracks radiated from the edges, but the central impression remained intact.

No name.

No office.

No date.

That was discourteous.

The Inquisition was rarely polite, but it was usually vain.

“Who placed it?” Javard asked.

Caldris looked at the cabinet.

Then at the rosette on the desk.

Then, with the air of a man choosing between two cliffs, back at the cabinet.

“I do not know.”

“That answer is becoming popular.”

“It is accurate.”

“Popularity remains suspicious.”

Caldris swallowed.

“The seal predates my appointment.”

“How long?”

“Seventeen years.”

Javard did not move.

The bell for Montpclair continued somewhere beyond the records chamber.

One toll.

Pause.

Another.

Medicae summons.

Still alive, then.

Or the bell-ringer had not been informed.

Both were plausible.

“Seventeen years,” Javard repeated.

Caldris nodded.

“The night the lighter returned.”

The factor behind Javard made a small sound.

Javard did not turn.

He had explained, repeatedly, that surprise was a private activity.

“Before or after the fourth passenger entered the cathedral?”

Caldris hesitated.

“After.”

“Who sealed it?”

“I was a junior copyist.”

“That was not the question.”

“I did not see.”

“Who did?”

Caldris’s brass fingers clicked against one another.

“The Canon Archivist.”

“Name?”

“Ephram Sol.”

“Alive?”

“No.”

“How did he die?”

Caldris looked uncomfortable.

Javard had noticed that questions about death divided Imperial citizens into two broad classes: those who became uncomfortable because the answer was tragic, and those who became uncomfortable because the answer was administrative.

Caldris was very clearly in the second.

“Officially?”

Javard sighed.

“Let us begin there.”

“Apoplexy.”

“And unofficially?”

“Also apoplexy.”

“That is unexpectedly tidy.”

“He suffered it after three days without sleep while answering questions from an Inquisitorial delegation.”

There it was.

Javard straightened.

“Names?”

“None recorded.”

“Ship?”

“None recorded.”

“Insignia?”

“The rosette.”

“Which rosette?”

Caldris looked at the one on the desk.

“Not that one.”

This was, Javard reflected, technically useful.

It eliminated one object from a galaxy containing several trillion alternatives.

He picked up his own rosette and brought it near the wax.

The tiny verification plate embedded beneath the sunburst woke with a reluctant amber glow. It scanned the seal.

For six seconds nothing happened.

Then it displayed:

AUTHORITY RECOGNIZED.

Javard waited.

A second line appeared.

AUTHORITY UNRESOLVED.

A third.

DO NOT COUNTERMAND.

The factor whispered, “Is that bad?”

Javard looked at him.

The man immediately discovered an urgent interest in the ceiling.

Caldris’s augmetic fingers flexed.

“What does it mean?”

“It means someone with valid authority placed the seal.”

“Whose authority?”

“Yes.”

Caldris frowned.

“That does not answer the question.”

“It answered the machine’s question. Machines enjoy a simpler life.”

The rosette blinked again.

ARCHIVAL AUTHORITY: PRIOR.

Javard’s expression did not change.

Internally, several very patient instincts stood up.

Prior.

Not expired.

Not superseded.

Not named.

Prior.

The word was bureaucratically elegant. It acknowledged that some authority had existed without committing the present system to the dangerous proposition of identifying who had held it.

It was exactly the sort of terminology produced when someone important wanted future readers to know that a thing had been deliberate while ensuring nobody could prove whose deliberate thing it had been.

Javard knew the technique.

He used it himself.

“Can you open it?” Caldris asked.

“Yes.”

The old clerk visibly disliked the speed of the answer.

“Should you?”

“No.”

The factor looked relieved.

Javard continued examining the cabinet.

Iron front.

Oak frame beneath.

Three hinge straps on the right.

The seal crossed the lock plate on the left.

He crouched.

The lower hinge had been repaired at some point with two mismatched bolts.

He smiled faintly.

Caldris saw the smile and became alarmed.

“No.”

“I have not said anything.”

“You looked at the hinges.”

“The hinges are structurally interesting.”

“No.”

“Sub-Dean, the seal prohibits opening the lock.”

“It seals the cabinet.”

“It seals the lock.”

“It is on the cabinet.”

“The Emperor is on the Throne. We do not therefore describe Terra as upholstery.”

Caldris stared at him.

Javard pointed to the hinge.

“The seal is intact. I have neither authority nor intention to break it.”

“You intend to remove the door.”

“I intend to remove the hinges.”

“That removes the door.”

“A regrettable secondary consequence.”

The El Shaban factor coughed into his fist.

Javard suspected laughter.

He approved, conditionally.

Caldris planted his human hand flat on the cabinet.

“This is an Inquisitorial seal.”

“So is this.”

Javard tapped his rosette.

“That does not make the cabinet yours.”

“No. But it does make the consequences of refusing me much more tedious.”

The sub-dean’s face tightened.

Outside, Montpclair’s bell stopped.

The silence that followed was worse.

Javard glanced toward the door.

Caldris did the same.

Neither commented.

The records servitor pulled another strip of parchment into its reader.

Scratch.

Click.

Scratch.

Click.

The sound was suddenly very loud.

Javard lowered his voice.

“Seventeen years ago, a ship returned without registration. Three men went out to meet it. Four returned. An unnamed Inquisitorial delegation arrived. Your archivist spent three days answering questions, then died. The relevant cabinet was sealed under valid authority whose identity the current system refuses to resolve.”

Caldris looked old.

Older than he had ten minutes before.

Javard continued.

“I am not asking whether this cabinet contains something dangerous.”

“No?”

“No. That would be optimistic.”

“What are you asking?”

“Whether the danger is inside.”

Caldris’s gaze moved toward the servitor.

Only briefly.

That was enough.

Javard turned his head.

The servitor sat beneath the green lumen.

Its body had once belonged to a large man. Age and surgery had reduced it to an arrangement of bone, cables, scar tissue and institutional purpose. Both legs ended beneath the desk in permanent mounting brackets. One eye had been replaced by a brass optical assembly. The other had been sewn shut.

Its parchment feeder clicked.

Scratch.

Click.

Javard looked back at Caldris.

“No.”

Caldris said nothing.

“No,” Javard repeated.

The brass fingers curled.

“His designation is Archive Servitor Nine.”

“I did not ask his designation.”

“No.”

“Who was he?”

Caldris’s silence became confession.

Javard walked to the servitor.

The machine-man did not acknowledge him.

A strip of parchment disappeared between rollers.

Letters appeared on a slate beside its hand.

INDEX COMPLETE.

FILE ROUTED.

NEXT.

Its remaining organic hand trembled once.

Not much.

Enough.

Javard had spent years around servitors. Most were less alive than doors and considerably less opinionated. The better-made ones retained habits. The badly made ones retained dreams.

The worst retained both.

“What was his name?” Javard asked.

Caldris answered behind him.

“Demas Vorn.”

The factor whispered something devotional.

Javard stared at the servitor.

“Lighter crew?”

“Yes.”

“One of the three?”

“Yes.”

“Which one?”

“Pilot.”

Javard looked at the old surgical scars around Vorn’s skull.

“Sentence?”

“Violation of sacred confidentiality.”

“By whom?”

“Ecclesiastical tribunal.”

“Convenient.”

“He confessed.”

“People frequently do.”

Caldris’s voice hardened.

“He begged for penance.”

Javard turned.

“People frequently do that as well. Especially when the alternative has already been explained.”

The sub-dean looked away.

Javard knew the shape of the process.

Confession.

Judgment.

Reduction.

A man could be converted into a tool with remarkable moral efficiency if enough prayers were recited during the cutting.

The Imperium did not waste useful material.

It merely changed the name on the inventory.

“What did he disclose?”

“Nothing recorded.”

“Of course.”

“The record is sealed.”

Javard looked at the cabinet.

Then at Vorn.

Then back at Caldris.

“You sealed the record beside the witness.”

“I did not.”

“Your institution did.”

“Yes.”

“And then kept the witness working in the room.”

Caldris’s expression became defensive.

“He cannot testify.”

“Officially?”

The old clerk did not answer.

Javard almost smiled.

There were times when doctrine became so cooperative it bordered on affection.

He crouched beside Archive Servitor Nine.

“Demas Vorn.”

No reaction.

“Pilot Vorn.”

Scratch.

Click.

“Cathedral lighter seven.”

The parchment stopped.

Caldris inhaled.

Javard did not look away from the servitor.

“Inner traffic ring.”

The brass optical lens tightened.

“Trade vessel Tides of Tomorrow.”

Vorn’s organic hand clenched.

The feeder rollers squealed.

A line printed itself across the slate.

NEXT.

“John Taggart Graveheart.”

The servitor screamed.

It did not sound human at first.

The vox unit in its throat had not been designed for volume. The note emerged as a shredded mechanical shriek, clipped at both ends, repeating faster than the speaker could reproduce it.

Caldris stumbled backward.

The factor swore.

Javard stayed where he was.

The servitor’s head snapped toward him.

Its sewn eye split open.

Not the eyelid.

The stitches.

Blood ran down the cheek.

A milky human eye stared from beneath.

“GRAVEHEART.”

The voice was wet now.

Organic.

Impossible.

“GRAVEHEART.”

The servitor tore one wrist free of its restraint.

Metal bent.

Skin tore.

The hand seized Javard’s coat.

The factor reached beneath his jacket.

Javard raised one finger.

Do not.

The factor froze.

Vorn pulled Javard close enough that old breath and machine oil touched his face.

“FOUR WENT OUT.”

Javard spoke softly.

“Yes.”

“FOUR CAME BACK.”

“Yes.”

“THREE CAME BACK.”

Javard felt his heartbeat change.

Not faster.

Narrower.

“What happened to the fourth?”

The servitor shook.

“THREE CAME BACK.”

Caldris whispered, “Emperor preserve us.”

Javard ignored him.

“Demas. What happened on the ship?”

The servitor’s grip tightened.

“FOUR WENT OUT.”

“Yes.”

“THREE CAME BACK.”

“You said four returned.”

The organic eye rolled toward the sealed cabinet.

“THE REGISTER SAID FOUR.”

Javard followed its gaze.

The iron cabinet seemed suddenly less like storage and more like an accusation.

“Who wrote the register?”

Vorn’s jaw worked.

No sound emerged.

Then:

“HE DID.”

“Who?”

The servitor convulsed.

Its free hand struck the desk hard enough to crack the slate.

“HE DID.”

“Name him.”

“NO NAME.”

“Describe him.”

“NO NAME.”

“Was he from the Tides of Tomorrow?”

The servitor went rigid.

“THE LETTER CAME FIRST.”

Javard glanced at Caldris.

The sub-dean had gone pale.

“What letter?”

Vorn’s head jerked toward a pigeonhole wall beyond the cabinet.

“GRAVEHEART.”

Javard stood slowly.

The old protocol.

Qual-2.

Beta Simplex.

Parchment.

Ordinary mail.

A phrase spoken.

A dead drop checked.

A letter bearing a citizen’s name that was not a citizen’s name at all.

John Taggart.

Graveheart.

The fourth man had not merely arrived under a false identity.

Someone had activated an Ecclesiastical intelligence channel before the lighter ever left the cathedral.

“Who spoke the trigger phrase?” Javard asked.

Vorn began to laugh.

It was a terrible sound.

Servitors were not supposed to laugh.

There were many things servitors were not supposed to do. Most of them became significantly more alarming when one did.

“PONTIFF.”

“Montpclair?”

The laughter stopped.

“OLD PONTIFF.”

“His predecessor.”

“OLD.”

“Did he send you to the ship?”

“YES.”

“Because of the letter?”

“YES.”

“Where is the letter now?”

The servitor’s bloody eye shifted to the sealed cabinet.

Caldris whispered, “No.”

Javard looked at him.

The sub-dean shook his head.

“No. The Graveheart drop was not in that cabinet.”

“Where?”

Caldris stared at the pigeonholes.

“The dead-drop correspondence was burned after reading.”

“Was it?”

“That is the procedure.”

Javard waited.

Caldris’s face collapsed by degrees.

“No,” the old clerk said.

There were few pleasures in inquisitorial work.

Hearing a witness finally choose truth over procedure was one of them.

It was usually temporary.

“Where?”

Caldris crossed to the pigeonhole wall.

He reached behind a wooden saint fixed between two shelves.

The saint represented some local martyr whose particular agony had been rendered in loving detail. His hands were missing, his ribs were open, and a brass halo had been nailed directly through his skull.

Javard considered the sculpture unnecessarily cheerful.

Caldris pressed one of the exposed ribs.

A compartment opened behind the saint.

Inside lay ash.

Nothing else.

The factor exhaled.

Caldris stared.

“No.”

Javard crouched beside the recess.

The ash was old, grey, undisturbed except for a small depression at the center.

Rectangular.

A letter had rested there after the fire.

Something had been placed on top of the ash.

Or removed from it.

“When was this last opened?”

Caldris did not answer.

Javard looked over his shoulder.

“Sub-Dean.”

“Seventeen years ago.”

“By?”

“Canon Sol.”

“The archivist.”

“Yes.”

“After the delegation arrived?”

“Yes.”

“What did he remove?”

“I do not know.”

“You have said that before.”

“This time I mean it.”

“That is rarely reassuring.”

Behind them, Vorn began striking his broken restraint against the desk.

Once.

Twice.

Three times.

Pause.

Once.

Twice.

Three times.

Javard stood.

“What is he doing?”

Caldris listened.

His expression changed.

“It is not random.”

“I had gathered.”

“It is a bell pattern.”

“Which?”

Caldris turned toward the servitor.

“Burial transfer.”

The room became colder.

Not literally.

Javard checked anyway.

The green lumen still burned.

The servitor hammered again.

Three strikes.

Pause.

Three.

“What burial?” Javard asked.

Vorn answered.

“SOL.”

Canon Ephram Sol.

The dead archivist.

Javard felt the investigation alter shape.

“Where was he buried?”

Caldris rubbed his human hand across his mouth.

“Cathedral ossuary.”

“Intact?”

“His remains were interred.”

“That was not the question.”

The sub-dean looked toward the door.

“After the tribunal, the Inquisitorial delegation ordered his body transferred.”

“Where?”

“I do not know.”

Javard’s eyes narrowed.

“Did anyone?”

“Montpclair’s predecessor.”

“Also dead?”

“Yes.”

“How?”

Caldris hesitated.

“Natural causes.”

Javard waited.

Caldris looked ashamed.

“Officially.”

“Excellent.”

The factor behind them muttered, “I am beginning to dislike that word.”

Javard glanced back.

“You are learning.”

Vorn hammered again.

Three.

Three.

Then his hand stopped.

The parchment feeder restarted by itself.

Scratch.

Click.

A strip emerged.

The servitor had not touched the controls.

Letters appeared.

BURIAL TRANSFER: EPHRAM SOL.

AUTHORITY: HOLY ORDOS.

DESTINATION: LOWER OSSUARY NINETEEN.

STATUS: PENDING.

DATE: SEVENTEEN YEARS PRIOR.

Javard read it twice.

“Pending?”

Caldris stepped closer.

“That cannot be.”

“Your institution appears to have developed a broad definition of cannot.”

“The lower ossuaries were sealed after the sump collapse.”

“When?”

“Twenty-two years ago.”

Five years before Sol died.

Javard looked at the strip again.

Destination: a sealed ossuary.

Transfer: ordered later.

Status: pending for seventeen years.

An Inquisitorial delegation had come.

An archivist had died.

His body had been ordered moved somewhere inaccessible.

The order had never completed.

And the cabinet containing the associated record remained sealed under authority the system described only as prior.

It was almost elegant.

Someone had hidden a body behind paperwork.

The Imperium usually did the reverse.

“Can the lower ossuary be reached?”

Caldris looked horrified.

“No.”

“Physically.”

“Oh.”

The old clerk thought.

“Possibly.”

“Better.”

“The access stair collapsed. The maintenance galleries intersect the old crypt level, but they were condemned after the sump breach. There is gas. Fungal bloom. Structural failure.”

“Anything dangerous?”

Caldris stared at him.

Javard waited.

The sub-dean realized.

“That was a joke.”

“Marginally.”

The factor’s expression suggested he was reconsidering his employment.

Javard picked up the burial strip.

The ink was fresh.

The order was seventeen years old.

The servitor was now motionless.

“Demas.”

No reaction.

“Pilot Vorn.”

Nothing.

“Graveheart.”

The servitor’s organic hand opened.

Inside its palm, beneath blood from the torn restraint, was a scar.

Not surgical.

Deliberate.

Three straight cuts crossing one another.

A crude symbol.

Javard had seen it before.

Not in person.

In a case file.

A marginal notation associated with Eckhart Black Cartel confiscations.

Not their official mark.

A reader’s mark.

A sign used in recovered texts to indicate a passage that had been disputed rather than condemned.

He touched nothing.

“Caldris.”

“Yes?”

“Did your tribunal put that on him?”

The sub-dean looked.

“No.”

“Did the Inquisition?”

“I do not know.”

“Did he have it before the lighter mission?”

Caldris stared at the scar.

“No.”

Javard believed him.

That was inconvenient.

The book had been on the Tides of Tomorrow.

Vorn had gone aboard.

Vorn had returned with a mark used by the book’s readers.

And somebody had spent seventeen years burying the route between those facts beneath seals, dead clergy and a transfer order to a crypt that had already been inaccessible for five years.

Javard looked back at the intact red wax.

He no longer wanted the cabinet opened.

Not yet.

The cabinet was evidence.

More importantly, it was a warning left by someone with sufficient authority to frighten the current authentication system.

There were situations in which curiosity was courage.

There were others in which curiosity was merely the fastest available route to becoming a cautionary memorandum.

Javard preferred memoranda in which he remained the author.

“Leave it sealed,” he said.

Caldris sagged with relief.

The factor blinked.

“We are not opening it?”

“No.”

“After all that?”

Javard folded the burial transfer and placed it inside his coat.

“We have acquired a better door.”

Caldris looked toward the chamber entrance.

“The ossuary.”

“Yes.”

“It is condemned.”

“So was the cartel.”

“That is not the same thing.”

“Not yet.”

The green lumen flickered.

Vorn’s brass eye rotated toward Javard.

The servitor spoke once more.

Quietly this time.

Almost like a man.

“Do not let him finish reading.”

Javard stopped.

Caldris whispered, “Who?”

Vorn’s eye closed.

The feeder printed one final line.

NEXT.

Then the servitor resumed its work.

Scratch.

Click.

Scratch.

Click.

As though nothing had happened.

Javard stood in the records chamber with a burial order seventeen years overdue, a sealed cabinet he had chosen not to violate, an old reader’s mark carved into the palm of a lobotomized witness, and a route into a condemned crypt beneath a cathedral whose pontiff had begun the morning by setting portions of his own head on fire.

He considered the day.

It was, by local standards, becoming productive.

“Get me a map of the maintenance galleries,” he told Caldris.

The sub-dean looked toward the door.

“And if Montpclair wakes?”

Javard picked up his rosette.

“Tell him the sermon was memorable.”

The bell began tolling again.

This time it was not the medicae pattern.

Caldris went still.

Javard listened.

Three strikes.

Pause.

Three strikes.

Burial transfer.

From somewhere below them, far beneath the cathedral floor, something struck back.
