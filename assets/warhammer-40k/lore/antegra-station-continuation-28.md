# Antegra Station

## Continuation XXVIII — The Class

*An original Cafarron Corridor chronicle continuation. Warhammer 40,000 and associated marks and setting elements are the property of Games Workshop. This non-commercial fan work is not endorsed by or affiliated with Games Workshop.*

By the time the first citizen refused, Antegra Station had printed eleven million forms.

This was impressive for two reasons.

The first was that Antegra contained considerably fewer than eleven million living people.

The second was that the station had run out of paper six minutes earlier.

Selene Marr regarded both facts as promising.

Captain Ardan Krell did not.

"Where is it getting the paper?"

A sheet slid from the Dock Fourteen printer and landed on his boot.

DO YOU ACCEPT?

YES.

NO.

DISPUTED.

Krell picked it up by one corner.

"That was not rhetorical."

Magos Vhal rotated three ocular lenses toward the printer.

"Material analysis indicates cellulose fibre, carbon pigment, binding resin, trace machine oil, human epidermal residue and approximately point-zero-zero-three percent unidentified particulate."

"Where is it getting the paper?"

"Unknown."

"Thank you."

"You are welcome."

Marr took the form from him.

The recipient field was blank.

The serial number was not.

7-R-00000000001.

"First one," she said.

Krell looked at the mountain of forms already filling three cargo pallets.

"That seems unlikely."

"It says first."

"The Imperium says many things."

"That is why we have courts."

Krell considered this.

"I had assumed courts existed to say more things."

"That too."

The brass hearing automaton stamped the table.

CLASS NOTIFICATION COMMENCED.

The station vox chimed.

Then every speaker on Antegra spoke with Marr's voice.

She had not recorded the announcement.

"All persons presently resident aboard Antegra Station, formerly resident aboard Antegra Station, potentially resident aboard Antegra Station, erased from Antegra Station, deceased while attached to Antegra Station, not yet born but materially affected by Antegra Station, or otherwise possessing a disputed interest in Case 7-OMNIA-NULL are hereby notified that you may accept, refuse, or dispute acceptance of the petition presently before the hearing."

Marr listened to herself pause.

"Failure to respond will not be construed as consent."

She looked sharply at the automaton.

It stamped.

CORRECT.

The vox continued.

"Any attempt by another party to answer on your behalf should be reported to Legal Annex Fourteen. Please do not shoot the forms. They appear to be self-replacing."

Rhyse lowered his pistol.

"That last instruction was unnecessary."

Marr looked at him.

"It was specifically for you."

The announcement ended.

For nine seconds, nothing happened.

Then Antegra Station became a democracy.

It lasted approximately fourteen minutes before everyone remembered why the Imperium generally avoided the practice.

The first queue formed outside Legal Annex Fourteen.

The second formed outside Chapel District Three because several thousand citizens wished to know whether saying NO to an unidentified nine-century petition constituted heresy.

The third formed outside the ration office because someone had spread a rumour that accepting the petition increased protein allotments.

The rumour was false.

This did not prevent six hundred people from accepting immediately.

The fourth queue had no obvious purpose at all, but by the time station security investigated it, seventeen clerks had been assigned to manage it and no one wished to risk disrupting an established process.

Marr moved through the station with Krell, Young Talbor Varik and Old Talbor Varik behind her.

The two Variks had each received forms.

Their serial numbers were identical.

Their answers were not.

Young Varik had checked DISPUTED.

Old Varik had checked NO.

Marr examined both.

"Why no?"

Old Varik shrugged.

"I have seen what happens tomorrow."

Young Varik looked at him.

"Have I?"

"Not yet."

"Was I there?"

"Yes."

"Did I survive?"

Old Varik thought about it.

"Define survive."

Young Varik checked DISPUTED again, harder.

The Collector arrived at thirteen minutes past the hour.

Not as a body.

As agreement.

Across Antegra, printers began producing identical forms.

Not merely identical in layout.

Identical in answer.

YES.

One after another.

Thousands.

Every station chronometer moved toward the same second.

Every public tally converged.

YES: 14,118.

NO: 8,902.

DISPUTED: 31,007.

Then:

YES: 19,604.

NO: 8,902.

DISPUTED: 31,007.

Then:

YES: 41,221.

NO: 8,902.

DISPUTED: 31,007.

Krell saw the tally on a corridor display.

"It's voting."

"No," Marr said. "It's answering."

"For whom?"

"Everyone."

The lights dimmed.

Black glass appeared in every viewport.

The Collector's hands pressed against the other side.

The station vox spoke with millions of voices compressed into one.

"ACCEPTED."

Marr stopped walking.

"Objection."

Nothing happened.

She looked toward the nearest printer.

"Objection."

The printer produced a YES form.

"Basis?" Krell asked.

"Class fraud."

The brass automaton was three decks away.

It stamped anyway.

The sound travelled through the hull.

BASIS REQUESTED.

Marr raised both Varik forms.

"Same serial number. Different answers."

The station trembled.

"If the Collector has reconciled the class into one recipient, these two members cannot simultaneously possess distinct valid responses. Yet the court has already preserved them as separate extant histories."

A pause.

The clocks hesitated.

Marr continued.

"Either the class remains disputed, in which case the Collector cannot answer collectively, or the court must first vacate its prior recognition of contradictory persons."

The automaton stamped.

PRIOR ORDER STANDS.

The black glass cracked.

The public tally flickered.

YES: 41,221.

YES: 19,604.

YES: 14,118.

YES: ERROR.

Then every counterfeit form caught fire.

This caused some excitement.

Station suppression systems deployed foam over three decks, two chapels and one very surprised grox butcher.

The genuine forms did not burn.

Krell watched a counterfeit curl into ash.

"How do we tell which are genuine?"

Marr held up Young Varik's.

"The genuine ones disagree."

"That is an appalling evidentiary standard."

"It is the first reliable one we have had."

The Collector withdrew from the viewports.

The clocks immediately resumed arguing.

One announced that it was yesterday.

Another rang thirteen times.

A third displayed a handwritten complaint.

Marr felt considerably better.

Then the dead began answering.

The first was Edrin Vale.

His form emerged from a maintenance terminal in a compartment he had been recorded as dying in forty-three years earlier.

NO.

A second form emerged beneath it.

The same name.

YES.

A third.

DISPUTED.

Vale himself arrived three minutes later carrying a wrench and looking offended.

"I only filled one out."

"Which?" Marr asked.

"No."

"Why?"

"Because anything that needs this much paperwork before telling me what I'm accepting is probably management."

Krell nodded solemnly.

"Sound doctrine."

Marr examined the other two forms.

Different dates.

One had been filed twenty-six years before Vale's birth.

The other seven years after his recorded death.

Both bore valid station seals.

"Class members from contradictory histories," she said.

The automaton stamped somewhere far away.

ADMITTED PROVISIONALLY.

The printers accelerated.

Forms came from children who had not been born.

Forms came from hab blocks demolished before Antegra was constructed.

Forms came from officers whose names appeared only on memorial plaques.

Forms came from three different station governors, two of whom had never held office and one of whom had been executed for holding it badly.

Forms arrived from ships that had docked at Antegra in histories no navigator remembered.

One came from a vessel listed as lost with all hands.

Its answer was NO.

A handwritten note beneath the box read:

WE REMAIN LOST. PLEASE STOP FORWARDING ADMINISTRATIVE CORRESPONDENCE TO THIS LOCATION.

Marr filed it.

By the second hour, the recipient count exceeded Antegra's highest recorded population.

By the third, it exceeded the population of every known Antegra history combined.

By the fourth, Vhal stopped attempting to count.

"The class is not bounded by population," the Magos said.

Marr looked up from a stack of forms.

"Affected persons."

"Correct. The petition's consequences propagate beyond station residency. Traders. Naval crews. Administratum personnel. Dependants. Descendants. Persons whose assignments changed because Antegra existed or failed to exist. Persons whose births depended upon those assignments."

Krell stared at the forms covering Dock Fourteen.

"How far does that go?"

Vhal's mechadendrites became very still.

"Potentially indefinitely."

The red maintenance door knocked once.

Respondent Prior stood beside it.

Its outline multiplied.

One figure became two.

Two became seven.

Seven became hundreds of faint alternatives occupying the same patch of deck.

Marr understood.

"Not everyone."

The alternatives settled.

Krell frowned.

"What?"

"The class cannot be everyone affected by Antegra forever. If it were, the petition could never be delivered."

The automaton stamped.

CORRECT.

"There must be a cutoff."

STAMP.

CORRECT.

"Where?"

The automaton did not answer.

Origin did.

The printer activated.

CLASS CLOSES AT FIRST REFUSAL.

Marr stared at the words.

"Which first refusal?"

A second sheet emerged.

THE FIRST.

"Chronologically?"

No response.

"Procedurally?"

No response.

"Causally?"

The printer shuddered.

Then produced:

DISPUTED.

Krell sighed.

"Of course."

Old Varik opened the first-hearing docket.

"There was a refusal."

Marr turned.

"At the first hearing?"

"Before it."

Young Varik stepped closer.

"Who refused?"

Old Varik looked toward the red door.

"I don't know. The record was already damaged when I saw it tomorrow."

"What did it say?"

Varik searched the pages.

He found one near the back.

It was blank except for a receipt line.

REFUSAL RECEIVED.

RECIPIENT NOT PRESENT.

DELIVERY INCOMPLETE.

Marr read it twice.

"That is impossible."

Krell looked at her.

"You have developed standards?"

"A refusal cannot be received from a recipient who is not present."

The automaton stamped.

OBJECTION NOTED.

Marr ignored it.

"Unless the refusal was filed by counsel."

Old Varik went pale.

"You said your client was absent."

"Tomorrow."

"Yes."

"And then?"

"Then you refused something."

The red door opened one finger's width.

Cold air moved through Dock Fourteen.

The forms nearest it lifted and fluttered.

Every NO response turned toward the gap.

Not physically.

The ink did.

Millions of handwritten marks, stamps, scratches and check-boxes pulled toward the door like compass needles.

Marr stepped back.

The printer began producing a new document.

COUNSEL PREPARATION COMPLETE.

"No," Marr said.

The printer continued.

CLASS IDENTIFIED.

"No."

FIRST REFUSAL LOCATED.

"Objection."

The automaton stamped.

BASIS?

Marr looked at Old Varik's impossible docket.

At the receipt from a hearing that had not yet happened.

At millions of citizens being asked to accept a petition no one could safely define.

At the red door waiting for her.

"Circular evidence. The first refusal cannot be used to define the class if the first refusal is produced by the class process itself."

The automaton's gears clicked.

Once.

Twice.

Then stopped.

Origin's printer went silent.

The red door remained open one finger's width.

The Collector appeared in the polished casing of the automaton.

Respondent Prior divided again.

The dead-letter darkness beyond the station perimeter leaned inward.

For the first time all three seemed to be waiting for the same answer.

The automaton stamped.

OBJECTION SUSTAINED.

Every form on Antegra changed.

The question remained.

DO YOU ACCEPT?

But beneath it, the boxes vanished.

YES.

NO.

DISPUTED.

Gone.

A new line appeared.

DEFINE WHAT YOU WERE ASKED.

Silence moved through Antegra Station.

Not ordinary silence.

The silence of millions of people discovering that the form had finally asked a sensible question.

Krell stared at his copy.

"What do I write?"

Marr looked at hers.

For the first time since the petition began, she did not know.

Then a fourth box appeared at the bottom of the page.

Not printed.

Handwritten.

In her handwriting.

I WAS NEVER ASKED.

The red door opened another centimetre.

Beyond it, someone took a seat.

The seventh chair creaked.

The automaton raised its stamp.

RECIPIENT PRESENT.

Marr looked through the narrow opening.

She could see only one hand resting on the table beyond.

Human.

Scarred.

Wearing no ring, no seal, no rank.

The hand turned over.

Written across the palm were four words.

DO NOT NAME ME YET.

Marr lowered her form.

"Fine," she said.

The Collector struck the door from the other side.

The entire station shook.

Marr smiled without humour.

"We can work with that."
