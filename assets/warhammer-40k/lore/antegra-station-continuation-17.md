# Antegra Station

## Continuation XVII — The Question of Standing

The thing beyond Dock Fourteen knocked a second time.

Selene Marr looked at the elderly adjudicator.

"Are we obliged to answer?"

"No."

Captain Ardan Krell exhaled.

"Good."

"You are obliged to acknowledge receipt."

Krell closed his eyes.

"Of course we are."

Marr took a receipt chit from the brass automaton, signed it, and pushed it beneath the outer hatch.

The paper vanished.

A moment later it returned stamped:

RECEIVED BY VENUE.

Magos Hester Vhal rotated two lenses toward the hatch.

"The exterior pressure remains zero."

"Then the venue has excellent clerical discipline," Marr said.

The elderly woman opened the second folder.

"We will proceed."

Young Talbor Varik stared at the photograph on the table. Old Varik had not looked away from it.

Seventy-one years ago.

Three years before the Antegra incident.

A younger version of the older man stood beside a woman who claimed they had met and a place nobody was permitted to name.

"I wasn't there," Old Varik said.

"That is not yet established," the adjudicator replied.

"I remember my life."

"So does the other one."

Young Varik stiffened.

The woman turned the photograph around.

On its reverse was a transit seal.

Marr recognized the structure immediately.

Not the authority. Not the destination.

The structure.

Transit-Null.

Her own impossible form, drafted less than an hour earlier.

Except this copy was seventy-one years old.

Krell said, "Marr."

"Yes."

"Tell me you didn't backdate it that far."

"I did not."

"Good."

"Old Varik backdated it sixty-eight years."

Krell stared at the ceiling.

"I withdraw the good."

The adjudicator slid another document across the desk.

DECLARATION OF CONTINUING EXISTENCE.

PETITIONER: ANTEGRA STATION.

DATE OF ORIGIN: DISPUTED.

Marr read the next line twice.

FIRST APPEARANCE: SEVENTY-ONE YEARS PRIOR.

Legate-Inquisitor Aveline Rhyse leaned forward.

"Antegra did not file anything seventy-one years ago."

"Perhaps," said the adjudicator.

"That is a fact."

"Facts are precisely what have made this proceeding dangerous."

The black windows brightened.

Hundreds of distant panes had become thousands.

Behind them were silhouettes.

Not people.

Possibilities of people.

Every time Marr tried to count them, the total changed.

Witness Lysa Nineteen whispered, "They're watching us decide who arrived first."

The brass automaton placed three red cords across the desk.

The first surrounded Young Varik's file.

The second surrounded Old Varik's.

The third surrounded Antegra Station's.

Then it tied all three together.

Vhal emitted a soft mechanical click.

"Identity graph convergence."

The adjudicator nodded.

"That is the problem."

Marr understood.

"If we prove which Varik is original, we give the Collector a reconciled identity."

"Yes."

"If we refuse to prove it, Antegra may lack standing."

"Yes."

"And if Antegra lacks standing, the injunction fails."

"Possibly."

Krell looked offended.

"Possibly?"

"Captain, certainty is the respondent's preferred weapon. We try not to issue ammunition."

For the first time, Rhyse smiled.

It was not a comforting expression.

The docket printer began chattering.

RESPONDENT MOTION:

DISMISS FOR LACK OF ORIGINAL PETITIONER.

Marr reached for the strip.

A second line appeared.

ASSERTED BASIS:

PETITIONER DID NOT EXIST WHEN CLAIM ORIGINATED.

Young Varik said, "Antegra existed."

Old Varik looked at the photograph.

"Maybe not this Antegra."

Nobody liked that sentence.

The elderly woman opened a third folder.

Inside were population rolls.

Antegra Station.

Seventy-one years ago.

The names were wrong.

Not corrupted.

Wrong.

Millions of people who had never lived there. Departments never built. Dock numbers extending far beyond the station's physical hull. A governor listed under the name Talbor Varik.

His age was given as forty-three.

Young Varik was forty-three.

Old Varik sat down.

"No."

Marr read the registry again.

"This is not our past."

"Correct," said the adjudicator.

"Then whose is it?"

"That question is inadmissible until standing is established."

Krell made a strangled noise.

"I am beginning to miss being shot at."

Rhyse looked at him.

"You say that because you have forgotten how often you complain while being shot at."

"Those complaints were operationally justified."

The outer hatch knocked a third time.

This time the docket answered before Marr could.

RESPONDENT PRESENTS EXHIBIT.

The hatch opened.

There was no decompression.

No void.

Beyond it stood Antegra Station.

Not the dock.

The whole station.

Seen from outside, hanging in blackness.

Except it was larger.

Older.

Its hull carried towers that had never been constructed and scars from wars nobody remembered. Around it drifted seven dead moons.

Young Varik stepped toward the threshold.

Old Varik seized his arm.

"Don't."

The younger man stopped.

Across the impossible distance, lights came on in the other Antegra.

One deck at a time.

A message formed in illuminated windows.

WE FILED FIRST.

Lysa Nineteen began crying.

"That's what the city was trying to tell us."

Marr did not look away.

"The dead city?"

Lysa shook her head.

"No."

The Collector's reflected shape appeared in every dark observation pane.

For once, it did nothing.

It simply waited.

The adjudicator's face had become very still.

Marr looked at her.

"You knew."

"We suspected."

"There is another Antegra."

"There is evidence asserting one."

"It has seven dead moons."

"So does your case."

Marr looked again at the impossible station.

The other Antegra's windows changed.

WE PETITIONED FOR PROTECTION.

Then:

PETITION DENIED.

Then:

WE APPEALED.

The final message took longer.

WE ARE STILL WAITING.

Old Varik whispered, "Seventy-one years."

The adjudicator corrected him.

"From your frame of reference."

The brass automaton untied the three red cords.

Then it produced a fourth.

It looped that cord around empty space.

The docket printed:

ADDITIONAL PETITIONER ASSERTED.

IDENTITY: DISPUTED.

STATUS: PRIOR.

The Collector moved.

Every reflection leaned toward the open hatch.

The other Antegra's lights flickered.

For the first time since the hearing began, the elderly adjudicator raised her voice.

"Respondent will remain where it is."

The Collector stopped.

Marr glanced at her.

"That sounded remarkably like a ruling."

"It was an instruction."

"Difference?"

"Survivability."

Fair enough.

Marr pulled a blank form toward herself.

"Then Antegra does not contest priority."

Young Varik looked at her.

"What?"

"We don't need to be the original petitioner."

The adjudicator watched her carefully.

Marr continued writing.

"We need to be an interested party to an unresolved petition."

Vhal's lenses widened.

Old Varik began to smile.

Krell pointed at him.

"Stop that. Every time one of you smiles, reality develops an administrative condition."

Marr signed the form.

MOTION TO CONSOLIDATE WITHOUT DETERMINATION OF IDENTITY.

The docket hesitated.

The Collector's reflections shuddered.

The other Antegra went dark.

Then the printer struck once.

MOTION RECEIVED.

A second strike.

RESPONDENT OBJECTION RECEIVED.

A third.

OBJECTION DEFERRED.

Krell laughed despite himself.

"We're back to deferred?"

"Captain," Marr said, "civilization is the art of making the end of the world wait its turn."

The open hatch began to close.

Before it sealed, one final light appeared aboard the other Antegra.

A single window.

A single room.

Inside stood a man.

Talbor Varik.

Not young.

Not old.

Older than both.

He lifted one hand.

Old Varik recoiled.

The hatch shut.

The docket printed a new notice.

CONSOLIDATED PETITION ACCEPTED FOR INTERIM REVIEW.

NUMBER OF PETITIONERS: THREE.

Marr stared at the number.

Young Varik said, "Three?"

Nobody answered.

The elderly adjudicator closed the folders.

"We will recess for seven minutes."

Rhyse looked toward the sealed hatch.

"Why seven?"

The woman stood.

"Because in eight minutes, one of you files the original petition."

Then she and the naval officer vanished.

The brass automaton remained behind.

It placed a blank form in front of Marr.

At the top was a date.

Seventy-one years ago.

At the bottom was a signature line.

SELENE MARR.

Marr regarded it for a long moment.

Krell leaned over her shoulder.

"Please tell me you aren't going to sign that."

Marr picked up her stylus.

"Captain, I have spent my entire career being told paperwork cannot change the past."

She looked at the date.

"It would be professionally irresponsible not to test the claim."
