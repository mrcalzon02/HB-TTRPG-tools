# The Long Patrol of Broussard

## XVII. THE MARGIN LEFT

**Author:** Mrcalzon02 / Christopher Vardeman

The convoy's first request was for a return time. Its second was for a fuel account.

Captain Broussard found the distinction encouraging. Somebody at the other end had begun to understand that the first answer depended on the second.

The *Hesperant Vale* stood two hundred and ten metres from where the recovery had begun. It was still far enough from the convoy that its lamps did not show above the horizon. Between them lay a surveyed shelf, a second ridge and a great deal of ice that had not been asked to carry a crippled freighter since the last change of weather.

The message from the *Saint Malderson* was plain. Two escort vehicles had been kept at idle longer than planned. The convoy's reserve fuel allocation was being drawn down. No one had yet ordered Broussard to abandon the freighter, but somebody had written the words *review of recovery expenditure* across the bottom of the dispatch.

Gabriel read them over his shoulder.

"A threat?"

"An accounting category."

"Those can be worse."

Broussard sent back the actual figures: distance completed, fuel consumed, the remaining recovery-hauler reserve, and the number of inspected movement stages still required before anyone could make an honest estimate. He included the replacement seals owed to the hauler by the freighter. There was no reason for the debt to become a surprise merely because the convoy was not yet close enough to collect it.

The recovery chief arrived while Broussard was sealing the message.

"We've a hollow ahead of the next marker."

"How large?"

"We don't know."

"Then you haven't a hollow. You've a reason to stop."

"That is exactly what I came to tell you."

The man sounded offended at having his caution anticipated. Broussard took his coat from the hook.

---

The new defect lay beyond the last certified anchor point. It was not visible from the bridge. A surveyor had found it by the absence of the sound he expected from his auger: the drill had passed through the upper plate, met resistance, and then dropped a handspan before biting again.

The survey chief had marked the hole and withdrawn his men to tested ground. His assistant, a narrow-faced young man called Etten, stood beside a coil of tether trying not to look at the place where the drill had dipped.

"We could bridge it," the recovery chief said.

Sella Varn crouched over the sketch. "With what?"

"A longer ground plate."

"You can bridge a hole with a plate. You cannot bridge the argument about what holds up the plate."

The chief pointed to the flanking holes. Both were sound.

"At the surface," she said. "The tow anchor pulls sideways as well as down. I need the underside."

The survey chief agreed. He wanted two new soundings, one beyond the suspected void and one toward the ridge, reached from the certified line. The weather gave him a narrow window before wind would bury the flags and turn every old hole into a new guess.

Broussard looked at the Chimeras. One held the inner approach; the damaged second vehicle remained farther back, where its plated vision block and missing track pads were liabilities only if somebody forgot they existed. Chimera One covered the survey team from firm ice. Chimera Three watched the far ridge.

"Take your time," Broussard told the survey chief.

"We have very little."

"Then don't spend it twice."

Gabriel volunteered to walk with the sounders. The survey chief accepted him for security and made it clear that security did not confer permission to choose a drill point.

"I have spent my life being told where to stand," Gabriel said. "One more expert won't injure me."

The chief looked at the commissarial badge, considered the possible replies and settled for handing him the spare tether.

---

Etten was setting the second probe when the first flag moved.

Not the wind. The flag was tied to a short black pole, and the pole itself slid sideways, slowly enough that the eye tried to explain it as a trick of distance. Gabriel saw the movement and lifted one hand.

"Stop."

The survey chief had already stopped drilling.

A sound came through the ice: three dull knocks, followed by a dragging vibration. It passed beneath the team and continued toward the pressure ridge. Etten turned his head to follow it.

"Back on the line," the chief ordered.

They began withdrawing. The nearest certified hole was fifteen paces away. Gabriel kept the tether clear of the sounder while the chief gathered the probe. The vibration stopped.

That was when the ice opened behind Etten.

The break was narrow at first, a black slit spreading across white ground. A hooked forelimb rose through it, followed by a plated ridge that struck the underside of the ice and split it into slabs. The animal was smaller than the one they had killed near the freighter, or less of it had emerged. Its mouth opened crosswise beneath the broken plates. There was no need to identify a species to know where it intended to go.

"Chimera One, hold fire!" Gabriel shouted.

The men were still between the turret and the creature.

Etten ran three steps and fell. His right leg disappeared to the knee between two slabs. The survey chief dropped his probe and caught Etten by the harness. Gabriel seized the other strap. For one instant all three were pulling against the ice and against the young man's scream.

The predator struck at the moving tether. Its hook caught the auger line and dragged the machine toward the opening. The chief let it go. The line ran across his glove hard enough to strip the outer fabric before the reel tore free.

Gabriel hauled Etten sideways while the chief pulled the trapped leg clear. The boot came out at a wrong angle. Etten tried to stand, collapsed and swore at the leg as though it had disobeyed an order.

"You can complain later," Gabriel said. "You may be quite good at it."

They carried him over the last marked hole. Behind them the predator drove its forelimb through the auger and crushed it against the edge of the crack. The survey chief shouted the position into his vox.

"Clear of the line!"

Chimera One fired a short multilaser burst into the exposed plates. The creature recoiled. Chimera Three could not get a safe angle past the ridge and did not fire. The first gunner struck again as the animal turned, scoring its side but not pinning it. It went down through the broken ice with the ruined auger, leaving a widening black hole and a noise that carried a long way beneath the surface.

Broussard ordered the vehicles to hold on certified ground. No one pursued it. The last glimpse had shown a wounded animal, not a dead one.

The survey chief knelt beside Etten and examined the leg with shaking hands. The lower bone was broken; the boot had kept the foot attached and the cold had begun doing the rest. Gabriel made the splint from two probe cases while the medic came forward on foot.

"My auger," Etten said through his teeth.

"You didn't lose it," the chief answered. "I let it go."

"They'll charge you."

"They can send the bill to the thing that took it."

The medic cut off the boot. Etten stopped arguing.

---

The survey took five hours and forty minutes after that. The first hour was spent abandoning the broken line and establishing a perimeter around the new opening. The second went into replacing the auger with hand sounders from the recovery stores. By the third, the survey chief had found that the hollow was not a single cavity but a thin roof over a narrow channel running beneath the eastern shelf.

The channel might have been there for years. It might have opened that morning. Neither answer would make it bear the freighter.

The chief shifted the route inland, then shifted it again when the soundings showed a thicker plate that sloped badly toward the ridge. The safe passage was not the shortest. Its first anchor point lay on old, dense ice; the second required the hauler to turn with its load released, move forward and take tension from a new direction.

The recovery chief drew the revised tackle angles on his slate.

Sella compared them to the old drawing and put her finger on a marker number.

"That mark was retired after the last seam survey."

"The ground hasn't moved there."

"We don't know that. You have copied an old position into a new calculation."

"It is only a reference point."

"Then reference a point you have measured today."

The chief started to argue, looked at the map and stopped. He took the drawing back. He had spent too long defending time already lost to risk another hour on a bad number.

The corrected drawing cost them forty minutes. Sella checked the new angle against the brace load traces, then walked out to inspect the pulley mount herself. She signed for the first stage only.

Broussard signed the movement order after her. He attached the survey chief's account of the predator and the medical report. Etten would need transport and treatment once they reached the convoy. Until then the freighter's medicae had room for him, but its facilities were built for ordinary shipboard injuries, not a crushed leg on a frozen ocean.

Gabriel found Broussard outside the command shelter.

"He'll keep the foot," he said. "The medic thinks so."

"And the survey chief?"

"Wants to go back to work. His hand is raw. He says he can supervise without touching anything."

"Can he?"

"Probably. His assistant has already told him which orders he intends to disobey when he gets up."

Broussard watched the chief directing the replacement sounders with his undamaged hand. There was no useful speech to make about bravery. The injured man would remember the ice without help.

---

Kelm had his own figures to consider.

The port refrigeration bank had returned to its proper temperature after the reserve-compressor service, but the cargo master could not promise it would stay there through another delay. The borrowed seals were holding. Their continued survival did not make the hauler's missing spare reappear.

Kelm spread the hold temperatures beside the convoy's latest food-allocation request.

"If we get back tomorrow, we can sell most of it. If we don't, we may have to dispose of the warmest bins."

The cargo master pointed to the forward hold. "Those are stable."

"For now."

"The restraints are good. The insulation is good. The weights are not all where the manifest says."

"They shifted in the impact."

"That explains some."

Kelm looked at him.

"What are you asking?"

"For a second weighing before the sale. Not a search. A weighing. If we send a false total to the convoy, somebody will have to make up the difference from stores that don't exist."

Kelm authorized it when the plant was off the emergency duty cycle. The bins were to remain closed; cargo inspection could wait until there was a safe unloading berth. He had no wish to expose chilled produce to the weather because an accountant might prefer a cleaner column.

Holt received the instruction and opened a new working sheet. One of the forward bins had been recorded heavy during the previous service. He copied the discrepancy rather than adjusting it to match the manifest. There were other imperfect figures, and no evidence that this one was a crime. The new sheet would allow the next watch to compare actual measurements without losing the first.

"You'll be asked why you didn't correct it," Kelm said.

"I don't know what the correct figure is, captain."

"Good. Keep it that way until you do."

Holt hesitated, then entered the instruction under Kelm's authority. He had learned to keep two imperfect records rather than manufacture one perfect one. It was a habit that made him useful and occasionally made his captain tired.

In the forward hold, cargo hands checked restraints under the working lights. A row of loose produce bins stood cold and dark behind their baffles. A worker touched one lid and found frost at its hinge. He moved on, satisfied that the seal had held. The inventory sheet recorded its weight and its temperature, not what lay below the top layer of roots.

---

The first movement was twenty-six metres.

The hauler took tension gradually. Sella watched the starboard brace trace while the survey chief stood beside the anchor with his bandaged hand tucked under one arm. Broussard kept the vehicles on the tested line and ordered infantry to watch the abandoned opening. The predator had not returned. It had not promised to stay away.

At thirteen metres the hauler chief called a pause. The ground plate had shifted a finger's width under load. The survey chief measured the displacement and found it inside the limit he had written on the new sheet, but Sella would not certify the remainder until the plate was reset and tested again.

The reset took thirty-one minutes. No one made a speech about it.

The freighter completed twenty-six metres, stopped, and settled onto the shelf. The fitters examined the brace and the new pulley run. Sella signed the completed stage, not the next one.

For the second stage, the hauler was moved forward without load. The line was reeved through the newly approved anchor; the recovery chief walked the angle with Sella and the survey chief before asking Broussard for permission to draw tension. The ship's restricted thrust was available only after the load had been accepted by the tackle. It was not a substitute for the tackle.

"Fifty," Sella said.

"And then?" asked the chief.

"Then we inspect fifty metres of work."

The hauler pulled. The freighter moved slowly enough for the people along its hull to count the marker poles. At twenty metres a vibration appeared on Sella's slate and disappeared when the chief reduced the pull. The fitters found a cable guide touching the repaired frame and shifted it clear. The next trace was clean.

The ship reached fifty metres and stopped. There was no cheering. A fitter had to crawl beneath the starboard quarter to examine a fastening, and the recovery chief needed his crew to unload the line before the new anchor could be left unattended. The ice did not care that a man might have preferred applause.

Holt entered the new total in the bridge record: two hundred and eighty-six metres from the original recovery position. The earlier figures remained legible beneath it. The fourteen-second interval before the impact remained missing. Nothing about the latest movement had made it return.

---

The evening report was longer than the one Broussard had sent that morning.

It listed seventy-six metres of new movement. It listed a fractured leg, a destroyed auger, a survey chief with a damaged hand, ammunition spent on a predator not confirmed dead, five hours and forty minutes of new surveying, two revised anchor positions, and the continuing loss of the hauler's reserve seals. It listed a refrigeration plant holding within limits but dependent on further service. It listed Chimera Two as mobile under restriction, not repaired.

It did not include an arrival estimate.

Kelm read the report before signing it. He added a cargo note: the forward bins would be reweighed before sale or disposal, and no discrepancy would be closed without a measurement. He did not ask Broussard to remove the predator from the account merely because the animal had gone away.

Gabriel watched them exchange the slate.

"Another honest document," he said.

"You disapprove?" Kelm asked.

"I'm beginning to fear they may become fashionable."

Broussard sealed the report and sent it toward the convoy. The reply would come when the relay had a clear path. Until then the freighter remained where it was, the men were fed, the sentries posted, and the wounded survey assistant was sleeping under the medicae's watch.

Outside, the lamps made small islands of light on the eastern shelf. The abandoned opening was beyond them, black beneath a thin rim of new ice. Nothing moved there that the sentries could see.

Broussard looked toward the unseen convoy. The distance already travelled was real. So was the distance remaining. He had learned to distrust anyone who tried to turn one into the other.

"Next survey at first light," he said.

The recovery chief nodded. He did not ask how far they would get.
