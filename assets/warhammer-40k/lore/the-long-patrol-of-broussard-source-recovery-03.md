# The Long Patrol of Broussard

## III. DETACHED COMMAND

**Author:** Mrcalzon02 / Christopher Vardeman  
**Based on the work of:** *The Long Patrol of Broussard*

In remarkably short order, Broussard found himself holding a packet of fresh orders and being ushered back toward the cold.

This was, he reflected, the Imperial Guard's preferred method of rewarding competence.

His troops had been promised rest.

That promise had lasted approximately twenty-seven minutes.

Instead of hot food, maintenance downtime, and several hours in which nobody was expected to shoot at anything, they were being detached from the convoy alongside three additional Chimeras and a recovery vehicle to secure a stranded freighter several kilometers off the main route.

The convoy commander had explained the situation with the calm precision of a man who would not personally be spending the next several days freezing beside a crippled merchant vessel.

The freighter had been attempting to join the convoy from a subsidiary route when something had gone wrong.

According to the surviving vox logs, it had been proceeding normally until it abruptly veered off course and struck a pressure ridge.

That alone should not have been fatal.

Freighters of its class were built to endure glancing impacts with ice formations, occasional hull strikes, and the general abuse associated with moving several thousand tonnes of cargo across a surface that periodically attempted to rearrange itself.

Unfortunately, this particular formation had contained a submerged spur of compressed ice.

The spur had punched through the lower hull.

Straight into the main engineering compartment.

The report described the resulting damage as catastrophic.

Broussard had learned long ago that Imperial technical language possessed its own hierarchy.

"Minor damage" meant something had caught fire.

"Serious damage" meant something important had caught fire.

"Catastrophic damage" meant the people writing the report had stopped trying to identify individual fires.

The freighter's main engineerium had been pierced, several power trunks severed, two gravitic control assemblies disabled, and a substantial portion of the propulsion plant flooded with freezing slurry before emergency bulkheads had sealed.

It had not sunk.

Yet.

That was apparently the optimistic portion of the briefing.

A surveillance flyer had already performed an overflight.

The vessel remained upright.

Its emergency systems were functioning.

The crew was alive.

Most of the cargo appeared intact.

And nothing large enough to worry about had yet emerged from beneath the ice.

The word yet had appeared several times in the report.

Broussard disliked reports containing the word yet.

His detachment would consist of four Chimeras, including his own, and a utility recovery hauler.

The recovery machine was an ugly little industrial vehicle designed according to the ancient Imperial doctrine that any machine fitted with sufficient hydraulics could eventually be classified as military equipment.

Its rear compartment carried repair materials, structural plates, power couplings, cable spools, sealant compounds, spare coolant lines, emergency generators, welding equipment, and enough miscellaneous engineering supplies to construct several smaller vehicles or very badly repair one large one.

Mounted along its flank was a heavy articulated manipulator arm capable of lifting replacement components, moving damaged plating, clearing debris, or—according to one handwritten annotation—"discouraging hostile fauna if necessary."

Broussard approved of that last function.

Accompanying the recovery vehicle would be a Magos of the Adeptus Mechanicus and several enginseers.

Their purpose was simple.

Determine whether the freighter could be repaired.

Then repair it.

The commander had delivered this portion with the sort of confidence generally reserved for tasks being performed by someone else.

If repairs proved possible, Broussard's detachment was to remain with the vessel until propulsion and steering were restored sufficiently for it to rejoin the convoy.

This would probably take several days.

During that time the convoy would continue moving.

Which meant the stranded vessel and Broussard's little formation would gradually fall farther and farther behind.

By the time repairs were completed, assuming repairs could be completed, they would likely find themselves somewhere near the tail of the entire movement.

Perhaps behind it.

Broussard had stared at the tactical display when the commander explained this.

"How far behind?"

"Dependent upon repair time."

"Naturally."

"Current estimate places recovery between thirty-six and seventy-two hours."

"And convoy speed?"

"Maintained."

"So several hundred kilometers."

"Potentially."

Broussard had nodded.

There were moments when military professionalism consisted primarily of not saying the first thing that entered one's mind.

If repairs proved impossible—or merely so time-consuming that convoy command decided the ship was no longer worth saving—they were to abandon it.

The crew would be evacuated.

Priority cargo would be recovered.

Sensitive equipment would be removed or destroyed.

Supplies would be distributed across the available vehicles.

Everything that could be strapped to a hull would be strapped to a hull.

Everything that could not would be left behind.

For this purpose they had been issued cargo netting.

A great deal of cargo netting.

Broussard had seen the bundles stacked beside the recovery hauler before departing the command deck.

That alone told him something.

The Munitorum did not issue that much netting because it expected everything to go according to plan.

The evacuation protocol was brutally practical.

Personnel first.

Weapons second.

Food, fuel, medical supplies, communication equipment, and other mission-essential stores after that.

Commercial cargo would be recovered according to value and transportability.

Anything left aboard after abandonment would either freeze, be crushed by seasonal ice movement, sink during the thaw, or become an extremely expensive nest for local wildlife.

The owners could submit compensation claims.

Broussard suspected their descendants would receive a response.

The final instruction had been delivered without humor.

If anything threatened the freighter, his men, the recovery crew, or the evacuation effort—

Kill it.

There had been no qualification attached.

No concern for ecological significance.

No hunting restrictions.

No conservation zones.

No authorization thresholds.

Just kill it.

Broussard had asked the obvious question.

"What size of threat are we anticipating?"

The convoy commander had looked at him for a moment.

"Captain, on this planet?"

"Yes, sir."

"Assume larger than your vehicle."

That had concluded the briefing.

Now Broussard stood inside the descending lift while the Saint Malderson's decks passed one by one beyond the indicator panel.

Command.

Officer country.

Operations support.

Habitation.

Engineering.

Supply.

The packet of orders felt heavier than it should have.

In his other hand rested the object he disliked considerably more.

A fresh Imperial seal of command.

It was a small thing.

Metal.

Stamped.

Numbered.

Official.

The aquila stood in raised relief across its face, surrounded by the identifying marks of convoy authority.

It had been issued specifically to him for this detachment.

For as long as the recovery operation lasted, Captain Broussard was not merely commanding his own Chimera section.

He was the senior military authority at the wreck site.

Four armored vehicles.

A recovery hauler.

A Mechanicus contingent.

The freighter's surviving crew.

Its cargo.

Its defense.

Its evacuation.

Its destruction, if necessary.

All of it now belonged to him.

Not permanently, of course.

Imperial authority was always temporary.

Responsibility, however, had a disturbing habit of becoming eternal.

He turned the seal over in his hand.

One side bore the Imperial aquila.

The other carried a serial number and a small activation rune confirming his temporary authority.

Such things were useful.

With it he could requisition equipment.

Override local objections.

Issue emergency orders to civilian crews.

Command convoy assets within the recovery perimeter.

Approve demolition.

Authorize abandonment.

And, if things became especially unpleasant, explain afterward why he had done all of those things.

The last function was probably the seal's true purpose.

Broussard had spent much of his career using regulation as armor.

Now regulation had handed him a badge and pushed him outside.

He considered Murphy's Law again.

Everything that could go wrong would go wrong.

The lift continued downward.

He opened the packet.

The first page contained the formal detachment authorization.

The second contained the vessel schematic.

The third contained casualty estimates.

The fourth contained cargo manifests.

Then came navigation data, engineering summaries, environmental conditions, wildlife warnings, command protocols, recovery thresholds, destruction authorities, evacuation priorities, and several pages explaining whose fault various things would legally become if circumstances deteriorated.

At the bottom of the final page sat a familiar sentence:

COMMAND DISCRETION AUTHORIZED WHERE OPERATIONAL CONDITIONS REQUIRE.

Broussard stared at it.

There were few phrases in Imperial regulation more dangerous.

It meant nobody knew what was going to happen.

The lift descended another deck.

He looked again at the new command seal.

His men had been promised rest.

Instead, they were going to spend several days stranded beside a wounded freighter on an empty frozen ocean while the rest of the convoy steadily disappeared over the horizon.

They would have limited supplies.

Limited heavy support.

A damaged ship.

A Magos.

Several dozen civilians.

And an entire ocean beneath them filled with creatures that occasionally mistook industrial machinery for food.

The lift chimed.

Vehicle deck.

The doors opened.

Cold air rolled through the corridor from the embarkation bays.

His troops were already gathering beside the Chimeras.

Someone saw Broussard carrying the command seal.

There was a brief silence.

Then Hessel—who had somehow survived both the lho-stick incident and the intervening years—looked at the seal, looked at Broussard, and sighed.

"Sir."

Broussard tucked the seal into his coat.

"Hessel."

"Are we fucked?"

Several nearby soldiers immediately became very interested in the answer.

Broussard considered the question.

"We have been assigned an independent recovery operation."

Hessel nodded slowly.

"Right."

"We have four Chimeras, a utility hauler, Mechanicus support, full emergency authority, and several days of supplies."

"Right."

"Our objective is to secure and repair a crippled freighter."

"Right."

"Several hundred kilometers behind the convoy."

Hessel looked toward the open loading ramp, beyond which endless white ice disappeared into blowing snow.

Then back at Broussard.

"So yes."

Broussard sighed.

"Yes, Hessel."

He lifted the packet.

"But administratively."
