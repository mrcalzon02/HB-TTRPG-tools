# The Long Patrol of Broussard

## XVIII. THE DISTANCE BETWEEN

**Author:** Mrcalzon02 / Christopher Vardeman

The reply from the *Saint Malderson* arrived before the survey team had finished its first meal.

The convoy would accept one urgent casualty. It would not detach a second recovery hauler. It could supply a replacement powered auger, provided somebody signed for the one destroyed on the eastern shelf, and it would issue fuel for one medical exchange along the established approach. Additional escort fuel would be considered after the next accounting period.

Broussard read the last sentence twice. There was a certain talent involved in making a clock sound like an answer.

Etten's medic stood across the table with a report folded into quarters. The young survey assistant had circulation in his injured foot, which was good. The swelling above the splint was worsening, which was not. The freighter's infirmary could keep him warm and watch the skin. It could not reduce the fracture properly if the fragments shifted.

"How long?" Broussard asked.

"Long enough that I don't want to find out."

The established inbound route ran from the freighter's original position to the convoy road. It had carried the detachment in; its markers were still checked by the rear sentries. The new eastern tow corridor was another matter. No vehicle would cross it merely because a wounded man had become inconvenient.

Chimera Three could carry Etten back to the original approach and meet a convoy ambulance at the road marker. The remaining vehicles would contract around the freighter. Chimera Two, with its plated vision block and damaged track pads, would stay on the inner line, where it could support the perimeter without pretending to be fit for a chase.

"We'll lose the far ridge watch," one commander said.

"We'll lose its vehicle. The observation post stays. Put a hard-line relay there and bring the men back if visibility closes."

Gabriel glanced at the report. "I'll go with him."

"You don't need to."

"I know. That makes the offer especially generous."

Broussard gave him the casualty papers. Hessel would drive. Neither man was to leave the certified approach to save minutes.

The medic took the order without thanks. He had already gone to prepare Etten.

---

The survey chief could not close his right hand properly.

He had wrapped the torn glove in cloth and insisted he could supervise the replacement sounders. Nobody disputed that. The question was whether he could supervise without taking a probe from someone too slow for his liking.

Sella found him trying to tighten a coupling with his bandaged hand.

"Use the other one."

"The other one's holding the pipe."

"Then ask for another pair."

He looked at her as if she had proposed hiring a second survey department. She held out her hand. After a moment he gave her the coupling.

The destroyed powered auger had been the quickest means of finding voids beneath the surface. Hand sounders required more people, more holes and more time. They also demanded patience from a crew whose last attempt at speed had put one man in the infirmary.

At the next flags, the chief assigned two men to probe from certified ground and a third to record the depth of each refusal. The replacement instrument, when it arrived, would have to be checked against these readings rather than treated as an oracle.

Sella examined the first new anchor site. Overnight cold had contracted the cable guide enough to change its clearance against the repaired starboard frame. The movement was small. The polished witness mark where the guide had touched metal was not.

She marked the guide for adjustment and told the recovery chief there would be no pull until it was done.

He had begun to anticipate these refusals. This time he sent for a fitter before she finished speaking.

"You're learning," she said.

"I'd rather be towing."

"So would I."

The survey chief called from the flags. The first line was sound. The second required another sounding farther inland. Sella went to check the load drawing. There were still places on the ice where a ship could stand. The problem was finding enough of them in the right order.

---

Etten was trying to persuade the medic he could sit upright when Gabriel entered the infirmary. The medic possessed a splint, a pain injector and no interest in the argument.

"You're leaving," Gabriel told him.

"Am I being sent back for breaking the auger?"

"That would be an expensive disciplinary journey."

"I was holding it."

"The chief released it. The animal destroyed it. You were occupied with keeping your leg attached."

Etten stared at the ceiling. "It was a good machine."

"Was it yours?"

"No. I was meant to be responsible for it."

"Those aren't the same thing."

The young man looked at him, not quite reassured. He had heard what the black coat meant long before meeting the man wearing it.

"Your chief signed the loss report," Gabriel said. "Broussard signed the casualty report. The convoy accepted the transfer. Nobody has asked you to confess to a broken machine."

"Not yet."

"No. Not yet. It is an Imperial convoy."

They moved him onto a litter with the medic directing each lift. Hessel secured it inside Chimera Three, checked the heater and then the fuel gauge with a more personal expression of concern.

"We can make the marker and back," he said.

"That is the plan," Gabriel answered.

"Plans used to be more cheerful."

They set out along the old flags. The track carried them away from the freighter's lamps and into a white landscape in which distance had very little shape. Hessel kept the speed low enough for the rear observer to read every marker. Gabriel held the casualty papers against his knee and listened to Etten's breathing over the engine.

At the road marker, the convoy ambulance was waiting with two attendants and a heated compartment. Its driver demanded a signed transfer, an injury time and the exact drugs administered. The freighter's medic had written all three.

There was no ceremony. They lifted Etten across, secured him, and counted the splint straps again.

"Will they send me back?" Etten asked.

"When you can walk, they may discover an urgent need for you somewhere with fewer teeth."

"That isn't an answer."

"No. It's the best I have."

The ambulance departed toward the convoy. Hessel watched its rear lamps disappear before turning back. Etten was no longer trapped aboard a ship whose infirmary could not treat him. The price was an afternoon without the outer escort.

---

Holt brought the cargo sheets to Kelm while the refrigeration compressor was on its scheduled low-load interval.

The second weighing had been completed without opening the bins. Cargo hands used the same calibrated deck scale, lifted each restrained bin clear of its support, let it settle and recorded the result. Where frost under a runner or an uneven sling made the number wander, they repeated the lift.

Of twelve forward bins, ten were within the declared handling tolerance. One was light. One was twenty-seven kilograms heavier than the manifest estimate after the repeat measurement.

Kelm looked at the figures. "Same one?"

"The one marked heavy before. The difference is larger now that we have a proper lift. The first figure was taken against a partly loaded support."

"Can we explain it?"

"Not from the papers."

"Can we sell it?"

"Not at that declared weight."

Every unnecessary hour of cold storage cost power. Every unnecessary opening cost cold air, exposed produce and a chance of damaging the cargo. A false weight could cost a buyer food already paid for.

"Keep it sealed until we have a safe unloading station. Mark it disputed. The cargo master can witness another weighing there."

Holt wrote the order without replacing either earlier measurement.

"Do you want Broussard told?"

"He'll get the cargo report. Don't turn a poor manifest into an arrest."

Holt nodded. He had become more comfortable with a captain who could leave a number unresolved. He was not more comfortable with the number itself.

In the forward hold, the cold bank cycled and the working lamps dimmed for a moment before recovering. A cargo hand checked the temperature recorder, then walked past the disputed bin without touching its lid. The frost at the hinge had grown thicker.

Under the upper layer of roots, something pressed slowly against the wooden partition and went still when footsteps returned. The restraint straps held. The scale had measured what was there, but no line in the manifest had been made for it.

---

The first report from the convoy said Etten had reached the road infirmary. A surgeon would examine the leg after the swelling was controlled. The report did not promise the foot. Broussard put it where the survey chief could read it himself.

The replacement auger would arrive with the next scheduled supply movement, not with the ambulance. The convoy would not risk a loaded return vehicle on an unchecked route. Broussard could hardly object without disowning his own orders.

"How far?" he asked Sella.

"Forty metres certified beyond the present mark. We can prepare another line if the sounders find what they expect."

"Do they?"

"Not yet."

The compressor service had taken longer than planned. One borrowed seal was holding, but the mechanic wanted the last serviceable spare reserved for refrigeration rather than consumed on an adjustment to the hauler's auxiliary circuit. The hauler chief protested that his machine was being treated as a donor.

"Your machine still pulls," Sella said. "The cargo doesn't stay cold on goodwill."

"It won't pull forever."

"Neither will the compressor."

Broussard set a limit: the hauler could work within its existing certified load, but there would be no increase in draw and no further movement after the next refrigeration service became due. The spare stayed with the cooling plant. The debt to the hauler remained on the account.

Kelm accepted the order, though it would make his ledger uglier.

"That's the second time you've spent my schedule on someone else's margin," he said.

"The first time it was your auxiliary bus."

"And now?"

"Your food."

Kelm looked at the temperature trace. "I suppose I should be grateful you haven't found anything else of mine to save."

Broussard signed the limit and handed back the slate.

---

They moved eighteen metres before the first halt.

The new cable guide ran clear. Sella inspected the brace while the hauler crew released tension and the survey chief checked the anchor for movement. His bandaged hand stayed in his coat. The inspection took twenty minutes.

The second stage carried them thirty-four metres along the thicker shelf. The recovery chief changed the pull angle midway, unloading the line and moving the hauler without the freighter's weight on it. The sounders found an uneven patch beyond the next marker, so he took the longer surveyed side rather than ask Sella to accept a short turn over uncertain ice.

With Chimera Three absent, ridge observers reported through a field cable laid back to the nearest protected post. Twice the wire was buried by drifting snow. Neither interruption meant an attack, but both cost time while men cleared the line and checked its insulation.

The far post reported no movement at the abandoned channel. The predator that had taken the auger remained unaccounted for.

At the end of the second stage, the recovery chief found the new anchor plate had settled unevenly. One corner stood proud. He stopped the pull himself, had the plate reset, and requested another proof load before asking Sella to sign.

She looked at him for a moment.

"What?" he asked.

"Nothing."

He went back to the plate with the expression of a man denied the satisfaction of an argument.

The last stage was forty-two metres, over ground sounded twice and marked from both ends. The freighter's restricted thrust assisted only after the tackle took the weight. A small vibration returned in the starboard brace; Sella reduced the load, compared it with the earlier trace and found the cable guide's known frequency at a different angle. She allowed the pull to resume at reduced speed.

The *Hesperant Vale* came to rest ninety-four metres beyond its morning position.

Holt entered the new total: three hundred and eighty metres from the original recovery site. He preserved the intermediate readings, the revised anchors and the unchanged fourteen-second absence in the impact recorder. The missing interval had acquired no explanation because the ship had travelled another distance.

The hauler crew shut down. Sella ordered the next compressor inspection before any new tow. The convoy was still out of sight.

---

Chimera Three returned after dark with a signed medical handoff and less fuel than Hessel liked. The observation post had remained quiet. No one had tested the weakened vehicle against the ridge.

Broussard's evening dispatch carried the distance, medical transfer, auger request, ammunition and fuel used, cooling plant's next service limit, and cargo reweigh. Kelm added that one forward bin remained disputed at twenty-seven kilograms above its declared estimate, with no finding as to cause. The document requested a safe unloading berth when the freighter reached the convoy, not an immediate investigation in a freezing hold.

Gabriel read Etten's handoff receipt and set it beside the dispatch.

"One man closer to a surgeon," he said.

"One vehicle farther from its fuel reserve."

"Both true."

The survey chief came in for the next day's route sheet. His hand had begun to swell again. Broussard sent him to the medic before discussing markers. The man obeyed with poor grace, which Broussard considered an improvement on disobedience.

The reply from convoy command was brief. The medical transfer was accepted. The auger remained scheduled. Further fuel would be issued against verified movement and consumption, not against a promised arrival date.

Broussard had not supplied one.

Outside, the ship's lamps showed a little farther across the eastern ice than they had that morning. A little was all the lamps could prove. The rest of the journey still had to be measured, paid for and survived.

In the forward hold, the disputed bin remained sealed. Its straps were sound; its weight was recorded; the men assigned to it had finished their shift.

A faint scraping beneath the produce was lost in the vibration of the compressor.

No one came to check it.
