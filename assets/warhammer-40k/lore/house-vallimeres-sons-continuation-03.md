# House Vallimere's Sons

## Continuation III — The Review Board

The curriculum office answered the second lecture by sending furniture.

Prite stood in the doorway of Simulation Hall Four and regarded twelve new chairs arranged in a straight line beneath the observation gallery. They were tall-backed, dark, polished, and sufficiently uncomfortable-looking to have been designed by somebody who considered posture a moral virtue.

Darcelle stopped beside him.

“Oh.”

Renault arrived carrying breakfast in a folded paper sleeve.

“Execution?”

“Review board,” Prite said.

Renault looked at the chairs.

“Same furniture.”

Iral Vane was already inside.

He had inspected the hall before dawn, then inspected it again after the Academy servants finished arranging the tables. Prite had stopped asking what he was looking for. If Vane found something important, he would say so. If he did not, there was no benefit in being told that the underside of a lectern remained free of assassins.

The Headmaster entered through the opposite door.

“Good. You’re early.”

“We work here,” Prite said.

“Yes. I continue to find that useful.”

Darcelle pointed at the chairs. “Who?”

“Curriculum, fleet instruction, House observers, two representatives from strategic assessment.”

“Only two?” Renault asked.

The Headmaster gave him a look.

Renault opened his breakfast.

Prite walked to the instructor’s station. A stack of copied pages waited there.

Their syllabus.

Mostly.

He picked up the first copy.

The opening line remained intact.

**THE ENEMY IS NOT OBLIGED TO RESPECT YOUR DOCTRINE.**

Below it someone had inserted a subsection titled *Approved Categories of Doctrinal Noncompliance*.

Prite turned the page.

Lesson Two had acquired a scoring rubric.

He stared at it.

Darcelle leaned over his shoulder.

“What’s wrong?”

Prite handed him the page.

Darcelle read for several seconds.

“Oh.”

Renault looked up. “Another execution?”

“They gave consequences points.”

Renault stopped chewing.

The Headmaster closed his eyes.

Prite flipped through the rest.

The practical examination from the previous day had been converted into a table. Civilian survival: fifteen points. Fleet preservation: twenty. Infrastructure retention: twenty-five. Objective control: thirty. Command efficiency: ten.

A cadet could now lose forty thousand civilians, preserve enough docks, hold the system and receive a passing mark of seventy-four.

Darcelle took another copy as if hoping it might contain a different universe.

It did not.

“They missed it,” he said.

“Yes.”

“All of it.”

“Yes.”

Renault folded his breakfast closed.

“That’s impressive.”

The Headmaster picked up the scoring sheet.

For a moment he looked very old.

Then he put it down.

“No shouting.”

Prite looked at him.

“I wasn’t going to shout.”

“No sarcasm.”

Renault looked offended.

“That seems discriminatory.”

“No duels.”

Darcelle said, “We didn’t submit any.”

“I know. I’m trying to preserve the streak.”

Vane, still near the rear entrance, said nothing.

Prite suspected this represented professional approval.

---

The board arrived at nine.

Nobody introduced themselves at length. They had names, ranks and offices, but Prite already had the slate in front of him and most of the room knew who everyone else was. The few people who did not were perfectly capable of reading.

The senior curriculum representative sat in the middle chair.

“Professor Prite.”

“Yes.”

“We have reviewed your submitted principles and yesterday’s exercise.”

“I noticed.”

The Headmaster shifted slightly behind him.

Prite remembered the prohibition on sarcasm.

Barely.

The representative continued. “There is substantial merit in the exercise. The difficulty is repeatability.”

Prite waited.

“We cannot certify an examination without a stable standard.”

“Why?”

Several people looked at him.

The representative frowned. “Because students must be evaluated consistently.”

“That isn’t what I asked.”

Darcelle’s fingers tightened around the edge of his slate.

Renault watched the board.

Prite continued.

“Why does the standard have to be a score?”

The representative glanced down at the amended syllabus.

“How else would you distinguish success from failure?”

Prite looked toward the simulation floor.

“Ask them why they did it.”

Silence.

One of the fleet instructors leaned forward.

“That is not an answer.”

“It is if you want officers.”

The instructor’s expression hardened.

Prite did not fill the silence for him.

Eventually the man said, “Explain.”

That was different.

Prite activated the previous day’s results.

Twelve systems appeared above the floor.

He selected Command Seven.

The dead stations glowed red.

“They held the system.”

The representative nodded.

“They also wrecked it.”

Another nod.

“So what score do you want me to give them?”

“That is precisely what the rubric provides.”

“No. The rubric tells them what to optimize.”

Darcelle looked at Prite.

Prite kept going.

“Give them fifteen points for civilians and they’ll learn exactly how many civilians fifteen points are worth.”

Nobody moved.

Renault spoke from beside the console.

“And after three years they’ll be very good at it.”

The senior representative looked toward him.

Renault shrugged.

“You asked for repeatability.”

One of the House observers covered a cough.

The fleet instructor ignored him.

“Then how do you prevent favoritism? One instructor approves a decision. Another condemns it.”

Darcelle answered before Prite could.

“You keep the record.”

His voice was quiet, but it did not shake.

The instructor turned.

Darcelle brought up the command logs.

“Orders, available information, timing, losses, alternatives they considered. Keep all of it. The instructor has to justify the assessment too.”

The representative frowned. “That would multiply review time.”

“Yes.”

“Considerably.”

“Yes.”

Darcelle waited.

The representative seemed to expect an apology.

None arrived.

Prite felt something dangerously close to pride and kept it to himself.

The fleet instructor stood and walked down to the simulation floor.

He studied the twelve results.

“What would you have done?” he asked.

Prite knew which exercise he meant.

“I don’t know.”

That answer caused more movement than anything else he had said.

The instructor turned.

“You designed the examination.”

“Yes.”

“And you don’t know the correct solution.”

“No.”

“Then what exactly are you teaching?”

Prite looked at the ruined refinery, the preserved moon, the fleet that had survived by sacrificing the thing it was sent to defend.

“Command.”

The instructor stared at him.

Prite pointed at Command Two.

“They made a choice with incomplete information. It failed. I want to know whether they understood the risk before they made it.”

He pointed at Command Seven.

“They made a choice that worked. I want to know whether they understood the cost before they made it.”

Then Command Eleven.

“They kept enough of everything. Maybe that was skill. Maybe the enemy simulation was kind. Maybe they got lucky.”

He lowered his hand.

“If I give them a number, the number becomes the lesson.”

Nobody answered immediately.

That was fine.

Prite was beginning to understand that silence in a classroom was not always failure.

The senior representative tapped the amended syllabus.

“The Navy cannot operate without standards.”

The Headmaster finally spoke.

“No one has suggested that it should.”

Every face turned toward him.

He walked down from the gallery.

“The question before you is whether judgment can be standardized without being replaced.”

The representative said, “And your position?”

“My position is that I hired three men who spent years defeating our standards while our officers insisted the standards were sound.”

Renault murmured, “Technically, we weren’t hired.”

The Headmaster did not look at him.

“Do not help me.”

Renault nodded.

The Headmaster faced the board again.

“You wanted their doctrine documented before it could be contaminated by politics, prestige and institutional convenience. You now have the first draft of institutional convenience.”

He lifted the scoring rubric.

“This.”

The senior representative’s mouth tightened.

“We are attempting to make their work usable.”

“Then use it.”

“How?”

The Headmaster handed the sheet back.

“Pilot it.”

---

By midday, the board had removed the scoring table.

It replaced it with nine pages of assessment procedure.

Prite regarded this as progress in the same sense that being shot in the leg was progress from being shot in the chest.

The pilot would run for one term.

Three instructors would observe each practical examination. Cadet command decisions would be assessed against the information available at the moment the order was issued. The review would preserve the decision record rather than reducing the result to territory held, ships surviving or bodies counted.

The Academy would still issue marks.

Institutions had needs.

But the mark would follow the reasoning instead of defining it in advance.

Prite could live with that.

Probably.

The board departed with copies of the revised procedure.

Vane waited until the doors shut.

“No one challenged anyone to a duel.”

Darcelle sat down heavily.

“Successful meeting.”

Renault opened what remained of his breakfast.

“It’s cold.”

Prite looked at the nine pages.

“So is victory.”

Renault stared at him.

“No.”

“What?”

“You’re not putting that in the syllabus.”

“I wasn’t.”

“You had the face.”

“What face?”

“The doctrine face.”

Darcelle laughed.

It escaped him suddenly, the exhausted laugh of somebody who had spent a morning expecting senior officers to destroy his life and instead watched them argue about grading procedure.

Prite gathered the amended pages.

On the first sheet, beneath the opening principle, the curriculum office had removed *Approved Categories of Doctrinal Noncompliance*.

Nothing replaced it.

Good.

Some sentences improved when people stopped helping them.
