# House Vallimere's Sons

## Continuation VIII — The Cost of an Answer

The fleet instruction office sent its account three days after the joint trial.

Prite had expected a recommendation. The account arrived instead: simulator hours, the lieutenant's detached duty, two fleet observers, engineering assessment, clerical sealing, and the replacement instructors required when all those people were absent from their ordinary work.

Renault added the columns.

"An expensive way to discover that another officer has opinions."

"She had orders," Prite said.

"Those cost extra."

Darcelle turned the slate around. The largest charge was not the lieutenant. It was the instruction time displaced at the fleet station where she normally served. Two engine-room emergency exercises had been postponed to release her.

"Can they run those tomorrow?" Prite asked.

"One. The other needed the same pressure rig as a scheduled damage-control assessment. It has gone to the next training cycle."

Prite looked again at the joint trial's signed finding. Cadet Iven had lost two simulated crew and delivered no filters before the depot closed. Lieutenant Morn had preserved her workers and her jurisdiction. Neither officer had been foolish. The exercise had shown how much damage two reasonable commands could suffer when they did not know what the other could afford to give.

It had not shown that the Academy's new teaching produced better fleet officers.

The Headmaster read the account without expression.

"Repeat the exercise," Prite said. "Not for every class. A small group."

"With whose lieutenant?"

"Fleet instruction could—"

"Fleet instruction has sent you the price of the first one."

Prite stopped.

The Headmaster placed the account beside the independent assessment. "You may spend the Academy's resources teaching. You may ask the fleet to spend its resources evaluating. You cannot call one the other merely because the invoice is inconvenient."

Darcelle had been silent until then.

"We can teach the distinction without borrowing another officer."

"Can you test it?" asked the Headmaster.

"Not the same way."

"Good. Keep those answers separate."

He authorized one limited command seminar using existing Academy personnel and simulator time. The independent fleet trial would remain exactly what its sealed findings said it was. The seminar would carry no new graded mark, confer no superiority claim, and replace no conventional-course examination. Prite would have to identify the instruction period it displaced.

"Who loses their room?" the Headmaster asked.

Prite had not considered that part.

---

Hall Four belonged to the navigation instructors on the second afternoon of each week.

Renault discovered this by asking the person who kept the keys. The navigation instructor listened to his proposal, looked at the training calendar and declined to surrender her examination slot.

"We only need two consoles," Renault said.

"And I need the room around them. My cadets are plotting a restricted approach. They cannot do it while yours argue over a tug."

"Mine may be very quiet."

"Yours are professors."

She returned to her chart.

Prite would once have treated the refusal as an invitation to find a maintenance entrance and use the machines after hours. That solution had been rather effective when he was an unauthorized technician. As a professor responsible for other people's records, he found it less attractive.

They obtained a shorter interval on the following morning by surrendering their own optional tactical review. Two cadets would miss a chance to rerun a difficult gunnery problem. The navigation class kept its room.

Darcelle made the exchange visible on the timetable rather than burying it under a maintenance adjustment.

"Does that matter?" Renault asked.

"Someone will ask why the gunnery review disappeared."

"Someone always asks."

"Then they'll get the actual answer."

The two stations were prepared with different briefs. One cadet would command a relief transport approaching a damaged orbital anchorage. The other would control the anchorage's traffic and emergency services. They would communicate by ordinary command channel. Neither could read the other's orders until both initial accounts had been sealed.

It was not Lieutenant Morn's independent fleet trial. Both officers would be cadets, both would know they were being taught, and the Academy would control both briefs. Darcelle put that limitation at the top of the instructor's sheet.

Prite crossed out a proposed mark for "successful negotiation."

"Why?" asked the observing instructor.

"Because a sensible refusal may be the best decision available."

"Then what do I record?"

"What each commander offered, what they understood, what they could still do, and what it cost them to wait."

"That sounds like a mark with more words."

"It could become one," Prite said. "Don't let it."

The instructor did not look entirely reassured.

---

The relief transport carried seven pallets of water-purification membranes and replacement valve assemblies. The anchorage needed the membranes before the next hospital filtration cycle. The valve assemblies could wait.

Its commander knew that the receiving berth had been restricted after a pressure-system failure. He did not know that the anchorage's only heavy tug was holding a damaged service lighter against a mooring whose clamps had not yet passed inspection.

The cadet playing the anchorage commander knew about the lighter, the clamps and the workers inside it. She had been ordered not to release the tug until a second mooring line was certified. She knew the hospital had requested urgent cargo. She did not know that the transport's auxiliary crane had failed its morning load test.

The transport arrived twelve minutes before the hospital's first requested delivery window.

"Request immediate tug assistance for berth approach and unloading," its commander transmitted.

"Unavailable," came the reply.

"Estimated release?"

"Pending mooring inspection."

"How long?"

"Unknown."

He looked at his cargo officer. Seven pallets, one damaged crane, and a hospital that had already waited too long.

"Can you give me the outer lighter berth?"

"Not with your displacement. The inner service platform can receive light cargo if you remain outside the pressure exclusion."

"How much cargo?"

The anchorage cadet checked her own loading table.

"Two pallets by utility shuttle before the next safety cycle. Three if you have an independent transfer craft."

The transport had one utility shuttle. It could carry a single pallet at a time. The craft's battery would require a recharge after two trips, and the auxiliary crane was unsafe for the heavier valve assemblies. The commander could send the membranes, but only by leaving the transport outside its protected berth while the shuttle worked.

He asked for an escort.

The anchorage cadet had one defense launch on station. Its crew were watching the damaged lighter, which would become a navigational hazard if the tug lost its hold. She could send the launch away, but she would then have no craft ready to warn traffic off the failing mooring.

"I cannot release it."

"Can you move the lighter?"

"Not until the second line is certified."

"Can you certify the line?"

"Engineering can."

"Then ask them to hurry."

"They are already working."

The transport commander closed his mouth. His navigator was looking at the clock.

"Begin shuttle transfer," he ordered. "Membranes only. Hold the valve assemblies."

On the other station, the anchorage cadet considered the same exchange. The transport had accepted her partial berth, but she had given him no estimate for the lighter. She requested one from engineering. The answer was six minutes if the damaged clamp seated properly, longer if it did not.

She could transmit six minutes. She could also transmit the condition.

"Relief transport, engineering expects six minutes to inspect the second line. That is not a release time. If the clamp fails, the tug remains committed."

"Understood."

The first pallet reached the service platform. The shuttle returned. The second was being loaded when the mooring inspection failed.

The lighter shifted against its first line. The tug's master reported that his remaining thruster margin was falling. He could hold the lighter or attempt to move it clear of the berth, but he would not do both while workers were still aboard.

The anchorage cadet had no spare tug to order into existence.

"How many workers?" she asked.

"Four."

"Can they transfer by the service platform?"

"Not without crossing the damaged clamp assembly."

She called the transport.

"Can your shuttle take personnel from the lighter?"

The commander looked at the second membrane pallet. It was already secured in the shuttle. His cargo officer said they could unload it, then make one passenger trip before the battery required attention. The hospital would receive two pallets rather than the three he had hoped to deliver.

"Yes," he answered. "After the current delivery."

"The tug may not hold that long."

He had no reason to think she was inventing the danger. He also had no reason to assume her estimate was exact.

"One minute to unload. Then the shuttle is yours."

The anchorage cadet checked the tug master's report. The lighter's drift was increasing. She could order an immediate emergency release and risk sending the workers across a damaged mooring, or accept the minute.

She accepted it.

The second pallet arrived. The shuttle crossed to the lighter and took the four workers aboard. The tug held its position until the shuttle cleared. Then the anchorage commander ordered the lighter moved away from the damaged berth, using the last of the tug's safe maneuvering reserve.

The hospital received two pallets in its first window. The remaining membranes stayed aboard the transport, and the valve assemblies were not unloaded. The lighter was saved from breaking free, but its berth would require repair. The transport had expended its shuttle reserve and remained outside the anchorage.

Both cadets were still arranging their next moves when the instructor ended the exercise.

Neither had won anything that could be placed on a banner.

---

The transport commander wrote that the anchorage had refused tug support and had later requested his shuttle. He believed he had preserved the hospital's most urgent delivery while accepting a reasonable rescue obligation.

The anchorage commander wrote that she had withheld the tug because the lighter's mooring was unsafe. She believed the transport's offer of one minute was the fastest rescue she could obtain without abandoning the hospital altogether.

The observing instructor sealed both accounts.

Only then did Darcelle release the opposite briefs.

The transport commander read about the lighter's four workers.

"I didn't know there were people aboard."

"You knew the tug was occupied," Prite said.

"I thought it was holding cargo."

"Did you ask?"

"No."

The anchorage cadet read the transport's crane report.

"You could not unload the valve assemblies."

"Not safely."

"I thought you were withholding them to bargain for the tug."

The transport commander looked offended.

"Why would I do that?"

"Because you kept asking for it."

He considered his own messages and did not answer immediately.

Prite waited. He had spent enough time teaching cadets to justify themselves. It was harder to let them recognize what their words had made another person believe.

The anchorage cadet turned back to her record.

"I should have told him about the workers when I first refused."

"Could you?" Darcelle asked.

"Yes. The lighter's status wasn't restricted. I was occupied with the clamp inspection."

The transport commander nodded. "I should have told you the crane was unserviceable. You offered a light-cargo platform and I kept talking about the tug."

The instructor made a note.

"Are those failures?" he asked Prite.

"Failures of communication. That doesn't tell you which ship should have taken the risk."

"What about the minute?" the transport commander asked. "If the tug had lost its hold, I'd have sent the shuttle sooner."

"Would you have known the hold was about to fail?" Prite asked.

"No."

"Then don't borrow that knowledge now."

The anchorage cadet looked at the simulation's end state.

"I could have refused his minute."

"You could," said Prite.

"And then the hospital would have received only one pallet."

"Yes."

"Which was correct?"

Prite glanced toward the sealed assessments. The exercise had not contained an answer to that question. It had contained two officers with real limits, and a situation that continued changing while they spoke.

"Tell me what you would need to know to choose," he said.

They spent the next quarter-hour discussing the tug's actual margin, the shuttle's loading time and the hospital's remaining filtration reserve. No one pretended those figures had been available to both commanders at the beginning.

Darcelle left the cadets' first accounts sealed. Their later understanding would be recorded as a debrief, not quietly inserted into the decisions they had already made.

---

Vane intercepted the House Caldrake observer outside the records chamber.

The observer carried the joint trial's signed findings and a new request for the seminar's command-channel transcript.

"The Academy has represented this work as a response to the fleet evaluation," the observer said. "My House wishes to see whether the correction was applied."

"The signed findings are yours under the observer agreement," Vane answered.

"And the correction?"

"The seminar is instruction, not an independent evaluation."

"Then the Academy has not tested its correction."

"Not with the fleet. It has taught it."

The observer examined the wording on the request.

"Those distinctions seem particularly convenient."

"They are also accurate."

Vane did not deny that House Caldrake had a legitimate interest in the Academy's public claims. Its observer rights were written down. He could not make those rights disappear because the House had previously filed dangerous petitions against the Three Admirals.

He could enforce their boundary.

The observer could receive the signed report describing the seminar's purpose, its use of existing staff, and its non-graded status. The private command-channel transcript and the cadets' individual debriefs were not included in the agreement.

"Will the Headmaster sign the refusal?" the observer asked.

"Yes."

"Then I shall request it formally."

"You may."

The observer departed without raising his voice. Vane watched him go, then entered the request in the security record. No new duel petition had accompanied it. That fact belonged in the record as much as the request did.

Prite found him there a few minutes later.

"Trouble?"

"An observer exercising a right."

"That sounds almost pleasant."

"Rights are most interesting when the people exercising them dislike you."

Vane handed him the draft response. Prite read it twice. He wanted to add an explanation of why the House could not be trusted with a cadet's private doubts.

"Don't," Vane said.

"I haven't written anything."

"You were about to."

Prite returned the slate. The existing agreement was sufficient. A personal accusation would make a narrow lawful refusal look like an attempt to conceal the Academy's results.

He had learned that from watching other officers defend boundaries. It was less satisfying when he had to do it himself.

---

The Headmaster signed the seminar account that evening.

It recorded two pallets delivered, four simulated workers evacuated, a damaged berth removed from service, an exhausted utility shuttle and five pallets still aboard the relief transport. It also recorded the Academy's lost tactical-review interval, the staff hours consumed and the absence of any independent fleet-performance finding.

The fleet instruction office agreed to consider a second live-counterpart assessment during the next review period, provided the Academy supplied an actual staffing plan. No officer had been assigned. No funding had been promised.

Renault studied the timetable.

"If we do this every week, we shall have to stop teaching something else."

"Yes," said Prite.

"What?"

Prite looked at the ordinary course beneath their experimental one. Navigation, gunnery, damage control, command procedure. The Academy had kept those classes running while he and his friends discovered how much its assumptions had shaped their private games.

He had once imagined that becoming a professor would mean having permission to use the machines.

Now permission was the easy part.

"Not the things that keep people alive," he said.

"That leaves us very little," Renault replied.

Darcelle entered the technicians' overtime hours on the same account as the simulator use. The maintenance team had stayed after the seminar to restore the two consoles for the navigation class. They were owed time, not congratulations.

The Headmaster looked at the complete cost.

"Will you ask for another session?"

"Yes," Prite said.

"Then bring me the class it displaces, the staff it requires and the question it can actually answer."

Prite took the signed report back to the workroom. His slate still held three numbered questions and one without a number.

**Who is allowed to say no?**

He had not found a rule that made every refusal wise. He had found people who could explain what a refusal protected, and other people who had to decide what to do afterward.

The Academy could teach that.

Whether it could afford to teach it often enough to matter was another question, and for once Prite wrote the question on the budget sheet rather than the syllabus.
