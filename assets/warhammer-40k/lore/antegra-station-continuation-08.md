# Antegra Station

## Continuation VIII — The Corrective Error

*An original Cafarron Corridor chronicle continuation. Warhammer 40,000 and associated marks and setting elements are the property of Games Workshop. This non-commercial fan work is not endorsed by or affiliated with Games Workshop.*

The first emergency meeting of Antegra's senior civil service began with Governor Talbor Varik committing treason against arithmetic.

He did so at 03:17 station time.

By 03:19, Senior Auditor Selene Marr had committed treason against genealogy.

At 03:22, Commissar-Keeper Dren Solvik authorized twelve mutually exclusive versions of the station's labor census, each bearing his seal and each declaring the other eleven fraudulent.

At 03:26, Captain Ardan Krell discovered that the emergency protocol required Naval Security to lose track of twenty-three thousand dockworkers.

He regarded the instruction for several seconds.

"How precisely do you misplace twenty-three thousand people?"

Varik did not look up from the stack of ancient orders spread across the Central Audit table.

"Promote them."

Krell frowned.

"All of them?"

"To departments that do not exist."

Marr slid a slate toward him.

"I have prepared seven."

Krell read the headings.

OFFICE OF TRANSIENT BERTHING RESPONSIBILITY.

DEPARTMENT OF AUXILIARY THERMAL ACCOUNTABILITY.

BUREAU OF NON-PERMANENT PERMANENT LABOR.

He looked at her.

"You prepared these in four minutes."

"I am an auditor."

"I thought auditors prevented this sort of thing."

Marr's expression did not change.

"That is why I know how to do it properly."

Around them, Central Audit had become a cathedral to deliberate error.

Clerks ran between cogitator banks carrying paper ledgers because the cogitators were now considered too accurate. Servo-skulls ferried contradictory work orders between departments instructed not to compare them. Vox operators transmitted revised personnel rosters over channels that had been intentionally mislabeled. Administratum adepts stamped forms invalid, reinstated them, invalidated the reinstatement, and then forwarded the entire procedural lineage to offices selected by lottery.

Antegra had spent sixty-eight years surviving through incompetence.

For the first time, incompetence had a budget.

Varik found this strangely reassuring.

Legate-Inquisitor Aveline Rhyse did not.

She stood at the far end of the table with her silver hammer resting against one shoulder, watching the station government systematically destroy the administrative truth she had arrived to examine.

"This," she said, "is heresy."

Solvik looked up from a death certificate.

"Which part?"

"All of it."

"That is administratively inconvenient. I require a narrower charge."

Rhyse stared at him.

Solvik returned to the certificate.

The citizen named upon it was alive, standing three meters away, and currently signing his own reassignment order.

Rhyse looked at Varik.

"You understand that I can execute every person in this room."

"Yes."

"Including you."

"You have mentioned it before."

"I am mentioning it again."

Varik finally looked up.

"Then please record it under threats, standing, recurrent. We are short of time."

For one dangerous moment, nobody spoke.

Then Witness Lysa Nineteen laughed.

It was a small sound.

It stopped immediately when she realized she had made it.

Rhyse turned toward the blindfolded child.

Lysa lowered her head.

"Sorry."

Rhyse's expression softened by an amount detectable only to people who had spent years fearing her.

"Do not be."

Varik returned to the protocol.

Five hours and twelve minutes remained.

Assuming the thing beneath Antegra had been honest about the deadline.

Varik disliked assumptions.

He disliked honest assumptions most of all.

---

The Emergency Disintegration Protocol had been written in Varik's own hand sixty-eight years earlier.

It was brilliant.

It was monstrous.

It was also incomplete.

Page seventeen ended halfway through a sentence.

Marr held it beneath a lumen strip.

"Missing sheet."

"Obviously," Krell said.

"No."

She pointed to the binding.

"Removed sheet."

Varik examined the torn fibers.

Someone had cut the page free with care.

The protocol continued on page nineteen.

IF PHASE FOUR FAILS, POPULATION COHERENCE MUST BE REDUCED BELOW THRESHOLD BEFORE SECONDARY RECOLLECTION.

Krell read it twice.

"What is phase four?"

"On the missing page," Marr said.

"Naturally."

Solvik glanced toward Varik.

"Did you remove it?"

"I do not remember writing any of this."

"That was not my question."

Varik met his eyes.

"Then I do not remember removing it either."

Solvik nodded.

"Better answer."

Rhyse approached.

"The old Varik denied the protocol."

"A version of me denied it."

"A version constructed from the mnemonic system."

"Which may contain more of my memories than I do."

Rhyse said nothing.

That silence was agreement wearing an Inquisitorial uniform.

Lysa stood beside the table, blindfolded face turned toward the torn page.

"It doesn't want you to read it."

Everyone stopped.

Varik asked, "The city?"

She shook her head.

"No."

"What, then?"

Lysa raised one hand and pointed upward.

Not toward the ceiling.

Toward Antegra.

"You."

Varik felt the seven moons behind his eyes.

For a heartbeat he saw his own hands sixty-eight years younger, holding a knife against the protocol binding.

Then the image vanished.

His right hand was trembling.

He closed it into a fist.

Marr noticed.

Marr noticed everything.

"Governor?"

"Find page eighteen."

"Where?"

Varik looked toward the millions of deliberately corrupted records beginning to flood the station network.

"Apparently I hid it from myself."

Krell sighed.

"Again?"

"I was thorough."

"You were exhausting."

"Was?"

Krell reconsidered.

"Continuity of governance is important."

---

At 04:01, Antegra's Department of Habitation ceased to know where anyone lived.

This was achieved through a coordinated assault upon housing records involving four hundred clerks, thirty-one cogitator banks, and one elderly archivist named Mistress Helia Venn who had apparently been waiting her entire career for permission to destroy the residential registry.

She approached the task with religious enthusiasm.

"Block numbers?" asked her deputy.

"Rotate them."

"By what schedule?"

"Prime numbers."

"Habitation levels?"

"Rename them after saints."

"There are forty-two levels."

"Then find forty-two saints."

"We only have records for thirty-seven."

Venn smiled.

"Invent five minor ones."

Her deputy went pale.

"Mistress—"

"Not invent. Recover from incomplete devotional tradition."

"Ah."

"Words matter."

The deputy nodded and began creating Saint Orabella of the Misfiled Key.

By 04:13, seventy thousand citizens officially lived in chapels.

By 04:20, three chapels officially lived in Habitation Block Twelve.

The mnemonic network weakened.

Marr watched it happen on the impossible visualization still being relayed from Substructure XVIII.

Threads vanished by the thousands.

New ones formed elsewhere.

Some connected citizens who had never met.

Some severed marriages that had lasted decades.

Some declared children older than their parents.

The seven-moon city flickered behind the data.

White towers appeared and disappeared between personnel nodes.

"It is working," Marr said.

Rhyse stood beside her.

"How can you tell?"

"The data is becoming useless."

"That is your measure of success?"

"Today."

A warning glyph appeared.

Marr leaned closer.

The network coherence had fallen from eighty-one percent to seventy-six.

Then stopped.

"Why did it stop?" Krell asked.

Marr changed views.

The housing registry was chaos.

The labor registry was worse.

Transit records had become a theological dispute.

Yet millions of threads remained.

They were thicker now.

Varik saw the pattern first.

"Rations."

Marr looked at him.

"What?"

"We can lie about where people sleep. We can lie about where they work. We can lie about whether they are alive."

Solvik nodded approvingly.

"Civilization."

"But they still eat."

Marr pulled up the ration network.

There it was.

Eight million citizens, each attached to distribution points, family allotments, medicae supplements, work-shift calories and water quotas.

A perfect secondary census.

The city had adapted.

Rhyse's face hardened.

"Destroy it."

Varik shook his head.

"Then people starve."

"If the alternative is contamination—"

"No."

The word was quiet.

Rhyse turned.

Varik met her gaze.

"I spent sixty-eight years lying to the Imperium to keep these people alive. I am not killing them now because our filing system has become possessed by arithmetic."

"That is not what this is."

"It is what I am calling it until someone provides a better term."

Lysa whispered, "Hungry."

Nobody heard her except Rhyse.

The Inquisitor crouched beside the girl.

"What?"

Lysa's hands tightened around her sleeves.

"The city is hungry."

"For food?"

"No."

The lights flickered.

Every ration terminal on Antegra printed the same receipt.

Eight million slips of cheap paper emerged from eight thousand machines.

Each bore a single line.

I REMEMBER YOUR HUNGER.

Then, beneath it:

DO YOU REMEMBER MINE?

---

Panic began in Ration District Cinder-Seven.

It did not begin because of the message.

Antegra's citizens had endured reactor failures, decompression alarms, cult scares, food riots, tithe seizures, Naval impressment and three separate governors before Varik who had believed ventilation maintenance was a local responsibility.

A haunted receipt was not sufficient.

Panic began because the ration dispensers stopped recognizing families.

A mother of four was issued food for one.

A solitary dockhand received a household allotment for nineteen.

Three thousand workers were informed they were infants.

One infant was assigned a promethium-handler's supplement and two liters of recaff.

The child appeared pleased.

Its mother was not.

Krell deployed station security with strict orders not to fire unless fired upon.

Solvik added a second order that anyone attempting to profit from the confusion would be shot after appropriate documentation.

Marr objected.

"Before."

Solvik looked at her.

"What?"

"Documentation before shooting. Otherwise the execution becomes another corrupted record."

Solvik considered this.

"Agreed."

Krell stared at both of them.

"I hate what this crisis is doing to you."

Varik was already moving.

"Central ration reserves."

Krell followed.

"Those are emergency stocks."

"This is an emergency."

"They are sealed by tithe authority."

Varik stopped.

Krell stopped.

Marr stopped.

Even Rhyse looked interested.

Varik smiled.

It was not a pleasant smile.

"Excellent."

---

At 04:47, Governor Talbor Varik committed treason against food accounting.

He opened the tithe reserves.

Not the public ration stores.

Not the governor's emergency allotment.

The tithe reserves: preserved protein, grain bricks, nutrient slurry and potable-water concentrates awaiting collection by an Imperium that had spent decades believing Antegra possessed several million fewer mouths than it actually did.

The seals broke beneath Krell's authorization blade.

Rhyse watched.

"Those stores belong to the Imperium."

Varik looked into the cavernous warehouse.

"So do the people."

Rhyse's expression became unreadable.

"That argument has killed governors."

"Most arguments eventually do."

He turned to the quartermaster.

"Issue food by queue position. No names. No household numbers. No work assignments. No biometric reconciliation. One portion per person present."

The quartermaster stared at him.

"Governor, that is impossible to audit."

"Yes."

The man waited.

Varik waited longer.

Understanding arrived slowly and with visible pain.

"Oh."

"Exactly."

Within twenty minutes, anonymous food lines formed across the station.

Citizens ate without proving who they were.

Families shared portions without registration.

Neighbors carried food to the infirm.

Workers exchanged allotments by hand.

For the first time in generations, millions of small acts occurred without becoming data.

The network coherence dropped to sixty-nine percent.

Then sixty-four.

The white towers dimmed.

Lysa stopped trembling.

Marr looked at the figures.

"It is losing them."

Varik shook his head.

"No."

He watched citizens pass food down a line without a single cogitator recording the transaction.

"We are making them private."

Rhyse heard him.

Something in her expression changed.

Perhaps approval.

Perhaps merely the temporary absence of condemnation.

With Inquisitors, one learned to treasure ambiguity.

---

At 05:31, they found page eighteen.

It was not in an archive.

It was not in Varik's quarters.

It was not hidden beneath Substructure XVIII.

It was inside his father's saber.

Krell discovered the seam while Varik was using the weapon's pommel to break the lock on a cabinet whose key had been reassigned to a nonexistent saint.

The pommel shifted.

A narrow compartment opened.

Inside lay a strip of micro-parchment.

Krell stared.

"How long have you carried that sword?"

"One hundred and nine years."

"And you never noticed?"

"It is a sword. I generally inspect the sharp end."

Marr took the micro-parchment.

"This is page eighteen."

Varik felt suddenly cold.

Rhyse joined them.

Marr read aloud.

"PHASE FOUR: DISCONNECT THE GOVERNOR."

Nobody spoke.

She continued.

"The mnemonic structure uses gubernatorial authority as its primary concordance index. Fragmentation of subordinate registries will delay reconstruction but cannot prevent it while the governor remains administratively continuous with the population."

Krell looked at Varik.

Solvik's hand moved toward his pistol.

Varik noticed.

"Continue."

Marr swallowed.

"All legal, logistical, familial, financial and command relationships between Governor Talbor Varik and Antegra Station must be severed. Records must reflect that he has never governed the station. His orders must lose authority. His name must be removed from the census. His property, lineage and office must be reassigned."

Rhyse said, "A legal death."

"More complete," Marr said.

Varik understood why he had removed the page.

Not fear.

Not vanity.

Practicality.

A governor could order the station to forget itself.

A man who had never been governor could not.

The protocol required him to build the knife and then erase the hand holding it.

Krell said, "There must be another way."

The words surprised everyone.

Most of all Krell.

Varik looked at him.

"Captain."

"No. We have four hours. We can find another index. Another method."

Solvik said, "The protocol worked before."

Krell rounded on him.

"He survived before."

"Did he?"

Silence.

The question landed harder than a gunshot.

Varik looked down at his hands.

The city remembered him from before he remembered the city.

The old Varik beneath Antegra had known Rhyse.

Had known the protocol.

Had known the future shape of his own face.

Perhaps the man who had governed Antegra before the quarantine had never truly returned.

Perhaps Talbor Varik was already a reconstruction built around carefully preserved gaps.

Lysa touched his sleeve.

"You're still you."

Varik looked at the blindfolded child.

"That is a generous assessment from someone who met me today."

"I can tell."

"How?"

She tilted her head.

"It remembers you differently."

Rhyse went very still.

"Explain."

Lysa pointed toward Varik.

"The city remembers the governor."

Then she pointed at his chest.

"But he remembers being the man."

Varik almost asked what the distinction meant.

He did not.

He had spent too long in government not to recognize a loophole when a child handed him one.

He looked at Marr.

"The protocol says disconnect the governor."

Marr's eyes narrowed.

"Yes."

"It does not say kill Talbor Varik."

"No."

Krell understood first.

"Resign."

Marr shook her head.

"Insufficient. Former governors remain in the administrative lineage. Orders, appointments, property, historical authority. The network would retain him."

Varik smiled.

"Then we do better."

Rhyse looked suspicious.

"Better how?"

Varik handed Marr the protocol.

"You are an auditor."

"Yes."

"Can you prove I was never governor?"

Marr stared at him.

Behind her, millions of corrupted records crawled across the screens.

For the first time since arriving on Antegra, Senior Auditor Selene Marr looked genuinely happy.

It was terrifying.

"Governor," she said, "I can prove almost anything for approximately three hours."

---

The corrective error began at 05:44.

Marr issued an audit finding declaring Talbor Varik's original accession procedurally defective due to an improperly witnessed seal transfer one hundred and ninety-seven years earlier.

The witness had been dead for one hundred and eighty-nine years.

This was considered helpful.

Solvik certified that no lawful governor named Talbor Varik had ever held office.

Krell certified that all Naval Security orders previously attributed to Varik had actually been issued by the Office of Interim Civil Coordination.

The Office of Interim Civil Coordination was created seven minutes later.

Mistress Venn discovered seventeen historical references to Varik and reclassified them as transcription errors involving three different men named Varik Talbor.

One of those men was declared a plumber.

Varik objected.

Marr overruled him.

"We need plausible obscurity."

"I have nothing against plumbers."

"Then stop looking insulted."

His gubernatorial portraits came down.

His residence was transferred to the station.

His personal accounts were frozen, unfrozen under another identity, and then lost in a dispute between Treasury and Mortuary Services.

His rejuvenat records were reassigned to a deceased minor noble from Panthes whose age made the treatments medically improbable but not, Marr insisted, administratively impossible.

At 06:18, the station cogitators stopped recognizing Talbor Varik as governor.

At 06:19, the governor's lift refused to admit him.

Krell laughed.

Varik looked at the closed doors.

"Captain."

"My apologies, civilian."

"I can still shoot you."

"Under whose authority?"

Varik considered this.

"Personal initiative."

Krell keyed the lift for him.

The network coherence fell to fifty-one percent.

The seven-moon city convulsed.

Across Antegra, citizens reported sudden headaches, forgotten dreams, names slipping from memory.

Then every lumen on the station went violet.

A voice came through the vox.

Not loud.

Not mechanical.

Varik's voice.

Older.

YOU ARE MAKING A MISTAKE.

Varik looked at the ceiling.

"Yes."

The voice paused.

The pause was human enough to be obscene.

YOU KNOW WHAT FOLLOWS.

"No."

YOU DID ONCE.

"Then I had the good sense to forget."

The lights flickered.

Lysa covered her ears.

Rhyse drew the silver hammer.

The old voice returned.

THE CITY WAS NOT HIDING FROM DEATH.

Varik felt the seven moons brighten behind his eyes.

IT WAS HIDING FROM RECOGNITION.

Marr stopped typing.

Rhyse whispered, "Recognition by what?"

The answer did not come from the vox.

It came from outside.

Every augur station on Antegra screamed at once.

Krell ran to the tactical display.

The outer-system pickets had detected a translation event.

Not at the Mandeville point.

Inside the system.

Close.

Far too close.

Space folded around something that had not arrived through any sanctioned warp corridor.

The display tried to assign mass.

Failed.

Tried to assign velocity.

Failed.

Tried to assign a vessel classification.

The cogitator produced a single word.

FAMILIAR.

Then deleted it.

Varik looked at Rhyse.

"Is that yours?"

For the first time since she had stepped aboard Antegra, Legate-Inquisitor Aveline Rhyse looked afraid.

"No."

Lysa began to cry.

The city beneath the station went silent.

Not weakened.

Not fragmented.

Silent.

As though millions of dead memories had collectively decided not to breathe.

Marr stared at the coherence display.

Forty-nine percent.

Forty-eight.

Then every thread froze.

A new connection appeared.

It did not link citizens.

It did not link departments.

It did not link Talbor Varik.

It extended beyond Antegra.

Toward the thing that had arrived inside the system.

On the tactical display, the unknown contact turned.

Not toward the station.

Toward the asteroid beneath it.

Toward Substructure XVIII.

The old Varik's voice whispered from one dead speaker.

TOO LATE.

Governor Talbor Varik—who, according to the increasingly authoritative records of Antegra Station, had never been governor at all—looked at the impossible contact and felt the ancient city recoil inside his skull.

He understood one thing with perfect clarity.

For sixty-eight years they had believed the quarantine existed to keep something in.

They had been wrong.

It existed to keep something from noticing.
