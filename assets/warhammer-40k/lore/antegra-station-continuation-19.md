# Antegra Station

## Continuation XIX — The Witness

*An original Cafarron Corridor chronicle continuation. Warhammer 40,000 and associated marks and setting elements are the property of Games Workshop. This non-commercial fan work is not endorsed by or affiliated with Games Workshop.*

The hatch began to open.

Captain Ardan Krell pointed his sidearm at it.

Legate-Inquisitor Aveline Rhyse pointed hers at it as well, which was considerably more reassuring until one remembered that reassurance was not normally among the intended effects of an Inquisitor aiming a weapon.

Magos Hester Vhal had the plasma cutter ready.

Selene Marr had a stylus.

Nobody laughed at her.

Experience had improved them.

The outer hatch rolled aside six centimetres and stopped.

A hand appeared in the gap.

Human.

Bare.

Grease beneath the nails.

Two fingers missing from the left hand.

Krell inhaled sharply.

"Vale lost those in the condenser accident."

Rhyse did not lower her pistol.

"According to which record?"

Krell thought about that.

"The one I remember."

"At present that is not a useful distinction."

The hand withdrew.

A man's voice came through the gap.

"Governor?"

Young Talbor Varik looked at Old Talbor Varik.

Old Talbor Varik looked at Young Talbor Varik.

"You take it," said the older man.

"Why?"

"Because he died on your watch."

"Apparently he died on several."

"Seniority."

"That is not how seniority works."

"It is how I have always used it."

Marr stepped between them.

"Edrin Vale?"

Silence.

Then:

"I think so."

Rhyse's expression became colder.

"That is an unsatisfactory answer."

"Lady, I have had an unsatisfactory morning."

Krell blinked.

"That sounds like Vale."

"Do not identify him by sarcasm," Rhyse said.

"It narrows the field."

The brass automaton stamped once.

WITNESS IDENTITY REMAINS DISPUTED.

Marr looked at it.

"Helpful."

The hatch opened another centimetre.

Beyond it there should have been the Dock Fourteen transit chamber.

There was not.

There was a corridor.

It resembled Antegra.

That was the first problem.

It resembled Antegra too well.

The deck plates were an older pattern, ribbed with reinforcement webs removed from the station decades before Marr was born. The lumen strips were narrow yellow tubes. Prayer strips hung from conduit brackets in the pre-revision maintenance style. Along the far wall, somebody had painted a deck designation in white stencil.

TWENTY-SEVEN.

Krell lowered his pistol by perhaps half a degree.

"Deck Twenty-Seven is fourteen levels above us."

Vhal answered immediately.

"Current Deck Twenty-Seven is fourteen levels above us."

"Thank you, Magos."

"Precision requested implicitly."

A man stepped into the gap.

Edrin Vale had been a maintenance supervisor.

Marr knew this because she remembered his personnel file.

She also remembered discovering that he had never possessed one.

He was fifty-three years old, perhaps. Broad shoulders bent by years beneath machinery. Grey beginning in black hair. A scar across his jaw. Antegra utility coveralls bearing an obsolete cog-and-seven-moons patch.

His face was familiar.

That familiarity frightened Marr more than the impossible corridor.

The Collector had erased Vale so thoroughly that even memories of him had become suspect.

Yet here he stood, looking tired.

Not spectral.

Not radiant.

Not possessed by any visible daemon.

Just tired.

Imperial citizens were often most convincing in that condition.

"Don't shoot," Vale said.

Rhyse kept her pistol level.

"Convince me."

Vale considered this.

"Ammunition requisition?"

Krell made a small choking sound.

Marr almost smiled.

"Where are you?" she asked.

Vale looked behind him.

"Antegra."

"Which one?"

He stared at her.

"There are more?"

The brass automaton struck the docket table.

WITNESS RESPONSE ENTERED.

IDENTIFICATION OF VENUE: ANTEGRA.

IDENTIFICATION OF VENUE: DISPUTED.

Vale noticed the automaton.

"Oh, Throne."

"You know it?" Marr asked.

"No. I know paperwork."

"Also useful."

The elderly adjudicator had gone very still.

The unnamed naval officer behind her whispered something Marr did not hear.

The adjudicator replied without looking away from Vale.

"Do not close the hatch."

Rhyse glanced at her.

"Why?"

"Because he is presently establishing continuity between venues."

"And if we close it?"

"We determine which side he belongs to."

Marr understood.

"And determination is reconciliation."

The adjudicator nodded.

The Collector appeared in the observation glass.

Not as a body.

As agreement.

Every reflection in the chamber suddenly showed the same angle.

Every face occupied precisely the same position relative to the glass.

The thing had returned to watch.

Vale saw it.

He went pale.

"That thing."

Young Varik stepped forward.

"You remember it?"

Vale's eyes remained on the glass.

"Remember? Governor, I've been fixing what it did for seventy-one years."

Nobody moved.

Old Varik spoke first.

"How old are you?"

"Fifty-three."

"You have been fixing it for seventy-one years."

"Yes."

"You see the difficulty."

Vale rubbed his face.

"Governor, I work maintenance. Difficulty is what management calls a problem after assigning it downstairs."

Krell lowered his weapon another degree.

"Definitely Vale."

Rhyse ignored him.

Marr approached the hatch but did not cross its threshold.

"Explain."

Vale looked back down the impossible Deck Twenty-Seven.

"I don't remember seventy-one years. I remember shifts."

"How many?"

"Too many."

"That is not a number."

"It is in maintenance."

Marr waited.

Vale sighed.

"Every time the station changes, somebody has to fix what doesn't fit. Doors open into the wrong pressure zone. Coolant lines connect to machinery that was removed twenty years earlier. Deck numbers change. Dead workers report for shift. Living workers discover their quarters belong to people who haven't been born yet."

Vhal's lenses brightened.

"You retained awareness across alterations."

"I retained work orders."

"Clarify distinction."

Vale reached inside his coveralls and produced a battered slate.

Its casing had been repaired with wire, devotional wax and what appeared to be part of a ration tin.

He held it through the hatch.

Vhal did not take it.

Marr did.

The slate was heavy.

Old.

Its screen flickered awake.

MAINTENANCE BACKLOG.

OPEN ITEMS: 4,118,009.

Krell whistled.

"That's obscene."

Vale shrugged.

"We're short staffed."

Marr scrolled.

The first work order was dated today.

The second was dated nineteen years from now.

The third had been filed sixty-eight years ago.

The fourth was older than Antegra Station.

The fifth listed the requesting department as MUNICIPAL WEATHER.

Antegra had no municipal weather.

The sixth concerned replacement of a chapel door after damage caused by Captain Ardan Krell.

Krell leaned over her shoulder.

"I have never damaged a chapel door."

The slate chimed.

WORK ORDER UPDATED.

CAUSE: CAPTAIN ARDAN KRELL.

Krell stepped away from the slate.

"I dislike predictive maintenance."

Marr continued scrolling.

Thousands of orders referred to stations she did not recognize.

Thousands referred to familiar decks in impossible configurations.

Some were closed.

Most were not.

One category appeared again and again.

DISCONTINUITY REPAIR.

"What is this?"

Vale's expression changed.

"That's why I'm here."

The Collector sharpened in the glass.

The brass automaton struck twice.

RESPONDENT OBJECTS TO TESTIMONY.

Marr glanced at the printer.

"On what grounds?"

The answer emerged.

WITNESS DOES NOT EXIST.

Marr looked at Vale.

"Do you object?"

"Strongly."

"Noted."

She wrote it on the strip.

WITNESS OBJECTS TO NONEXISTENCE.

The adjudicator closed her eyes briefly.

"You cannot simply—"

The brass automaton stamped the notation.

OBJECTION RECEIVED.

Marr looked at the adjudicator.

"Apparently I can."

Vale smiled for the first time.

It was a tired maintenance man's smile: the expression of someone discovering that another department had accidentally become useful.

"Discontinuity repair," Marr prompted.

Vale nodded.

"When the station changes, most people change with it. They remember the new corridor. The new office. The worker who was always there. The worker who never was. Maintenance doesn't always get that luxury."

"Why?"

"Because pipes are stubborn."

Vhal emitted a burst of binharic approval.

Vale continued.

"You can change a record. You can change a memory. But if a coolant pipe used to turn left and now history says it always turned right, sometimes there's still three metres of pipe inside the wall that disagrees."

Marr looked at the slate.

"Material contradictions."

"We call them jobs."

Old Varik moved closer.

"You have been collecting physical evidence of previous Antegra states."

"No."

"No?"

"I've been repairing them."

Old Varik stared.

Vale frowned.

"What? You want the station to vent atmosphere because the universe has a filing problem?"

Krell nodded solemnly.

"He has a point."

Marr understood before anyone else did.

The Collector reconciled histories.

Antegra survived through contradiction.

But contradictions were not merely in archives, memories and duplicated identities.

They were in the walls.

Seventy-one years of them.

Millions of small physical scars where one Antegra had failed to become another cleanly.

And maintenance had been cataloguing them without knowing what they were.

The slate was not a maintenance backlog.

It was a fossil record.

The Collector moved.

Every reflection turned toward Marr's hand.

Vhal's mechadendrites snapped forward.

"Do not permit respondent access to slate."

Rhyse raised her pistol toward the windows.

"Can it take it?"

The elderly adjudicator answered.

"If the slate is admitted as evidence, it becomes part of the case."

Marr looked at her.

"Protected?"

"Disputed."

"Better."

She placed the slate on the docket table.

"Petitioners move to admit maintenance backlog as physical evidence of unresolved venue identity."

The printer screamed.

RESPONDENT OBJECTS.

"Grounds?"

IRRELEVANT.

Marr looked at four million open work orders.

"That is optimistic."

The automaton stamped.

EVIDENCE ADMITTED PROVISIONALLY.

The Collector struck.

The chamber lights went white.

For one impossible instant Antegra became singular.

Marr saw the station as the Collector wanted it.

One history.

One set of decks.

One Talbor Varik.

One Edrin Vale.

One correct past.

It was beautiful.

That was the horror of it.

No contradictory casualty lists.

No duplicated children.

No dead workers collecting rations.

No corridors remembering different centuries.

Every grief had a cause.

Every death had a date.

Every person had one life.

For half a heartbeat Marr wanted it.

Then she saw what was missing.

Choice.

Not freedom. The Imperium had never been profligate with that commodity.

But the smaller, uglier human thing beneath it: the capacity to be inconsistent.

To regret yesterday and contradict oneself tomorrow.

To remember a dead man incorrectly because one loved him.

To lie.

To forgive.

To change.

The Collector's perfect history had no room for error.

Therefore it had no room for mercy.

The vision broke.

Marr was on her knees.

Krell was bleeding from his nose.

Rhyse had fired twice into the ceiling.

Vhal was reciting corrupted checksum prayers.

Both Variks were screaming.

Vale remained standing in the hatch.

He had one hand on the frame.

The impossible Deck Twenty-Seven behind him flickered through a dozen architectures.

"I know that one," he said.

Marr forced herself upright.

"What?"

"That failure."

He pointed at the Collector.

"It keeps trying to close the work order."

The slate chimed.

A new item appeared.

DISCONTINUITY REPAIR.

LOCATION: DOCK FOURTEEN / DECK TWENTY-SEVEN / UNRESOLVED.

FAULT: VENUE RECONCILIATION IN PROGRESS.

PRIORITY: ABSOLUTE.

ASSIGNED: EDRIN VALE.

Vale sighed.

"Of course it is."

He reached for the slate.

Marr held it back.

"If you take this through the hatch, do we lose the evidence?"

The adjudicator said, "Possibly."

Vale said, "If I don't take it, I can't do the job."

Krell looked at Marr.

"Can we make a copy?"

Every person in the room turned toward him.

Krell frowned.

"What?"

Marr smiled slowly.

"Captain, that may be the most intelligent thing you have said all week."

"I resent the qualification."

Vhal extended a data-spike.

The adjudicator raised a hand.

"A copy risks reconciliation."

Marr shook her head.

"Not a copy. Copies agree."

She looked at Vhal.

"Make seven. Introduce transcription errors. Different formats. Different timestamps. Remove random fields. Add fields that never existed."

Vhal's optics pulsed.

"Deliberate data corruption."

"Distributed evidentiary preservation."

"Ah."

Krell muttered, "That sounds more expensive."

Seven slates were prepared.

One listed 4,118,009 open jobs.

One listed 4,118,010.

One insisted Vale was named Edrin Vail.

One had no dates.

One had too many.

One translated every priority code into ecclesiastical feast days.

The seventh contained only the work orders that contradicted all six others.

The brass automaton stamped each.

EVIDENCE RECEIVED.

EVIDENCE DISPUTED.

EVIDENCE RECEIVED.

EVIDENCE DISPUTED.

By the fifth slate, the Collector had vanished from the glass.

Vale took the original.

The hatch opened wider.

Behind him, Deck Twenty-Seven stabilized just enough to reveal something at the far end.

A maintenance door.

Red paint.

Ancient hinges.

A small inspection window.

Something moved behind it.

Vale's face lost what little color remained.

"That door shouldn't be there."

Marr stepped to the threshold.

"Which Antegra does it belong to?"

"None of them."

The elderly adjudicator stood abruptly.

For the first time since appearing, she looked afraid.

"Close the venue."

Marr turned.

"You said not to close the hatch."

"Circumstances have changed."

"How?"

The naval officer behind her drew his sword.

Not a ceremonial blade.

A powered weapon.

"That door is not evidence from Antegra."

Old Varik stared through the hatch.

"Then where is it from?"

The adjudicator looked at him.

"From before the petition."

The red maintenance door opened.

Not outward.

Not inward.

It became open.

Beyond it was darkness.

Not the darkness of an unlit room.

The darkness of a place for which light had not yet been specified.

Vale's slate chimed.

A new work order appeared.

He read it.

Then read it again.

"Marr."

"What?"

He held the slate toward her.

DISCONTINUITY REPAIR.

LOCATION: ORIGIN.

FAULT: PETITIONER EXISTS PRIOR TO PETITION.

PRIORITY: ABSOLUTE.

ASSIGNED: EDRIN VALE.

STATUS: OVERDUE 71 YEARS.

Marr looked at the darkness beyond the red door.

The Collector did not appear in the glass.

That frightened her more than if it had.

The brass automaton printed a final strip.

FIRST HEARING COMMENCED.

WITNESS: EDRIN VALE.

VENUE: DISPUTED.

RESPONDENT: ABSENT.

Then, beneath it:

ADDITIONAL RESPONDENT REQUESTS RECOGNITION.

Marr stared at the words.

Krell raised his pistol again.

"Is that bad?"

The elderly adjudicator backed away from the hatch.

"Captain," she said, "the Collector was never the first thing here."

From beyond the red door came the sound of somebody stamping paperwork.

Once.

Twice.

Then a third time.

Edrin Vale closed his eyes.

"I hate inherited jobs."

The darkness answered with a fourth stamp.
