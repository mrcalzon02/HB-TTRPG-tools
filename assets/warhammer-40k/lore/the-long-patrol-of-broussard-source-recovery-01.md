# The Long Patrol of Broussard

## Source Recovery 01 — I. THE LONG PATROL

Broussard liked to think of himself as a man of action, justice, integrity, and all the other noble virtues the Imperial Guard was fond of engraving upon monuments after everyone involved was safely dead.

He had been trained as an officer of the Imperial Guard and, through a combination of competence, stubbornness, regulation, and the occasional professionally engineered misfortune of someone more deserving of demotion, had risen steadily through the ranks.

Not rapidly. Rapid promotion in the Guard was generally a sign that either everyone above you had been killed or someone intended for you to be.

Broussard preferred the slower method.

He had become something of a champion of Imperial regulation, which sounded considerably less heroic than charging a traitor emplacement with a chainsword but had, in practical terms, saved considerably more lives. He possessed an almost priestly knowledge of deployment statutes, transportation codes, readiness requirements, environmental safety mandates, Munitorum provisioning regulations, and several obscure appendices whose authors had probably died centuries before Broussard's grandparents had been born.

His men loved him for it.

Not because any guardsman naturally loved paperwork. There were limits even to Imperial indoctrination.

They loved him because Broussard had discovered that, properly wielded, bureaucracy could occasionally stop bullets.

Many of the soldiers beneath him had attended the same training class in which Broussard had first learned the subtle distinction between command presence and standing in front of a formation while desperately trying to remember what came next. They had witnessed his early fumbles, his occasional catastrophic misunderstanding of parade-ground etiquette, and the memorable afternoon when he had accidentally saluted a maintenance servitor.

The servitor had not returned the salute.

This had apparently bothered Broussard more than anyone else.

But those same men had also watched him grow into the uniform.

They had seen him learn that military honor was not merely dying beautifully when someone important made a stupid decision.

Sometimes honor consisted of ensuring the stupid decision never reached your soldiers.

And Broussard had become extremely good at that.

Their regiment had avoided more than one deployment which, in retrospect, would almost certainly have ended with the unit being reduced to a paragraph in somebody else's after-action report.

They had heard the stories from other detachments.

They had seen the men who came back.

An escort platoon once assigned to assist the red-robed priests of Mars had returned from a six-week industrial containment operation with less than half its original complement and considerably fewer legs.

The Mechanicus facility had suffered a minor containment leak.

"Minor," in this case, meant that the industrial sewers beneath the complex had filled with corrosive biomud sufficiently acidic to dissolve combat boots, flak armor, human flesh, and apparently several varieties of maintenance servitor.

The surviving guardsmen had been congratulated for their service.

The regiment's quartermaster had subsequently received an invoice for the missing boots.

Another detachment sent to reinforce security at the planetary governor's royal facilities had fared worse.

Several returned under armed escort.

Several did not return at all.

The official charges had included treason, sedition, unlawful observation of restricted activities, violation of gubernatorial privacy, and one particularly impressive accusation involving "malicious spatial positioning."

The soldiers had been little more than disposable hands, feet, eyes, and ears at the Governor's Palace. Useful until those eyes happened to see something. Useful until those ears happened to hear something. Useful until a man took the wrong corridor at precisely the wrong moment and discovered that His Excellency's commitment to traditional family values apparently possessed several confidential exemptions.

Palaces were dangerous places.

Not because of assassins.

Assassins were straightforward.

The danger was doors.

Open the wrong one and a perfectly ordinary guardsman could become a state secret.

Broussard therefore cultivated an almost religious affection for standard patrol assignments.

Patrol duty was boring.

Patrol duty was repetitive.

Patrol duty involved standing in miserable weather while absolutely nothing happened.

It was wonderful.

It kept his men honest. It kept them useful. It kept them reasonably pure. Most importantly, it kept them far away from the sticky entanglements produced whenever the Imperium's military, aristocracy, priesthood, bureaucracy, industrial caste, and secret police were forced to occupy the same building.

High Presidio had more than enough of those institutions already.

The planetary capital sat upon a world whose geography and climate encouraged civilization to collect itself into a relatively small number of large coastal cities. Vast stretches of the planet remained sparsely inhabited, and for roughly half the local year most of its oceans froze solid.

This complicated trade.

High Presidio possessed an enormous ocean-based commercial economy, which meant its merchants had been forced to solve the minor inconvenience of their principal transportation network periodically becoming a continent.

They had solved it rather elegantly.

The planet possessed a remarkably mature domestic skimmer industry. Machines ranged from tiny personal float-cars to enormous freight carriers capable of hauling thousands of tonnes across the planetary oceans.

During summer they behaved much like ordinary ships, their gravitic systems reducing displacement and allowing their hulls to skim across the water with surprising efficiency.

Winter changed matters.

When the seas froze, those same vessels simply continued moving.

The hull rose.

The water disappeared beneath kilometers of ice.

And the ships became land vehicles.

For months at a time enormous commercial convoys crawled across the frozen ocean, freighters gliding several meters above the ice while escort vehicles travelled alongside them.

It was, Broussard had always thought, an admirably practical solution to an absurd planetary problem.

Unfortunately, the planet's wildlife had also adapted.

And unlike the engineers of High Presidio, the wildlife did not possess regulatory committees.

The creatures beneath the ice had spent millions of years dealing with the seasonal transformation of their world. For half the year the ocean surface was a permeable boundary between water and atmosphere.

Then winter came and someone put a roof over the entire sea.

Evolution had responded enthusiastically.

Many of the native creatures still required atmospheric oxygen. Some surfaced through natural fissures. Others maintained breathing holes. Others migrated.

And others had evolved the considerably more direct strategy of attacking the ice until it stopped being ice.

Broussard remembered learning about them in school.

Much of High Presidio's wildlife supposedly descended from ancient Terran stock introduced during some forgotten age of colonization. That was what the educational pict-slates insisted.

Broussard had always considered this explanation suspicious.

Ancient Terra had apparently contained an astonishing variety of animals if every hideous thing humanity encountered on another world could be blamed upon it.

Take the snarloff.

The creature resembled what might happen if a whale and a hippopotamus had been assembled from memory by someone who deeply resented both.

It possessed a barrel-shaped body, enormous lungs, thick insulating blubber, stubby limbs, and a head that appeared specifically designed to settle arguments.

During winter, snarloffs migrated beneath the ice and periodically smashed their way through to breathe.

Then there were the seals.

There were hundreds of varieties.

Small seals.

Large seals.

Long seals.

Blind seals.

Armored seals.

Seals with external gill structures despite still requiring air, which Broussard considered a particularly cruel evolutionary joke.

And then there were the screwheads.

The males possessed enormous spiraling horns that grew outward from reinforced sections of skull. Older males developed increasingly large and elaborate structures until some possessed nearly a meter of twisted bone projecting from their faces.

The name was crude.

It was also difficult to improve upon.

During the winter migration the strongest males would locate thinning ice, accelerate upward, and throw themselves into a violent rotational charge.

The resulting impact was called the death tumble.

The animal struck the underside of the ice while twisting its entire body behind the horn. When successful, the horn bored upward, fractured the surface, and allowed the male to smash open a breathing hole.

The herd followed.

When unsuccessful, the animal usually died.

Sometimes immediately.

Sometimes several meters beneath the air it had almost reached.

Every summer their skeletons washed ashore.

Broussard remembered finding one as a boy.

There had not been much left.

Nothing remained dead for very long in High Presidio's oceans.

Crustalloids descended upon corpses in glittering masses. Decayvores followed. Tiny scavengers burrowed through hide while larger things stripped away muscle. Anything that stopped moving for more than several hours was rapidly and efficiently returned to the planetary food chain.

Nature, Broussard had learned, was exceptionally good at recycling.

The Departmento Munitorum could have learned something from it.

The peculiar annual rhythm had shaped nearly every oxygen-dependent species in the ocean.

Winter was death.

Spring was desperation.

Summer was abundance.

Autumn was panic.

When the first ice began to form, entire ecosystems reorganized themselves around survival.

Some animals moved beneath persistent openings maintained by geothermal currents.

Some migrated to coastal caverns.

Some burrowed into the seabed and reduced their metabolism almost to nothing.

Others crawled onto the ice.

Every autumn creatures hauled themselves from the water in desperate migrations toward land, sucking atmosphere into lungs never designed for prolonged exposure while competing for hibernation pits, thermal caves, or great communal nests dug into the snow.

The weak died.

The old died.

The unlucky died.

The slow died.

The stupid died.

The Imperial Creed insisted this was evidence of the Emperor's perfect ordering of creation.

Broussard privately thought it sounded suspiciously like Guard doctrine.

He had no idea why all of this had returned to him now.

Perhaps boredom.

Perhaps age.

Perhaps because there were only so many hours a man could spend staring through the vision slit of a Chimera before childhood biology lessons began resurfacing from wherever the brain stored information it had previously decided would never again be useful.

Outside, miles of frozen coastline slid past.

White ice stretched toward a gray horizon beneath a pale sun that seemed to regard the world as an administrative obligation.

The Chimera's engine growled beneath him.

His men sat around the compartment checking weapons, sleeping, playing cards, arguing quietly, or practicing that uniquely military discipline of remaining completely motionless for hours while technically being awake.

Broussard was in command of the escort detail.

One freight vessel.

One convoy segment.

One exceptionally long frozen ocean.

Their assignment was beautifully straightforward.

Follow the tanker.

Protect the tanker.

Kill anything attempting to eat the tanker.

Do not allow the tanker to sink.

File Form 88-Delta if the tanker sank anyway.

Broussard loved uncomplicated orders.

The vessel they escorted was enormous. It hovered above the ice with the slow certainty of a cathedral deciding to migrate. Its gravitic engines produced a constant bass vibration that could be felt through the hull of the Chimera even hundreds of meters away.

Behind it stretched other vehicles in staggered formation.

Freighters.

Fuel haulers.

Cargo skimmers.

Maintenance crawlers.

Civilian transports.

Military escorts.

The convoy extended for kilometers.

Every few minutes another vehicle emerged from blowing snow behind them or disappeared into it ahead.

The biggest danger came from below.

A man could travel these routes for a week and see nothing.

Two weeks if the Emperor particularly favored him.

But eventually something beneath the ice noticed the convoy.

The great beasts of High Presidio's oceans were accustomed to hunting by shadow, vibration, heat, and pressure.

A massive tanker moving slowly across the frozen sea produced all four.

Broussard had never personally spoken with one of the creatures and therefore could not confirm their thought process, but the prevailing theory among local fishermen was that the animals mistook the ships for surface whales.

The logic was difficult to dispute.

The great winter whales were enormous lumbering behemoths, mountains of blubber and muscle that hauled themselves across the ice using stubby forelimbs while keeping close to seasonal fractures and breathing fields.

From beneath, their silhouettes must have resembled the freighters.

Large.

Slow.

Warm.

Heavy.

Edible-looking.

Which was unfortunate for everyone involved.

The first warning was usually a vibration.

Then cracks.

Then something enormous struck the underside of the ice.

Broussard had seen pict-recordings.

Tentacles hundreds of meters long exploding upward through shattered plates of ice.

Great circular mouths opening beneath convoys.

Hooks and claws searching for purchase against hull plating.

Sometimes an animal would wrap itself around a tanker and simply try to pull.

When that happened, every escort unit in range poured fire into the opening.

Lasguns.

Autocannons.

Heavy bolters.

Missiles.

Anything available.

The objective was not usually to kill the animal.

Nobody was entirely certain some of them could be killed with standard Guard equipment.

The objective was to make the creature reconsider its lunch.

An admirable tactical principle applicable to wildlife, enemies of the Imperium, and junior officers seeking promotion.

Broussard watched the tanker through the narrow window.

Nothing moved beneath the ice.

Nothing exploded.

Nothing screamed over the vox.

A perfect patrol.

He settled deeper into the commander's seat.

His mind wandered again.

Screwheads.

Snarloffs.

Crustalloids.

Things that crawled onto the ice every winter because some ancient part of their biology demanded air.

It occurred to him that almost everything on this planet spent half its existence trapped beneath something trying to kill it and the other half desperately preparing for the next time it happened.

There was probably a sermon hidden in that somewhere.

The Ecclesiarchy would have found one.

Something about mankind struggling beneath the crushing ice of heresy until the Emperor's light provided the breathing hole.

There would be banners.

There would be candles.

Someone would sell devotional screwhead figurines.

Broussard smiled faintly.

Outside, the frozen coastline continued sliding past.

His men continued sleeping.

The tanker continued moving.

Everything was quiet.

Which, in his professional experience, meant that somewhere nearby the universe was preparing an objection.
