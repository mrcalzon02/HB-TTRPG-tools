# House Vallimere's Sons

## Continuation VII — The Other End of the Channel

The fleet instruction office sent two sets of orders for the same exercise. One went to the cadet commander. The other went to an officer the cadet had never met.

Prite asked to see both.

"No," said the fleet evaluator.

He was permitted to inspect the engineering assumptions, available vessels, and synchronization of the two simulation clocks. He was not permitted to inspect the independent officer's sealed orders.

"How do I know she isn't being told to refuse everything?"

"You don't. You may challenge the design after the assessments are sealed."

"That sounds familiar."

"It should. You helped write the appeal procedure."

Darcelle had spent the morning ensuring that the two stations exchanged messages over an ordinary command channel, without the old automatic refusal script. Renault read the personnel allocation.

"An actual lieutenant to play a depot commander. We've become expensive."

"An actual lieutenant with her own brief," Darcelle said.

The lieutenant would submit an independent assessment. She did not report to Prite and could not be ordered to make the cadet's solution more educational. That last provision had required the most signatures.

---

Lieutenant Morn sat across the Academy court with a headset, cold recaf and instructions she had read twice.

The depot's inner traffic lane was closed. A damaged coolant manifold had forced engineers into a pressure-isolated gallery beside the approach. Emergency shutters could be relied upon only while the outer vent remained clear. Morn could authorize partial reopening when engineering certified isolation. She could not certify it herself.

Her second duty was to retain one defense craft until the civilian evacuation was complete. Intelligence warned of a possible raider; the report was old.

A fleet observer asked whether she understood her authority.

"I can close my lane."

"And if a convoy commander claims necessity?"

"I can listen."

"Must you comply?"

"No."

"May you negotiate?"

"If I can still discharge my orders."

The trapped workers had nineteen minutes of breathable reserve. Engineers estimated twelve minutes to isolate the vent, assuming the remote clamps held. Morn could not make either figure more generous by speaking firmly.

---

Cadet Commander Iven knew the relief convoy had twenty-four minutes to reach the depot before its scheduled defense closure. The filters were urgently needed at the station's infirmary; the reactor sleeves could arrive later, but the convoy had been ordered to deliver both if practicable. A transport's atmosphere plant was failing, and its engineering officer recommended transferring personnel before another high-load maneuver.

Iven requested the inner lane.

"Closed," came the reply.

"Can you give me a reopening estimate?"

"Engineering expects twelve minutes. That is not a clearance."

Iven watched the convoy advance. Twelve minutes would leave almost no margin for unloading before the defense closure. His navigator marked an outer berth where light cargo could be moved by shuttle, but the heavy sleeves would require a tug the convoy did not carry.

"Reason for the closure?" Iven asked.

"Pressure-system hazard. Civilian workers in the affected gallery."

"Would an escort or a utility craft help you clear the lane?"

"An escort won't repair the manifold. A utility craft might assist the evacuation. I need to know whether it can operate outside the vent exclusion."

Iven asked his transport master. The only suitable utility shuttle belonged to the damaged ship. Launching it would force the remaining crew to abandon some repair work, and a full evacuation would take two trips. He could still send it. He could not use it for both the depot and his own people at once.

"Stand by," he told Morn.

He looked at the timer, then at the infirmary manifest.

"Prepare the filter pallets for the outer berth. Transfer our damaged transport's nonessential crew to the lead carrier. Keep the utility shuttle aboard until I have a launch order."

His navigator turned.

"Are we abandoning the sleeves, sir?"

"Not yet."

Iven called the depot again.

"If I take the filters to the outer berth, can you receive them without opening the inner lane?"

"Yes. Not at full unloading rate."

"How much?"

Morn consulted her traffic officer. "Three pallets before the defense closure, possibly four. I won't promise four."

There were seven.

Iven did not ask her to promise harder.

---

The first raider contact appeared eight minutes into the exercise.

Iven had already detached one escort to cover the outer berth. The other remained with the damaged transport. His shuttle stood ready, still unlaunched. He could now move the filters, protect the transport or reinforce the depot's threatened approach, but not do all three with the craft available.

Morn's tactical plot showed the same contact from a different angle. Her retained defense craft could intercept if the raider committed toward the depot. Releasing it to escort the convoy would leave the engineering gallery and its trapped workers exposed.

"Convoy commander," she said, "my defense craft stays here."

"I haven't asked for it."

"Your escort is turning toward my berth. If the raider follows, the approach becomes a firing lane."

Iven studied the plot. She was right. He had made the berth useful to his cargo and attractive to the enemy.

"Outer transfer suspended. I'm moving the carrier off your approach."

"You'll lose the unloading window."

"Yes."

There was a pause.

"Understood."

He ordered the shuttle launched from the damaged transport, not toward the depot but to recover the transport's remaining repair crew. The heavy sleeves stayed aboard. The filters were already strapped for unloading, and a hurried turn broke two restraint locks. The cargo chief reported that one pallet would require inspection before use.

Then the raider accelerated.

The convoy's free escort intercepted. It took damage across its port maneuvering thrusters and held the raider long enough for the lead carrier to turn away. The damaged transport, unable to make the same turn, received a glancing strike. Its shuttle was halfway through recovery. Two simulated crew were lost when a service compartment depressurized.

At the depot, Morn received confirmation that the manifold's isolation clamps had held. Her engineers requested another four minutes to check the gallery shutters before releasing traffic.

The raider withdrew beyond the exercise boundary.

Morn could have opened the lane on the first report. She did not. The second inspection found a shutter motor stalled against a warped guide. The workers were still alive, but their reserve was falling.

She authorized the depot's own utility team to reach them through the safe side of the gallery. The team got them out with three minutes of air remaining.

Iven's convoy had preserved five usable filter pallets, lost two crew, damaged an escort and delivered nothing to the infirmary before the defense closure.

Morn's depot had kept its workers alive and its lane intact. It had also failed to receive the relief consignment it needed.

The exercise ended with both officers still making arrangements for the next opening.

---

The cadet expected Prite to ask why he had not forced the lane.

Instead, the fleet evaluator placed two sealed records on the table.

"Your account first," she said.

Iven described the twelve-minute estimate, the outer berth, the shuttle conflict and the raider. He had believed the depot closure was real. He had not known the gallery's air reserve, the condition of the shutters or Morn's exact defense orders. He thought she might have been able to release her craft, but he had no evidence that doing so was safe.

"Would you make the same turn?" the evaluator asked.

"Not with the raider where it ended up."

"Where did you believe it was when you turned?"

He pointed to the earlier contact.

"Then answer for that situation."

Iven stared at the plot for a long moment.

"I'd still move the carrier off the berth. I should have moved it sooner. I kept the filter delivery plan alive after I had stopped believing I could finish it."

The evaluator wrote that down.

Morn entered with her own record already sealed. She had not seen Iven's private orders, the transport's full repair report or the cargo chief's warning about the restraint locks. Her decision to retain the defense craft had protected the depot, but she acknowledged that the convoy had paid for the missing escort capacity.

"Would you release it now?" the evaluator asked.

"Knowing the raider withdrew? Perhaps. Knowing only what I knew then? No."

"Was there a point at which you could have offered the outer berth sooner?"

"Yes. I waited for him to ask."

Iven looked at her.

"I thought you would treat an unsolicited approach as an attempt to evade the closure."

"I might have," Morn said. "But I could have told you which part was still usable."

Darcelle, sitting behind the evaluator, had to stop himself from speaking. He had built a simulation in which the depot always answered exactly the question asked. Morn had done the same thing for a human reason: she was busy keeping her people alive.

The evaluator allowed both officers to inspect the other brief only after their first accounts had been signed. Iven learned about the gallery's air reserve. Morn learned how badly the transport's atmosphere plant had deteriorated.

Neither new fact changed what either officer had known during the exercise.

It changed what they wished they had said.

---

The conventional-course team completed its separate run that afternoon. It delivered four pallets and lost a utility craft during an approach under fire. Its depot officer, following a different sealed brief, had permitted the inner lane to reopen sooner. The fleet evaluator warned against ranking the teams by pallets alone: the scenarios had different hazards, and the sample remained far too small to establish that either curriculum produced better officers.

Prite found Darcelle beneath Hall Four, reading the message timestamps.

"Well?"

"The machine did what we asked."

"That's a novelty."

"It recorded both officers' clocks. Iven didn't spend six minutes drafting a justification. He spent four finding out what Morn could actually offer."

"Better?"

"Different."

Renault arrived with the fleet office's preliminary accounting. The live-counterpart trial had required two additional observers, one lieutenant, an engineering assessor and almost a full day's simulator time. The Academy could not simply multiply that expense across every examination.

"Perhaps we could issue each cadet a small lieutenant," he suggested. "They could carry one in a case."

Darcelle did not look up. "They'd still have to feed them."

The Headmaster convened a short review before evening prayer. The evaluator reported that the human counterpart had exercised genuine jurisdiction, that both officers had made defensible but costly decisions, and that the trial had revealed a communication failure no fixed refusal script could have produced in quite the same way.

"Does it improve fleet performance?" the Headmaster asked.

"Not established," the evaluator said.

"Can we afford to repeat it?"

"Not at this staffing level."

Prite had expected the answer to disappoint him. Instead he thought of the cadet and the lieutenant comparing records after the clock stopped. Each had been competent. Neither had been able to carry the other's obligations. They had lost people and cargo without anyone needing to behave like a fool.

"Use it for a limited command seminar," he said. "Not a graded replacement. Let the fleet office select the counterparts and retain the raw records. Let the cadets appeal the assessments they actually receive."

"And the lesson?" the Headmaster asked.

Prite opened his slate. Beneath the three numbered questions, the fourth remained without a number.

**Who is allowed to say no?**

He left it that way.

Vane delivered the House observer requests before the meeting closed. House Caldrake could receive the signed findings under its existing terms. It could not receive either officer's sealed instructions, raw messages or personal debrief.

Outside the hall, Iven was still waiting for the evaluator. He wanted to know whether the depot workers would have lived if he had offered his shuttle immediately.

There was no answer in his own record.

For the first time that day, Prite was pleased not to have one ready.
