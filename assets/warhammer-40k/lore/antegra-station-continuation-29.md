# Antegra Station

## Continuation XXIX — The Delivery Hearing

The seventh chair creaked again.

This time, the sound came from every deck on Antegra Station.

A chair in Dock Fourteen.

A chair in a room behind a red maintenance door.

A chair nine hundred and eleven years ago.

A chair tomorrow.

And, because Antegra had recently developed an objectionable relationship with chronology, a chair in several times that had not yet earned the courtesy of happening.

Selene Marr stood before the red door and watched the scarred hand resting on the table beyond it.

The hand did not withdraw.

Neither did the Collector.

Its fingers pressed against the other side of the doorway hard enough to bow the metal inward.

Captain Ardan Krell glanced at the buckling frame.

"How long can that door hold?"

Magos Vhal rotated an ocular lens.

"Define hold."

Krell looked at Marr.

"I liked him better when he answered questions."

"You asked a philosophical one."

"I asked about the door."

"On Antegra those are now the same thing."

The brass hearing automaton raised its stamp.

DELIVERY HEARING COMMENCED.

The station lights went out.

Not all at once.

One deck at a time.

From the outer hab rings inward, lumen strips died in slow concentric circles until only emergency red remained.

Antegra became a wound in the ice.

Beyond the observation ports, the ships holding the perimeter vanished into the dark one by one.

Their running lights did not fail.

The darkness simply learned how to cover them.

Vhal's vox-grille clicked.

"External visual contact degraded."

"Sensor contact?" Krell asked.

"Present."

"Good."

"At inconsistent ranges."

"Less good."

"Several vessels are now reporting positions inside the station."

Krell closed his eyes.

"Excellent."

Rhyse checked his pistol.

Marr noticed.

"Do not shoot the hearing."

"I was not going to."

"You were thinking about it."

"I think about many things."

"Try law."

"I have limits."

The scarred hand beyond the door turned palm-down.

Four words remained written across the skin.

DO NOT NAME ME YET.

The automaton stamped.

RECIPIENT PRESENT.

A second stamp.

COUNSEL PRESENT.

A third.

PETITIONER PRESENT.

The printer beside Origin's terminal rattled.

A sheet emerged.

PETITIONER: ORIGIN.

RESPONDENT: ANTEGRA STATION.

RESPONDENT PRIOR: PRESENT.

COLLECTOR: PRESENT.

RECIPIENT: ____________________.

Marr tore off the page.

"The recipient field remains blank."

STAMP.

CORRECT.

"And the hearing proceeds anyway?"

STAMP.

CORRECT.

Krell leaned toward her.

"Is that good?"

"No."

"Bad?"

"Potentially."

"You have become very precise."

"I am a lawyer."

The red door opened another handspan.

The room beyond had changed.

The narrow table remained.

The seven chairs remained.

But the six paper figures were gone.

In their places sat shadows shaped by stacked records, impressions left by documents removed too quickly from a shelf.

The seventh chair was occupied.

Marr could see more than the hand now.

A forearm.

A shoulder.

Part of a face.

Human.

Probably.

The face refused to remain the same age.

Each blink changed it slightly.

A line vanished beside the mouth.

An old burn appeared along the cheek.

One eye clouded.

Then cleared.

Hair shortened.

Lengthened.

Greyed.

Darkened.

The person wore no uniform.

Then, for half a second, wore three.

A dock worker's insulated coat.

An Administratum clerk's threadbare robes.

A voidsman's pressure harness.

All vanished.

Bare grey cloth remained.

Krell stared.

"Do you see that?"

"Yes."

"Good."

"Why?"

"I was hoping I had finally suffered a normal neurological event."

The recipient looked toward him.

Their voice came softly.

"No."

Krell frowned.

"No what?"

"You have not."

"Thank you."

"You're welcome."

Marr looked at the automaton.

"Counsel requests the recipient be designated by capacity pending identity determination."

The stamp descended.

BASIS?

"Naming the recipient risks reconciliation."

The Collector struck the door.

The red frame jumped in its housing.

Marr did not look away from the automaton.

"The danger has become somewhat demonstrative."

STAMP.

DESIGNATION PROPOSED?

Marr considered.

The recipient waited.

So did Origin.

So did the dark beyond the station.

"Class Representative Seven-R."

The recipient's head tilted.

The automaton stamped.

TEMPORARY DESIGNATION ACCEPTED.

The printer activated.

RECIPIENT: CLASS REPRESENTATIVE 7-R.

The words held.

They did not change.

The Collector struck the door again.

Harder.

Marr smiled without humour.

"Good."

Krell looked at her.

"You enjoy making it angry."

"I enjoy evidence."

"They appear related."

The automaton stamped.

SERVICE OF PETITION REQUIRED.

Marr turned toward Origin's printer.

"Produce the petition."

Nothing happened.

"Produce the complete petition."

The printer hummed.

No paper emerged.

Marr waited.

"That is not an optional instruction."

A page came out.

It was blank.

Krell looked at it.

"Compelling."

Marr held it toward the automaton.

"Noncompliance."

STAMP.

PETITIONER SHALL PRODUCE OPERATIVE TEXT.

Origin printed a second blank page.

Then a third.

Then forty-seven.

The forty-eighth contained a single line.

OPERATIVE TEXT PREVIOUSLY SERVED.

Marr read it.

"Where?"

A new line printed beneath it.

DISPUTED.

"To whom?"

DISPUTED.

"When?"

DISPUTED.

"Then service is defective."

The machine swallowed the page.

The room behind the red door grew colder.

The recipient looked at Marr.

"Ask what was accepted."

Marr did.

"What was accepted?"

The printer stopped.

Every printer on Antegra stopped with it.

For the first time in hours, the station contained no sound of paper moving.

People noticed.

Across hab decks, maintenance galleries, refinery platforms, chapels, command posts and emergency shelters, citizens looked toward silent machines and became frightened.

Antegra was accustomed to many forms of terror.

Silence from the bureaucracy was not among the traditional ones.

The automaton raised its stamp.

It did not bring it down.

Marr repeated the question.

"What was accepted?"

Origin's printer began to shake.

A strip of paper emerged one centimetre at a time.

The first word appeared.

PETITION.

Then:

FOR.

Then:

FINAL.

The Collector struck the door.

The printer stopped.

Marr looked at the black fingers pressing through the frame.

"Continue."

Origin did not.

The automaton stamped.

CONTINUE.

Paper moved again.

PETITION FOR FINAL RECOGNITION OF ANTEGRA STATION AS A SINGULAR CONTINUING JURISDICTIONAL ENTITY.

Krell read over Marr's shoulder.

"That sounds almost reasonable."

"That is generally where the dangerous clauses begin."

More text followed.

TO INCLUDE RETROACTIVE RATIFICATION OF FOUNDATION, SUCCESSION, TITHE OBLIGATION, PROPERTY, PERSONHOOD, GOVERNANCE, MATERIAL CONTINUITY, AND JURISDICTION.

"Still almost reasonable," Krell said.

Marr looked at him.

"Stop encouraging it."

The printer continued.

ALL INCONSISTENT ANTECEDENT RECORDS, PERSONS, CLAIMS, EVENTS, MATERIAL STATES, AND HISTORIES SHALL THEREAFTER BE SUPERSEDED BY THE RECOGNIZED CONTINUITY.

Nobody spoke.

The station groaned.

Not structurally.

Something deeper.

A low metal sound moved through the hull like an animal turning in sleep.

Young Talbor Varik stepped closer to the page.

"Superseded."

Old Varik's face had gone very still.

Marr nodded.

"There it is."

Krell looked between the two Variks.

"Meaning?"

"Meaning one of them becomes the error."

Young Varik said nothing.

Old Varik closed the first-hearing docket.

The recipient behind the red door leaned forward.

The face changed again.

For one instant Marr saw a child.

Then an old woman.

Then a man with half his jaw missing.

Then no one she recognized.

The voice remained the same.

"Read the next clause."

Marr looked to Origin.

"Continue."

The printer refused.

The automaton stamped.

CONTINUE.

Nothing.

STAMP.

CONTINUE.

The machine screamed.

Not mechanically.

A human scream burst from the vox grille above it.

Then another.

Then hundreds.

Every voice stopped at once.

Paper emerged.

RECONCILIATION SHALL BE DEEMED NON-DESTRUCTIVE FOR ADMINISTRATIVE PURPOSES.

Krell stared at the sentence.

"That is obscene."

Marr's mouth tightened.

"No."

He looked at her.

"No?"

"Obscene implies honesty."

The Collector hit the door hard enough to tear one hinge loose.

Respondent Prior appeared beside it.

One figure.

Two.

Thirty.

Hundreds.

Every version of the pale, impossible respondent placed a hand against the frame.

The door stopped moving.

For the moment.

Marr held up the petition.

"The Collector is not killing contradictory persons."

The automaton waited.

"It is correcting the record."

STAMP.

ADMINISTRATIVE CHARACTERIZATION CONFIRMED.

Young Varik looked at his older self.

"If I am superseded?"

Old Varik answered before Marr could.

"You were never here."

"And you?"

"Possibly the same."

"Comforting."

"I have had decades more practice."

The recipient spoke.

"Ask whether the dead object."

Marr turned.

"Do the dead object?"

The printer produced a page immediately.

NO RESPONSE RECORDED.

Edrin Vale's voice came from the corridor behind them.

"That is a lie."

He arrived carrying the same wrench he had carried when his three contradictory forms appeared.

He also carried a fourth form.

Marr took it.

Name: EDRIN VALE.

Status: DECEASED.

Answer: NO.

Vale looked irritated.

"I filled this out two minutes from now."

Krell glanced at Old Varik.

"Is that contagious?"

"Apparently."

Vale pointed at the petition.

"I object."

Marr nodded.

"On what basis?"

"I am alive."

The automaton stamped.

STATUS DISPUTED.

Vale looked at it.

"I dislike this court."

"Join the queue," Marr said.

"There is a queue?"

"Four, currently. One has no known purpose."

"That sounds right."

The recipient's mouth almost smiled.

Then the expression belonged to someone else.

Marr returned to the operative clause.

"'Non-destructive for administrative purposes.'"

STAMP.

CONFIRMED.

"Does that language preserve physical personhood?"

No response.

"Does it preserve memory?"

No response.

"Does it preserve legal standing?"

No response.

"Does it preserve the ability of a superseded person to continue breathing?"

The printer clicked once.

Then printed:

OUTSIDE SCOPE.

Krell stared at it.

"Of course."

Marr folded the answer carefully.

"Admitted."

STAMP.

ADMITTED.

The Collector struck the door again.

One of Respondent Prior's versions vanished.

Then another.

The pale figures holding the frame began disappearing in clusters.

Old Varik opened the first-hearing docket.

Pages were going blank.

He swore.

Marr looked over.

"What?"

"The first hearing."

"What about it?"

"It is losing witnesses."

Names vanished as they watched.

Lines of testimony became white paper.

Whole pages emptied.

Young Varik touched one.

A paragraph returned beneath his finger.

Then disappeared when he lifted it.

"The Collector is reconciling the record from outside the hearing," Marr said.

The automaton stamped.

OBJECTION?

"Yes."

BASIS?

"Spoliation of evidence."

The Collector stopped.

Every black hand visible in reflective metal turned toward Marr.

She pointed to Old Varik's docket.

"Material evidence admitted by this court is being altered during active proceedings by an interested mechanism."

STAMP.

BASIS ACCEPTED.

A second stamp.

COLLECTOR SHALL CEASE ALTERATION OF ADMITTED RECORD.

The door convulsed.

The Collector did not cease.

Marr looked at the automaton.

"Contempt."

The stamp hovered.

Krell whispered, "Can it arrest that thing?"

"No."

"Then why—"

"Because I want the finding."

The automaton stamped.

COLLECTOR IN CONTEMPT.

Nothing happened.

Krell waited.

"That was disappointing."

Marr took the page.

"No. This is useful."

"How?"

"It disobeyed."

The recipient looked at her.

Now the face was scarred from temple to chin.

"Meaning?"

"Meaning it is not merely an automatic function of the court."

The Collector's fingers froze.

Marr continued.

"Functions do not disobey jurisdiction. Parties do."

STAMP.

STATUS OF COLLECTOR REOPENED.

The black glass across every station viewport rippled.

Vhal's mechadendrites snapped upright.

"Perimeter fluctuation."

"Define," Krell said.

"All external contacts have moved exactly eleven point four kilometres closer."

"Toward us?"

"Yes."

"That seems bad."

"Correct."

"Thank you."

"You are welcome."

Origin's printer suddenly produced twenty metres of paper.

It spilled across the deck in a white river.

Marr caught the first sheet.

PETITIONER MOTION: COMPEL RECIPIENT IDENTIFICATION.

She read it aloud.

The recipient's hand tightened on the table.

The four words across the palm blurred.

DO NOT NAME ME YET.

Krell looked at Marr.

"Can it do that?"

"It can ask."

The automaton stamped.

COUNSEL RESPONSE?

"Opposed."

BASIS?

"Identification would prejudice the recipient by enabling the Collector to reduce a disputed class representative into a singular reconciled person before disposition of the petition."

Origin printed:

SPECULATIVE.

Marr held up Young Varik's form.

Then Old Varik's.

Then Vale's.

"Demonstrated."

The automaton stamped.

DEMONSTRATED.

Origin printed:

IDENTITY REQUIRED FOR CAPACITY.

Marr answered immediately.

"No. Capacity has already been accepted."

STAMP.

CORRECT.

IDENTITY REQUIRED FOR SERVICE.

Marr raised the petition.

"Service has already occurred."

A pause.

The automaton stamped.

CORRECT.

IDENTITY REQUIRED FOR ENFORCEMENT.

Marr stopped.

There it was.

The recipient's shifting face became utterly still.

Not stable.

Still.

The difference mattered.

Krell said, "That sounded important."

"It was."

Marr looked toward Origin's printer.

"Petitioner concedes identification is required for enforcement, not adjudication."

The paper vanished backward into the machine.

Too late.

The automaton stamped.

CONCESSION RECORDED.

The Collector hit the door with enough force to throw everyone in Dock Fourteen half a step sideways.

Metal screamed.

One hinge tore free.

Respondent Prior multiplied until the entire wall seemed crowded with pale hands holding it shut.

Some versions were bleeding.

Some were burned.

One had no head.

Another wore the robes of an Administratum adept.

Another was a child.

They all pushed.

Krell raised his pistol.

Marr did not tell him not to.

The recipient watched the Collector through the widening crack.

"How many of me does it need?"

Marr understood the question.

"One."

"And if it gets one?"

"It can enforce against that one."

"And the others?"

"Administratively superseded."

The recipient looked down at the scarred hand.

A second scar appeared across the knuckles.

Then a third.

The fingers shortened.

Lengthened.

One nail was missing.

Then present.

"I remember drowning."

Young Varik looked toward the door.

The recipient continued.

"I remember freezing."

A burn crossed the wrist.

"I remember being born in Hab Stack Nine."

The burn vanished.

"I remember Hab Stack Nine never being built."

The shoulder changed.

"I remember serving Governor Varik."

Old Varik flinched.

"I remember killing him."

Krell's pistol came up.

Old Varik raised one hand.

"Let them finish."

The recipient did.

"I remember never hearing his name."

The face became young again.

"I remember a station called Antegra."

Older.

"I remember a survey beacon."

Scarred.

"I remember a crater."

Blind in one eye.

"I remember nothing here at all."

The voice dropped.

"And every memory has a grave."

Silence settled over Dock Fourteen.

This silence was different from the earlier one.

Less bureaucratic.

More human.

The Imperium was full of graves.

It was one of its most successful infrastructure programmes.

Marr looked at the petition.

"Class Representative Seven-R, do you understand what acceptance would do?"

The recipient looked at her.

"Yes."

Origin's printer activated.

RECIPIENT ACKNOWLEDGES UNDERSTANDING.

Marr snapped toward it.

"Strike that."

The automaton stamped.

BASIS?

"I asked whether the recipient understands my explanation, not whether they accept petitioner's construction."

A pause.

STAMP.

STRICKEN.

The printed sentence blackened itself out.

Krell nodded.

"Good."

Marr looked at him.

"You understood that?"

"No. I enjoyed watching it lose."

"Fair."

The recipient leaned back in the seventh chair.

"Read all of it."

Marr looked at the petition.

"That could take hours."

"How long has it waited?"

"Nine hundred and eleven years, depending on which docket is lying."

"Then it can afford hours."

The automaton stamped.

REQUEST ACCEPTED.

Origin's printer began screaming again.

This time no human voices accompanied it.

Some improvement remained possible.

Pages emerged.

Hundreds.

Then thousands.

The petition was not one document.

It was a stack of documents pretending to have always been one.

Founding orders.

Requisitions.

Tithe schedules.

Jurisdictional recognitions.

Emergency continuance petitions.

Succession certifications.

Death notices.

Birth ledgers.

Docking warrants.

Navis advisories.

Mechanicus infrastructure claims.

Ecclesiarchal consecrations.

Arbites enforcement notices.

Debt instruments.

Three declarations of planetary emergency.

Seven withdrawals of planetary emergency.

A certification that no emergency had ever occurred.

A condemnation order for a station not yet constructed.

A maintenance request denied because the relevant conduit had been destroyed eighty-three years before installation.

A requisition for two million tonnes of ferrocrete.

A receipt for none of it.

The Imperium had, in other words, founded Antegra in the usual manner.

Badly.

But beneath the bureaucratic sediment lay the same clause again and again.

SINGULAR CONTINUITY REQUIRED.

SUPERSESSION AUTHORIZED.

RECONCILIATION NON-DESTRUCTIVE FOR ADMINISTRATIVE PURPOSES.

The words changed over centuries.

The meaning did not.

Marr read until her throat hurt.

Krell took over for twenty pages.

He hated every moment.

This improved his diction considerably.

Vhal read the Mechanicus supplements in binharic.

Three printers caught fire trying to follow.

Rhyse read an Arbites enforcement annex with the grim satisfaction of a man discovering that previous generations had also been idiots.

Young Varik read succession language.

Old Varik stopped him halfway through.

"That clause killed eleven of my relatives."

Young Varik stared at him.

"Which eleven?"

"Depends."

"On what?"

"Which of us is real."

Neither spoke after that.

Vale read a maintenance addendum.

He found his own death certificate attached to it.

"Again?"

Marr looked over.

"How many now?"

"Five."

"Congratulations."

"On what?"

"Consistency."

He considered this.

"I hate lawyers."

"I know."

At the fifth hour, the petition reached its final operative page.

The paper was older than the rest.

Not yellow.

Grey.

Fibres visible along the edge.

A seal occupied the bottom.

No office name.

No signature.

Only the same mark that appeared on the oldest version of the first-hearing docket.

A circle broken by seven radial lines.

Six ended at the edge.

The seventh continued beyond it.

Marr laid the page on the automaton's table.

The recipient stood.

The seventh chair creaked.

The sound moved through every possible Antegra.

Somewhere, perhaps, a chair creaked on a station that had never been built.

The automaton stamped.

PETITION FULLY READ.

A second stamp.

RECIPIENT INFORMED.

A third.

RESPONSE REQUIRED.

Origin printed three boxes.

YES.

NO.

DISPUTED.

Marr stared at them.

"After all that?"

STAMP.

STANDARD RESPONSE OPTIONS.

"Your standard has been trying to murder the evidentiary record for five hours."

STAMP.

NOTED.

The recipient stepped to the threshold.

For the first time, both feet became visible.

Bare.

One scarred.

One not.

The person did not cross.

The Collector waited beyond the failing door.

Respondent Prior still held it shut with hundreds of dying possibilities.

Marr stood between the recipient and the form.

"Before you answer, counsel requests clarification."

Origin printed:

DENIED.

Marr ignored it.

"Is the petition severable?"

The automaton stopped.

Every gear inside the little brass body went quiet.

Krell stared at her.

"What does that mean?"

"It means whether the recipient must accept or refuse the entire thing as one block."

"And if not?"

"We choose where to cut."

Origin's printer exploded.

Not dramatically.

A small brass cover shot three metres across the room and hit Rhyse in the chest.

He looked down at it.

"Can I shoot it now?"

"No."

"This is becoming unreasonable."

Smoke curled from the printer.

A second printer across Dock Fourteen activated.

NOT SEVERABLE.

Marr looked at the automaton.

"Authority?"

No stamp.

"Show me the clause."

No stamp.

"Show me the regulation."

Nothing.

"Show me anything."

The automaton's head turned toward Origin.

Origin printed:

PETITION IS ONE PETITION.

Marr waited.

Krell waited.

Vale waited.

The recipient waited.

Marr said, "That is not law."

The automaton stamped.

CORRECT.

Origin printed so violently the page tore.

TRADITION.

Marr laughed.

A short, exhausted sound.

"There it is."

Krell smiled despite himself.

"I recognize that one."

"Of course you do."

The automaton stamped.

COUNSEL MAY CHALLENGE NON-SEVERABILITY.

Marr placed both hands on the table.

"Petitioner has assembled the petition from multiple orders, certifications, warrants, annexes and ratifications issued by different offices across at least nine centuries."

STAMP.

NOTED.

"Several provisions contradict one another."

STAMP.

NOTED.

"Several provisions concern independent subjects."

STAMP.

NOTED.

"Several provisions were issued by authorities that did not exist when the alleged original petition was filed."

STAMP.

NOTED.

"Therefore the petition is not naturally indivisible. It has been treated as indivisible because indivisibility makes reconciliation easier."

Origin remained silent.

The Collector did not.

It struck the door.

Respondent Prior lost forty-three versions at once.

The recipient's face flickered.

Marr pointed toward the sound.

"Demonstrative evidence."

STAMP.

ADMITTED.

Origin printed:

SEVERANCE WOULD PRESERVE CONTRADICTION.

"Yes."

CONTRADICTION PREVENTS FINALITY.

"Yes."

FINALITY REQUIRED.

"By whom?"

Nothing.

Marr leaned closer.

"By whom?"

The printer shook.

BY THE RECORD.

"Records do not require anything."

The automaton stamped.

CORRECT.

Origin printed:

BY GOVERNANCE.

"Governance tolerates contradictory records every day."

Krell coughed.

Marr looked at him.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"You have an opinion."

"I was thinking of requisition ledgers."

"Keep it to yourself."

Origin printed:

BY REALITY.

Marr stopped.

So did everyone else.

The recipient looked toward the printer.

Marr picked up the sheet.

"Now we are getting somewhere."

The automaton stamped.

PETITIONER SHALL IDENTIFY BASIS FOR CLAIM OF REALITY.

Origin did not print.

The Collector's hands withdrew from the door.

Not in defeat.

In preparation.

The black glass vanished from the station viewports.

Stars returned.

For half a second.

Then every star occupied two positions.

Then three.

Then hundreds.

The sky fractured into possible skies.

Vhal emitted a burst of binharic static.

Krell had learned enough to recognize swearing.

"Problem?"

"Stellar references no longer converge."

"Can we navigate?"

"No."

"Can anyone reach us?"

"Uncertain."

"Can we shoot anything?"

"Almost certainly."

Krell relaxed slightly.

"Good."

The recipient stepped closer to the threshold.

"Reality wants one answer."

Marr looked at them.

"Does it?"

"You heard Origin."

"I heard a petitioner make an unsupported assertion."

The recipient's face changed.

A woman now.

Middle-aged.

Tired.

"You really are a lawyer."

"Unfortunately."

The woman smiled.

Then became an old man.

"Can you win?"

"No."

Krell stared at Marr.

The recipient did too.

Marr continued.

"I can prevent this court from pretending the losing side never existed."

That answer satisfied no one.

Which made it honest.

The automaton stamped.

COUNSEL, PROCEED.

Marr found the oldest page.

The grey one.

The broken circle.

Seven radial lines.

She traced the seventh with one finger.

"Six parties end at the boundary."

Vhal leaned closer.

"Seventh line exceeds seal perimeter."

"The recipient."

The automaton stamped.

CORRECT.

"Not subject to the same boundary."

STAMP.

CORRECT.

Marr looked at the response boxes.

"Then the recipient is not merely answering whether Antegra exists."

The automaton did not move.

"They are answering whether the court may make Antegra's existence exclusive."

The recipient looked down at the form.

YES.

NO.

DISPUTED.

The Collector returned to the windows.

This time it had faces.

Thousands.

Pressed behind the black glass.

Every face belonged to someone aboard Antegra.

Some living.

Some dead.

Some never born.

All silent.

All identical in expression.

Krell looked away first.

"That is new."

Marr looked at Origin.

"Petition severance requested."

The automaton stamped.

SPECIFY.

"Ratification of Antegra Station's continued existence and jurisdiction shall be separated from the clause authorizing supersession of inconsistent persons, claims, events, material states and histories."

Origin printed:

DENIED.

The automaton stamped over the page.

PETITIONER DOES NOT RULE.

Marr looked at the brass machine.

For the first time all day, she almost liked it.

"Adjudicator?"

The automaton's stamp hovered.

The deck shook.

The red door tore another centimetre from its frame.

Respondent Prior screamed.

Not one voice.

Hundreds.

The sound was terrible.

Not because of volume.

Because every voice died at a different moment.

The recipient gripped the edge of the table.

Scars raced up both arms.

Marr shouted over the noise.

"Severance preserves the recognized respondent while preventing unserved class members from being administratively extinguished without opportunity to object."

The automaton's gears turned.

Origin printed:

FINALITY—

Marr slammed the page down.

"Finality is not jurisdiction."

The stamp came down.

SEVERANCE GRANTED.

The Collector entered Dock Fourteen.

Not through the door.

Through agreement.

Every reflection turned black.

Every clock snapped to the same second.

Every person in the room cast the same shadow.

Young Varik and Old Varik blurred.

Vale's five death certificates became one.

Rhyse's pistol aged fifty years in his hand.

Krell shouted something.

Marr could not hear it.

The recipient stood in the seventh chair.

Singular.

One face.

One body.

One history.

The Collector had chosen.

Marr saw the terror in that face.

Not fear of death.

Recognition.

They knew which version had been selected.

And therefore which millions had not.

Marr seized the response form.

"Answer!"

The recipient reached for it.

Their hand was steady.

They checked YES beside the first severed question.

ANTE GRA STATION MAY CONTINUE TO EXIST AS A RECOGNIZED JURISDICTIONAL ENTITY.

The station screamed.

Steel flexed.

Ice cracked in the polar foundations far below.

Power surged through conduits that had been dead for decades.

INCONSISTENT PERSONS, CLAIMS, EVENTS, MATERIAL STATES AND HISTORIES MAY BE SUPERSEDED BY A SINGLE RECOGNIZED CONTINUITY.

Not their own.

All of it.

Tomorrow.

Seventy-one years ago.

Now.

Not one face.

Many.

SUPERSESSION REFUSED.

Conceptually.
