# The Long Patrol of Broussard

## V. HARD LESSONS

**Author:** Mrcalzon02 / Christopher Vardeman  
**Based on the work of:** *The Long Patrol of Broussard*

*An original non-commercial Warhammer 40,000 fan chronicle. Warhammer 40,000 and associated marks and setting elements are the property of Games Workshop. This fan work is not endorsed by or affiliated with Games Workshop.*

The next several hours were, to Broussard's mild surprise, rather pleasant.


This bothered him at first.


Pleasant things in the Imperial Guard were rarely free.


They generally turned out to be either clerical errors or the opening stages of an ambush.


Still, as the detachment pushed out from the Saint Malderson and settled into its long drive across the ice, nothing immediately tried to kill them, the vehicles remained mechanically sound, and Commissar Gabriel Hamden Jalesthesian proved to be considerably less oppressive company than his uniform implied.


That alone took some adjustment.


The command Chimera rolled at the center of the small column, tracks grinding steadily across the frozen surface while the recovery hauler lumbered behind them. Snow rattled against the hull. The engine's vibration filled the troop compartment with a constant metallic hum. Every so often the vehicle rocked across a pressure seam in the ice and some unsecured piece of equipment reminded everyone that gravity remained doctrinally loyal to the Emperor.


Jalesthesian sat among the command crew without ceremony.


Not above them.


Not apart.


Among them.


Broussard noticed this immediately.


Commissars he had known before tended to occupy space as if the room itself had been ordered to stand at attention.


Gabriel simply found an empty seat.


He asked names.


Remembered them.


Asked where men had grown up.


Remembered that too.


He spoke with the driver through the intercom, traded remarks with the gunner, listened to Hessel tell an unnecessarily detailed story about a woman in South Regilia and correctly identified the precise point at which Hessel had begun lying.


This earned him considerable respect.


"She was an officer's daughter," Hessel insisted.


"She was a laundry clerk."


"Administrative officer's daughter."


"Her father cleaned boilers."


"Government boilers."


Jalesthesian smiled.


"Then you have my apologies. Clearly a woman of distinguished lineage."


The compartment laughed.


Broussard watched carefully.


Not because the conversation concerned him.


Because this was, in its own way, an inspection.


The commissar was familiarizing himself with the crew.


Their personalities.


Their relationships.


Who joked with whom.


Who deferred to whom.


Who spoke too much.


Who said nothing.


Who possessed the sort of confidence built upon competence and who possessed the sort built upon never yet having suffered appropriate consequences.


A competent political officer did not need to interrogate men individually.


He merely needed to listen.


Broussard respected the method.


Perhaps because he used much the same one.


Eventually the conversation turned toward the Schola.


It began innocently enough.


One of the vox operators asked what the Regilian Third Continental Schola had been like in its first years.


Gabriel leaned back against the vibrating hull.


"Cold."


Someone laughed.


"I'm serious. Half the heating system wasn't finished when we arrived."


"Imperial efficiency," Hessel said.


"Absolutely. The dormitory blocks existed. The plumbing mostly existed. The instructors existed in overwhelming numbers. Heat was apparently scheduled for a later phase of construction."


"How later?"


"Second winter."


That received a sympathetic groan.


"So what did you do?" one of the crewmen asked.


"We became extremely devout."


Broussard looked over.


Gabriel continued.


"Not spiritually. Practically. The chapel had functioning heaters."


That earned another laugh.


"The priests thought their sermons were inspiring record attendance."


"Were they?"


"No."


"Did you tell them?"


"We wanted to stay warm."


"Good call."


Gabriel nodded.


"Faith flourished."


The stories continued.


Some were absurd.


Cadets stealing extra blankets and discovering afterward that the blankets belonged to a visiting Tempestus delegation.


An entire dormitory being punished because someone had replaced a devotional recording with marching music played backward.


A trainee attempting to hide contraband sweets inside the hollow base of a statue of Saint Drusus and then discovering, during inspection, that rats had found them first.


Gabriel described the resulting theological discussion as "complicated."


Others were darker.


Much darker.


A cadet who had broken both ankles during a forced march and finished anyway because failing the final navigation test meant removal from the program.


Another who had collapsed during endurance training and died before the instructors reached him.


A girl from the northern archipelagos who could field-strip five different lasweapon patterns blindfolded but was expelled because she could not bring herself to order simulated artillery onto a position containing friendly casualties.


The Schola did not teach people to be good.


Not in the ordinary sense.


It taught them to become useful.


Useful under pressure.


Useful while exhausted.


Useful while frightened.


Useful while surrounded by the dead.


Useful when every instinct inherited from ten thousand generations of human beings insisted that the correct response was to run.


Gabriel spoke about this with remarkable calm.


Not pride exactly.


Certainly not shame.


It was simply what had happened.


"How many started?" Marr asked over the vox when the conversation spread through the vehicle network.


Gabriel thought about it.


"Our intake?"


"Yeah."


"One hundred and eighty-six."


There was a pause.


"And how many finished?"


"Twenty-three."


Nobody spoke for several seconds.


Even Hessel stopped smiling.


Broussard looked toward Gabriel.


"Twenty-three?"


"Twenty-three received full graduation seals."


"What happened to the rest?"


"Some transferred."


"Transferred?"


"Failed aptitude in one discipline and were reassigned elsewhere. Administratum. Naval discipline corps. Arbites support. Guard officer schools. Ecclesiastical security."


"And the others?"


Gabriel shrugged slightly.


"Some failed."


The meaning of that word sat heavily in the compartment.


"How many died?" someone asked.


Gabriel looked toward the speaker.


"During the full program?"


"Yeah."


"Thirty-one."


The driver muttered something under his breath.


Gabriel continued before anyone asked.


"Training accidents. Exposure. Weapons exercises. Live-fire testing. Medical failures. Two suicides. One disciplinary execution."


Nobody laughed now.


Broussard had expected something like this.


He had not expected the commissar to say it so plainly.


The Imperium frequently obscured ugly things beneath magnificent vocabulary.


Sacrifice.


Duty.


Correction.


Purity.


Martyrdom.


Words polished until the blood could no longer be seen on them.


Gabriel appeared to have little patience for that particular form of varnish.


"Thirty-one," Broussard repeated.


"Yes."


"And the rest?"


"Removed, reassigned, failed, or refused final qualification."


Hessel shifted in his seat.


"Refused?"


Gabriel looked at him.


"There are tests at the end."


"What kind?"


The commissar was quiet for a moment.


Not hesitant.


Merely considering how much explanation the question deserved.


Then he answered.


"The kind designed to determine whether everything before them worked."


The Chimera rolled onward.


Outside, snow swept across the ice in long white ribbons.


"The instructors told us early," Gabriel said, "that the purpose of the Schola was not to make us stronger than everyone else."


He looked around the compartment.


"It was to make us stronger than ourselves."


Nobody interrupted.


"You learn doctrine. Law. Combat. Investigation. Command. Political discipline. Oratory. Interrogation. Logistics. Ecclesiastical structure. Regimental structure. Imperial history. Weapons. Survival. Psychological conditioning."


He smiled faintly.


"And paperwork. Vast amounts of paperwork. Apparently moral certainty requires excellent penmanship."


That broke the tension for a moment.


Then it returned.


"But eventually," Gabriel continued, "they run out of things they can teach you in classrooms."


Broussard already knew where this was going.


Perhaps not the detail.


But the shape.


There were institutions within the Imperium that considered trust an untested weakness until it had been deliberately broken.


The Commissariat was one of them.


Gabriel rested his hands loosely between his knees.


"Near the end, each cadet is assigned a partner."


"Combat partner?" Orro asked over the vox.


"Everything partner."


The commissar's eyes settled somewhere beyond the compartment.


"You train together. Eat together. Drill together. Stand disciplinary review together. Conduct field operations together. They encourage competition, but they encourage dependence too."


Hessel frowned.


"Why?"


Gabriel looked at him.


"Because eventually they need you to care."


Nobody spoke.


"For months you rely on that person. Sometimes longer. They choose pairings carefully."


"Compatible?" Broussard asked.


"Very."


There it was.


Not random.


Engineered.


The realization passed through the vehicle like cold air.


Gabriel continued.


"The final loyalty sequence is different depending upon track and instructor, but for commissariat qualification the principle was simple."


He paused.


"They give you an order."


Broussard said nothing.


Gabriel's voice stayed level.


"Your partner is declared compromised."


One of the crewmen shifted uneasily.


"Actually compromised?"


"Sometimes."


"Sometimes?"


"Sometimes the evidence is fabricated."


The compartment went very still.


"You aren't told which."


That was worse.


Far worse.


Broussard felt his jaw tighten.


"The charge might be cowardice. Sedition. Doctrinal contamination. Concealment of heresy. Disobedience. Sympathy toward prohibited political material."


"And you're supposed to investigate?" Hessel asked.


"No."


Gabriel looked directly at him.


"You're supposed to execute them."


Silence.


The engine suddenly seemed louder.


The tracks louder.


The snow against the hull louder.


Everything mechanical rushed inward to fill the absence of human speech.


Gabriel continued with the same calm tone.


"No tribunal. No appeal. No delay."


His eyes moved around the compartment.


"Your instructor gives you the order."


"And that's it?" someone asked.


"That's it."


"What if they're innocent?"


Gabriel's smile had vanished.


"Then the test becomes more informative."


Broussard stared at him.


There was no bravado in the young man's expression.


That disturbed him more than enthusiasm would have.


"What's being tested?" Broussard asked.


Gabriel answered immediately.


"Whether your loyalty to the Emperor remains superior to your loyalty to any single human being."


There it was.


The beautiful horror of Imperial logic.


Simple enough to carve into stone.


Terrible enough to destroy a life.


Hessel looked uncomfortable.


"So you kill someone you've trained beside for years."


"Yes."


"Just because you're told."


Gabriel nodded.


"That's the point."


Nobody seemed particularly eager to continue the subject.


But Gabriel did.


Perhaps because once a thing had been opened, he preferred not to leave its darkest parts hidden.


"The instructors watch hesitation."


He held up one finger.


"Not merely whether you obey."


A second.


"How quickly."


A third.


"How cleanly."


A fourth.


"Whether you ask unnecessary questions."


Broussard felt something unpleasant settle in his stomach.


"The faster the better," he said.


Gabriel gave him a small nod.


"The more decisive the better."


"That's monstrous," someone muttered.


The commissar looked toward him.


"Yes."


The answer came so quickly that several men looked up.


Gabriel leaned back again.


"Life is hard."


His voice was quieter now.


"The Imperium is harder."


He looked down at his hands.


"The instructors believed that if we were going to be given authority over thousands of armed men, then whatever softness remained inside us had to be known."


"Removed?" Broussard asked.


Gabriel shook his head.


"Known."


That answer surprised him.


"You can't remove it all," the commissar continued. "Not without producing something useless."


Broussard's interest sharpened.


Gabriel glanced toward him.


"A commissar without compassion becomes a butcher. A commissar without discipline becomes a liability. A commissar without loyalty becomes a traitor."


He paused.


"So they teach you exactly where each thing lives inside you."


"And then make you choose."


"Yes."


Broussard looked through the forward slit toward the endless white ahead.


"Who was yours?"


The question escaped before he decided whether he should ask it.


Gabriel did not seem offended.


"A cadet named Josef Arlen."


No title.


No surname elaboration.


Just the name.


"Friend?"


"My closest."


The vehicle seemed colder.


"He was from the western islands. Fisherman's son."


Gabriel smiled faintly at the memory.


"Terrible shot when we started. Extraordinary memory. Could quote entire sections of the Lex Imperialis after hearing them once."


"What was he accused of?" Broussard asked.


"Compromised loyalty."


"Specific?"


"No."


That was almost obscene in its simplicity.


"What happened?"


Gabriel looked at Broussard.


"I was given a pistol."


The men waited.


"And an order."


No embellishment.


No dramatic pause.


No attempt to soften it.


"What did you do?" Hessel asked.


Gabriel met his eyes.


"I followed it."


Nothing more.


That was enough.


The question nobody asked hung there anyway.


How quickly?


How cleanly?


Had Josef begged?


Had he understood?


Had he hated Gabriel at the end?


Had Gabriel cried afterward?


The commissar did not volunteer any of it.


Perhaps some things belonged to the dead.


Eventually Broussard asked the safer question.


"Was he actually compromised?"


Gabriel looked toward the vision slit.


"No."


The answer hit harder than Broussard expected.


"You found out afterward?"


"Immediately."


Hessel swore softly.


Gabriel gave a humorless smile.


"The instructor congratulated me."


No one spoke.


"That was the test."


Broussard felt suddenly angry.


Not at Gabriel.


At the architecture of it.


The deliberate construction of trust for the sole purpose of demanding its destruction.


"What happened if someone refused?" he asked.


"Removed from commissariat qualification."


"That's all?"


"Usually."


Usually.


There was an Imperial word.


"And if they hesitated?"


"Depends how long."


"How long did you?"


Gabriel finally looked at him.


The easy smile was gone.


The young affable commissar vanished for a heartbeat and something trained in darker rooms looked back.


"Not long."


Broussard believed him.


Then, almost as quickly, the expression softened.


"Which," Gabriel said, "is apparently why I get this ridiculous coat."


The release of tension was so sudden that Hessel barked a laugh before realizing he had done so.


Gabriel glanced down at the black greatcoat.


"Very warm, though."


"That the selling point?" the driver asked.


"Honestly? On this planet, yes."


A few more chuckles moved through the compartment.


The story did not disappear.


Nothing could make it disappear.


But it settled.


Broussard understood something now.


The commissar's friendliness was not ignorance.


It was not softness.


Gabriel knew precisely what his uniform meant.


He had paid for it.


Possibly more than most men wearing one ever admitted.


And perhaps that was why he did not feel compelled to parade his severity in front of everyone he met.


A man who had already proven he could kill a friend on command had little need to demonstrate toughness by shouting at soldiers over improper boots.


Broussard found that strangely comforting.


Not entirely comforting.


A commissar should never become entirely comforting.


That would be like finding a loaded pistol soothing because one knew its manufacturer.


But enough.


The conversation moved on.


Gabriel told lighter stories again.


A training sergeant who had lost three teeth arguing with a combat servitor.


A cadet who accidentally addressed a visiting cardinal as "sir" and spent the next month copying ecclesiastical titles by hand.


The first class's ceremonial graduation march, which had taken place during freezing rain while the reviewing platform's heating system functioned flawlessly.


Naturally.


Hours passed.


The convoy lights behind them grew distant.


Eventually even the massive bulk of the Saint Malderson disappeared into haze and snowfall.


Ahead waited the stranded freighter.


Behind them lay warmth, support, and several thousand armed friends moving steadily away.


Inside the Chimera, Commissar Gabriel Hamden Jalesthesian was telling a story about smuggling sausages into a final doctrinal examination.


Broussard listened.


He found himself smiling.


The young man was pleasant company.


Which, Broussard reflected, made perfect sense.


The Imperium had apparently gone to extraordinary lengths to ensure he knew exactly how little that would matter if orders ever changed.
