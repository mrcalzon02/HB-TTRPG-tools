# On the Fuzzy Edge

## Continuation IV — The Question That Remained

**Based on the work of:** *On the Fuzzy Edge.*

Pontiff Montpclair had survived his sermon.

This was good news for the cathedral, which had begun preparing three different forms of succession before anyone had thought to ask the medicae whether he was dead.

It was less good news for Montpclair.

His left ear had been removed. The brass socket of his ruined bookmetic eye was packed beneath a surgical dressing, and a narrow strip of skin above his cheek had been shaved to accommodate the clamps holding the dressing in place. He sat upright only because Sister-Medicae Halva had allowed the bed to be raised.

She had not allowed visitors.

Javard found this out at the door.

"You came down thirty metres of condemned shaft with me," he said.

"That was a different kind of foolishness."

"I have questions."

"He has a pulse. At present I value that more."

"How long?"

Halva considered him as a physician might consider a patient who had elected to swallow his own boots.

"Ten minutes. No raised voices. No theological arguments. If he becomes confused, you stop. If I say stop, you stop."

"Understood."

"And you will not use the rosette to overrule me."

Javard glanced at the device beneath his mantle.

"I was hoping not to."

"Good. Because then I would have to explain to the Inquisition why its interrogator killed a patient by talking at him."

Caldris waited beyond the door, his ring of keys hanging motionless from one hand. Serik remained with the cathedral guards. Neither had been invited into the ward.

Inside, the Pontiff was writing on a slate.

His right hand shook enough to spoil the first letter. He rubbed it out and began again.

**I AM NOT DEAF.**

Halva read the words over his shoulder.

"Your remaining ear is under observation, Your Reverence."

Montpclair struck the slate with his chalk.

**THEN SPEAK ON THAT SIDE.**

Javard moved to the right.

The Pontiff examined him with one bloodshot human eye. For a man who had recently lost two expensive pieces of his head, he retained a formidable talent for disapproval.

"You," he said.

Javard inclined his head.

"Interrogator Javard."

"I know."

"That is one of the things I came to discuss."

---

Montpclair would not allow a clerk to record the interview.

He would allow Halva, because she already knew more about his private anatomy than any clerk should, and he accepted that Javard would make his own notes.

"Did you ask whether I had opened Sol?" Javard said.

"Yes."

"Why?"

Montpclair looked toward the wall, where a devotional panel depicted a martyr refusing the comforts of an extraordinarily well-furnished torture chamber.

"Because I needed to know."

"What would you have done if the answer were yes?"

"I do not know."

"That is an unusual answer."

"I am recovering from surgery. You will have to endure some."

Javard accepted the rebuke. A weak witness could still be a competent man, and he had no intention of confusing exhaustion with deceit.

"Who told you my name?"

The Pontiff frowned.

"Someone must have."

"Who?"

"I cannot recall."

"Did you know it before the sermon?"

Montpclair closed his eye.

Halva watched his breathing rather than his expression.

"I remember the sermon. I remember the pain. I remember someone telling me not to move. Then the recovery room."

"Before the sermon?"

"I had a visitor's appointment to make."

"With whom?"

"A commercial delegation. I had intended to refuse it."

Javard made a note. His own planned approach had been through a commercial cover. That might account for Montpclair expecting a visitor. It did not account for his knowing the name concealed behind the cover.

"Did the name Javard appear on the appointment?"

"No."

"How can you be certain?"

"Because I never saw the appointment. My secretary deals with people who wish to sell the cathedral things it already owns."

The Pontiff looked tired, but his irritation was precise.

"Do not turn an absence in my memory into proof that someone erased it."

"I wasn't going to."

"You were considering it."

"I consider a great many unpleasant things. Most are disappointed."

Montpclair's mouth almost moved.

Almost.

---

Javard placed the burial-transfer strip on the bedside table without putting it into the Pontiff's hand.

"Ephram Sol."

Montpclair's gaze settled on the name.

"Canon Archivist."

"Deceased seventeen years."

"Yes."

"Transfer pending."

The Pontiff's fingers tightened around the chalk.

"Yes."

"Yet his destination is maintained. Every year. On the Vigil of Saint Orphael."

Montpclair did not ask how Javard knew. That was interesting. It was not proof of anything beyond the fact that the old priest could still follow an investigation.

"Did you authorize the maintenance?" Javard asked.

"The burial office answers to the cathedral."

"That was not the question."

"No. It was the answer I am permitted to give without pretending I have read every instruction issued in my name."

Javard let the silence work.

"I have signed the annual provision for the ossuaries," Montpclair said eventually. "I have not personally inspected every niche."

"Do you believe Sol is buried there?"

"I believe we have never certified his arrival."

"Do you believe he is dead?"

"I knew the man who performed his last rites."

"Did he see the body?"

"He said he did."

"Did you?"

"No."

Montpclair looked down at the strip.

"There are two different duties here, Interrogator. A priest may conduct a rite for a dead man. A custodian must account for what has been entrusted to him. One cannot discharge the second duty by repeating the first."

"Who held custody?"

"That is the question my predecessor could not get answered."

"From whom?"

Montpclair's expression hardened.

"You have seen the seal, I take it."

"Yes."

"Then you have met the only answer he was given."

It was not a revelation. It was an admission of jurisdictional failure. The cathedral could maintain a destination and pay for its care while another authority withheld the means of certifying the transfer. It was precisely the sort of compromise that could survive for seventeen years because each side could prove it had done something.

"Why keep paying?" Javard asked.

"Because if a man is ever delivered for burial, there should be somewhere to put him."

"And if the niche is already occupied?"

"Then someone owes the dead an explanation."

Javard thought of the clean plaque and the probe that had brought away organic residue. Halva had not certified a body. He would not make her examination say more than it did.

---

"Why did you say opened?" he asked.

Montpclair stared at him.

"Not found. Not visited. Opened."

The Pontiff put the chalk down.

"My predecessor used that word."

"When?"

"When he warned me about Sol."

"What warning?"

"He told me not to sign a receipt for a thing I had not received."

"Was that all?"

"No."

Javard waited.

"He told me that when the matter came back, someone would ask the wrong question first. Whether Sol had been moved. Whether Sol had been buried. Whether the register could be corrected."

"And the right question?"

Montpclair looked at the strip again.

"Whether anyone had opened him."

Javard did not write immediately.

"Did he mean the niche?"

"I asked him."

"What did he say?"

"He told me I would know when I had to."

"Did you?"

"No."

The Pontiff spoke the last word with evident anger. Not fear, or not fear alone. He had inherited a warning that sounded important, preserved it because his predecessor had been a man he respected, and discovered that obedience had not made it intelligible.

"Why ask me now?"

"Because I woke with your name in my mouth and the question beside it."

Halva shifted slightly.

"That is what you told us," she said.

Javard turned.

"When?"

"Before he was fully oriented. He was able to state his name and office. He asked for you. I asked whether he knew where he was. He answered correctly. He asked about Sol again."

"Had anyone mentioned Sol in the ward?"

"Not in my hearing."

"Had anyone mentioned me?"

"Not in my hearing."

Javard wrote both qualifications exactly.

Montpclair watched him.

"You think I have been instructed."

"I think you remember an instruction. I do not know whether it accounts for what you said today."

"Nor do I."

The Pontiff looked at Halva.

"May I have water?"

She gave him a cup, waited until he had swallowed, and checked the dressing beneath his eye.

When she withdrew her hand, the cloth carried a small stain.

"Enough," she said.

Javard closed his notebook.

"I have one more question."

"No," Halva said.

Montpclair lifted one finger.

"Let him ask."

"Your Reverence—"

"One."

Javard considered the questions still waiting. He could spend his last opportunity asking about the visitor's appointment, the predecessor's warning, or the sealed cabinet. None was likely to produce a reliable answer from a patient whose strength was failing.

"Do you wish the niche opened?" he asked.

"No."

"Because of the warning?"

"Because if you open it now, the argument will become about what you found. Before anyone has established who was responsible for putting it there."

The Pontiff looked at the burial strip.

"And because a man who may have been denied his burial deserves an account that survives the discovery."

Javard nodded.

That, at least, was an answer he understood.

---

In the corridor, Caldris was waiting with a petition for the Pontiff's signature.

Halva took it from him and read the heading.

"Appointment of an acting celebrant."

"Only for tomorrow's offices," Caldris said.

"He is not dead."

"I am aware."

"Then stop asking him to sign things while I am trying to keep him alive."

Caldris opened his mouth, reconsidered, and put away his pen.

Javard found himself briefly sympathetic. The cathedral still needed someone to lead prayers, and Montpclair could not do it. Its machinery would continue to turn whether the patient approved or not.

"Arrange it through the standing delegation," Javard said.

Caldris looked at him.

"There is a standing delegation?"

"You tell me."

"Yes."

"Then use it."

The sub-dean looked offended that a stranger had noticed a procedure he had been avoiding.

Serik joined them at the stair.

"Did he know?"

"He knew some things."

"Useful things?"

"Possibly."

"That is not much."

"He was bleeding through his bandage. I elected not to ask him for a complete history of the galaxy."

Serik glanced toward the ward.

"Fair."

Javard stopped at the records chamber and asked Caldris for two separate statements. Halva would record what she had heard in the ward, including the limits of her attention. Caldris would record when he first heard Montpclair ask about Javard and Sol, and who had been present. Neither was to see the other's account before signing.

"You think one of us is lying?" Caldris asked.

"No. I think honest people repeat one another."

Caldris considered that.

"Will you put my statement under seal?"

"I will put the original where your institution can account for it. I will take an authenticated copy."

"And the old cabinet?"

"Remains sealed."

"The ossuary?"

"Remains closed."

Caldris's relief was immediate.

"Do not mistake that for abandonment," Javard said.

The relief faded.

"Have the burial office preserve the annual inspection cards and the maintenance orders that produced them. Originals, not a new summary. No new work at Sol's niche without a written record of who authorized it. Ordinary maintenance elsewhere continues."

"Can you order the burial office?"

"No. You can request it through your own chain. I can make refusal a matter of formal inquiry."

Caldris sighed.

"That sounds more expensive."

"It will be."

"And the transfer?"

"Still pending."

---

Archive Servitor Nine was feeding parchment when Javard returned.

Scratch.

Click.

Scratch.

Click.

He did not speak to it.

He did not show it the new notes.

The printed warning remained in his coat: **DO NOT OPEN UNTIL HE ASKS.**

Montpclair had asked whether Sol was opened.

That did not establish that Montpclair was the person meant by *he*. The servitor might have meant Sol, an officer, a reader, or someone Javard had not yet encountered. A damaged machine and an injured priest could each preserve a phrase without sharing its meaning.

Javard had seen investigations ruined by men who mistook two matching words for one matching cause.

He made a separate note of the temptation.

Serik was examining the original burial transfer under the green lumen.

"Something wrong?"

"Seventeen years," Serik said. "All this trouble, and nobody can say whether they buried a man."

"Someone can say they performed his rites."

"That's different."

"Exactly."

Serik set the strip down.

"Are we going back below?"

"Not today."

"Tomorrow?"

"Not until the records are preserved."

The factor looked toward the cabinet with its old red seal.

"And if somebody opens it before us?"

"Then we will have another question. Who had the authority, and why did they use it?"

Javard folded the fresh interview notes, marked the time and witnesses, and gave Caldris a receipt for the copy he intended to take.

It was a small, unimpressive act.

An injured Pontiff had not explained the mystery. A niche remained unopened. The old seal still refused to identify its owner. The Tides of Tomorrow had not become any less impossible on paper, and Demas Vorn had not become any more reliable.

But the question had acquired a history, a witness who admitted his limits, and a reason for being asked that was not simply curiosity.

In the ward upstairs, Montpclair would sleep if Halva had anything to say about it.

Below the cathedral, a maintenance servitor would continue to keep a destination ready for a transfer nobody had certified.

Javard left the cabinet untouched.

For the moment, the most useful thing he could do with the dead was not to disturb them.

It was to stop the living from losing the paperwork again.
