# The Long Patrol of Broussard

## Source Recovery 04B — IV. THE COMMISSAR ARRIVES (remainder)

*Verbatim recovery from the surviving Google working draft, continuing immediately after “Especially then.” No new continuation prose is introduced here.*

The briefing continued.

Assignments were distributed.

Chimera One would remain Broussard's command vehicle.

Chimera Two would take forward security.

Three would cover the southern arc around the crippled freighter.

Four would establish the northern perimeter and maintain rapid-response readiness.

The recovery hauler would remain close to the vessel under Mechanicus direction.

The Magos would control engineering operations.

Broussard would control everything capable of shooting.

He considered this division of authority pleasantly straightforward.

Ancillary orders followed.

Private vox channels.

Encrypted command frequencies.

Emergency fallback channels in case atmospheric interference or enemy action compromised primary communications.

Updated recognition codes.

New cipher slates.

Navigation coordinates.

Rally points.

Emergency casualty collection locations.

Vehicle recovery priorities.

Ammunition conservation guidance.

Thermal exposure protocols.

Night-watch rotation.

Instructions concerning wildlife contact.

Instructions concerning wildlife contact escalations.

Instructions concerning what to do if the wildlife contact was larger than anticipated.

That final section was notably short.

Use heavier weapons.

The Guard respected simplicity.

Broussard was midway through explaining their revised encryption rotation when the conversation drifted toward the absence of political oversight.

It was Orro who started it.

"No commissar assigned?"

Broussard looked down the order sheet.

"None listed."

Several soldiers visibly relaxed.

Broussard understood.

Commissars were rare on convoy detachments.

The units were too scattered.

A political officer could supervise a company concentrated in one trench line.

It was considerably harder to supervise a mechanized escort force spread across eighty kilometers of moving ice.

Convoy command therefore tended to reserve its limited commissariat presence for command vessels, major defensive groups, supply centers, and situations in which somebody had already done something deserving investigation.

Broussard personally considered this one of the greatest advantages of convoy duty.

He respected discipline.

He respected Imperial law.

He respected the Emperor.

He respected the commissariat in much the same way one respected an unexploded artillery shell.

From an appropriate distance.

"Looks like we're unsupervised," Broussard said.

There were several quiet cheers.

He raised a hand.

"Within reason."

More laughter.

"And before anyone becomes ambitious, remember that I still possess temporary command authority, so if you decide this is your chance to establish an independent republic on the ice, I am legally required to object."

"How strongly?" Hessel asked.

"Strongly enough."

"Shame."

"We'll survive the disappointment."

Broussard glanced again at the orders.

"Anyway, given that there is no commissar accompanying the detachment—"

Something changed in the expressions of the men directly in front of him.

Not much.

Just enough.

It was the peculiar facial transformation of soldiers who had suddenly noticed something behind their officer and were desperately attempting not to react to it.

Broussard stopped.

His body understood before his mind did.

Cold sweat immediately appeared along his forehead.

Very slowly, he shifted his eyes to the side.

Black.

Red.

Gold.

Greatcoat.

High collar.

Cap.

Commissariat insignia.

A young man stood perhaps three meters behind him.

He had apparently emerged from the access ramp leading down from officer country without Broussard noticing.

Which meant either the man moved remarkably quietly or Murphy's Law had acquired corporeal form.

Broussard stared at him.

The commissar stared back.

Young.

That was the first surprise.

The face was sharply chiseled but not severe. Clean-shaven. Alert eyes. Broad shoulders beneath the greatcoat.

And he was smiling.

Not the smile of a man about to execute someone.

Probably.

It was an easy expression.

Almost friendly.

This was somehow worse.

Broussard looked back at his soldiers.

Every man who had been laughing moments before now stood with the expressionless solemnity of saints awaiting martyrdom.

Hessel had somehow achieved parade rest.

Broussard could not remember seeing him move.

He swallowed.

"As I was saying."

Nobody breathed.

"The commissariat presence assigned to this detachment had not yet been reflected in the briefing materials available to me."

The commissar's smile widened slightly.

Broussard decided not to interpret this.

"We will therefore enjoy the additional benefit of direct commissariat support."

Nobody cheered.

Cowards.

Broussard moved rapidly through the remainder of the briefing.

Not hurriedly.

Never hurriedly.

Simply with an efficiency suggesting that perhaps several pages of optional commentary had suddenly become unnecessary.

Deployment time: nineteen hundred local.

Vehicles fueled by eighteen-thirty.

Final equipment check at eighteen-forty.

Vox testing eighteen-forty-five.

All personnel aboard eighteen-fifty.

Departure immediately after receiving clearance from deck control.

Private frequency assignments distributed to vehicle commanders.

Encryption slates updated every six hours.

Recognition codes changed at midnight.

No unnecessary external transmissions.

No civilian access to encrypted military channels.

Questions?

There were a few.

Marr asked about ammunition resupply.

Orro asked whether recovery equipment had been checked for low-temperature hydraulic failure.

Voss wanted clarification on the route approach to the wreck.

A medic asked how many freighter crew were aboard.

Broussard answered each.

Throughout the entire process, the commissar simply waited.

This disturbed Broussard considerably.

Every commissar he had encountered before had possessed an almost supernatural ability to interrupt.

Sometimes for doctrinal correction.

Sometimes to remind everyone of proper military courtesy.

Sometimes because an officer had neglected to mention the Emperor often enough within a five-minute period.

Commissars were trained representatives of political authority, ideological discipline and battlefield morale.

Many behaved accordingly.

Rigid.

Formal.

Performative.

Some seemed physically incapable of allowing an opportunity for visible devotion to pass unexploited.

This one said nothing.

He did not interrupt.

He did not correct Broussard.

He did not insert a speech about courage.

He did not remind the soldiers that cowardice was punishable by death.

He simply stood at the edge of the gathering with his hands loosely folded behind his back and waited for the commanding officer to finish commanding.

Broussard found this unnerving.

Competence was always more frightening than enthusiasm.

At last the briefing ended.

"Vehicle commanders, collect your ancillary packets. Squad leaders, supervise final loading. Anyone with equipment deficiencies reports them now, not after we are thirty kilometers onto the ice. Dismissed."

The gathering broke apart.

Immediately.

Men discovered urgent responsibilities elsewhere.

Within seconds, the open space around Broussard had become remarkably empty.

The commissar stepped forward.

Broussard prepared himself.

The young man extended a hand.

That was unexpected enough that Broussard looked at it for half a second before accepting.

"Gabriel Hamden Jalesthesian," the commissar said. "Regilian Third Continental Schola."

Broussard blinked.

"Regilian?"

"Yes."

"Here?"

"Born about two hundred kilometers southeast of High Presidio."

Broussard stared at him.

"A native."

"Guilty."

The commissar's handshake was firm but not theatrical.

Broussard released it.

"I wasn't aware the Regilian Schola had been operating long enough to produce commissariat graduates."

The young man's smile returned.

"You're not alone."

Broussard looked him over again.

"You graduated there?"

"First class."

That explained something.

And introduced several new concerns.

The Regilian Third Continental Schola was young.

Very young.

Broussard remembered hearing about its establishment.

There had been announcements.

Construction contracts.

Recruitment notices.

The usual speeches concerning the development of planetary institutions and the glorious expansion of Imperial service opportunities.

He had also heard occasional rumors about cadet formations being sent across High Presidio for practical exercises.

Some had gone off-world.

Others had been attached to domestic Guard units, naval patrols, Administratum offices, and planetary security services.

He had simply never considered that enough time had passed for one of those cadets to become this.

"First commissariat graduates?" Broussard asked.

"First full graduating cohort."

"And they sent you straight into convoy duty."

Jalesthesian laughed quietly.

"Not quite straight."

"No?"

"The old dogs were tentative about letting us off the leash."

That phrase, coming from a commissar, nearly caused Broussard to reassess the conversation.

The young man continued.

"Most of us were given domestic deployments first. Something supervised. Something reasonably predictable. Opportunities to exercise authority without immediately being dropped into an active war zone and accidentally shooting everyone."

Broussard stared.

The commissar smiled.

"That was a joke, Captain."

"I was hoping."

"You're allowed to laugh."

"I'll require written authorization."

"Can provide it."

Broussard felt one corner of his mouth twitch.

Dangerous.

Very dangerous.

The commissar continued.

"The Schola considered domestic convoy duty a good practical assignment. Enough operational uncertainty to be educational. Enough military structure to keep us from embarrassing the institution."

"You say that as though you expect embarrassment."

"I'm young, Captain. I try to maintain realistic expectations."

Broussard studied him.

This was not how commissars spoke.

At least, not the ones Broussard knew.

There was confidence there.

Zeal too, somewhere beneath the humor.

The uniform was immaculate.

The insignia exact.

The man carried himself with the precision of somebody who had spent years being corrected for standing incorrectly.

But there was no visible hunger to demonstrate authority.

No immediate demand for formal courtesies.

No attempt to dominate the room.

That could mean he was sensible.

It could mean he was inexperienced.

It could mean he was terrifyingly good at hiding what he was thinking.

All three possibilities seemed plausible.

"I've heard about your cadets," Broussard said.

"I'd be surprised if you hadn't."

"Training deployments around the planet."

"Several."

"Some off-world."

"Yes."

"How long?"

"A few years now. Mostly observation and support assignments at first. Security detachments. Naval administrative work. Arbites coordination. Guard regimental attachments. Some ecclesiastical work."

Broussard grimaced.

"Unfortunate."

Jalesthesian smiled again.

"Educational."

"That's one word."

"I learned several others."

Broussard decided he liked him.

This was a serious tactical error.

"So," Broussard said, "you are assigned to us?"

"I am."

"For the recovery operation?"

"And until we return to convoy command."

Broussard nodded slowly.

"Orders?"

Jalesthesian reached into his greatcoat and handed over a thin document case.

Broussard opened it.

Official seal.

Temporary commissariat attachment.

Operational authority.

Standard ideological and disciplinary responsibilities.

The usual collection of phrases capable of becoming lethal depending upon interpretation.

Nothing unusual.

He handed it back.

"I should warn you."

The commissar raised an eyebrow.

"Should you?"

"My men complain."

"I noticed."

"They swear."

"I also noticed."

"They gamble."

"I would have been shocked otherwise."

"They occasionally disregard minor regulations."

Jalesthesian's expression became solemn.

"Captain."

Broussard waited.

"I graduated from a Schola."

"Yes."

"I have witnessed seventeen-year-olds attempt to ferment alcohol inside devotional candle containers."

Broussard paused.

"Successfully?"

"Disturbingly."

That earned the first genuine laugh.

Quiet.

Brief.

But real.

The commissar inclined his head.

"My purpose is not to make your command less functional."

Broussard's amusement disappeared.

That was an interesting sentence.

Jalesthesian continued.

"You have a reputation."

"So I keep hearing."

"A good one."

"That's usually when someone assigns me more work."

"Also apparently accurate."

He glanced toward the Chimeras.

"Your unit returns from assignments."

Broussard said nothing.

"People notice that."

"Some people don't consider it a virtue."

"I do."

There was no humor in the commissar now.

The shift was subtle.

Almost comfortable.

But Broussard suddenly understood that beneath the friendly smile was still a man trained by the Commissariat.

"I was taught that soldiers belong to the Emperor," Jalesthesian said. "Wasting His property through stupidity is no demonstration of devotion."

Broussard looked at him for several seconds.

That was dangerously close to something Broussard himself might have said.

The commissar's smile returned.

"So I suspect we'll manage."

"You're very optimistic."

"No."

Jalesthesian glanced toward the open embarkation ramp where frozen wind carried blowing snow into the far end of the maintenance deck.

"I'm told we're about to spend several days parked over an ocean full of things large enough to mistake freighters for prey."

He looked back at Broussard.

"I am attempting to enjoy the warm part of the assignment."

Broussard considered that.

Then nodded.

"Deployment at nineteen hundred."

"I'll be aboard."

"Which vehicle?"

"Yours."

Of course.

Broussard closed his eyes for a moment.

Murphy remained undefeated.
