# House Vallimere's Sons

## Continuation II — The Practical Examination

**Based on the work of: House Vallimere's Sons.**

Prite discovered at three in the morning that doctrine became considerably less elegant when it required footnotes.

The sentence on his slate read:

**THE ENEMY IS NOT OBLIGED TO RESPECT YOUR DOCTRINE.**

Below it, the Academy curriculum office had attached seventeen requested clarifications.

The first asked him to define *enemy*.

The second asked him to define *obliged*.

The third, which Prite suspected had been written by a man with a personal grievance against language, requested an approved citation establishing that hostile forces were permitted to disregard Imperial naval doctrine.

He stared at it until the letters became hostile forces.

Across the table, Darcelle was asleep with his face on a stack of tactical diagrams.

Renault was awake, although only because he had discovered that the kitchen staff would provide recaf indefinitely if the request form cited “instructional readiness.”

“I think,” Prite said, “we have found the real enemy.”

Renault sipped from his cup.

“Forms?”

“Definitions.”

“Forms containing definitions.”

Prite considered this.

“Yes.”

At the door, Iral Vane remained upright.

He had been upright for so long that Prite had begun to suspect the Inquisition issued replacement spines.

Vane glanced at the slate.

“Do not declare war on the curriculum office.”

“Wasn’t planning to.”

“You have written ‘hostile administrative action’ in the margin.”

“That is classification, not intent.”

Vane accepted this distinction with the grave patience of a man who had probably heard worse arguments from people carrying explosives.

The door opened.

Darcelle woke instantly.

This was less impressive than it appeared. Since four noble Houses had submitted duel petitions, he had begun waking whenever hinges moved.

The Headmaster entered carrying no papers.

Prite distrusted him immediately.

“You are all still here,” the Headmaster said.

“We were ordered to write a syllabus,” Prite replied.

“Yes.”

“It is resisting.”

“That is encouraging.”

Renault lowered his cup. “Encouraging?”

“Anything that survives first contact with the curriculum office may survive the Navy.”

The Headmaster walked to the tactical display and placed a small brass token on the table.

It bore the Academy seal.

Prite knew enough about Academy seals to understand that this one authorized something expensive.

He also knew enough about the Headmaster to understand that the expense would shortly become their problem.

“What is that?” Darcelle asked.

“Your second lecture.”

Prite looked at the token.

“No.”

The Headmaster smiled.

Prite hated it when he did that.

---

At ten hundred, the same one hundred and twenty cadets assembled in Simulation Hall Four.

There were fewer observers.

This worried Prite more than the previous crowd.

The angry Admiral was absent. So were the curriculum representatives.

Instead, the upper gallery contained six officers Prite did not recognize, two representatives of House Vallimere, Vane, and the Headmaster.

Nobody wore enough gold braid to be mistaken for furniture.

“Where is everyone?” Darcelle whispered.

“Recovering,” Renault suggested.

Prite looked at the cadets.

They were excited.

That was worse.

Yesterday they had been skeptical. Skepticism was useful. Skeptical students tried to prove the instructor wrong.

Excited students tried to prove the instructor right.

Prite stepped onto the platform.

“Yesterday,” he said, “we showed you a weaker fleet defeating a stronger one by attacking the assumptions holding the stronger fleet together.”

Several cadets nodded.

Too quickly.

“You liked it.”

More nods.

“That is a problem.”

The room quieted.

Prite activated the display.

A star system unfolded above them.

One inhabited moon.

Two orbital stations.

A refinery complex.

A convoy route.

A damaged cruiser.

A squadron of escorts.

Supply depots.

Civilian traffic.

Repair yards.

Fuel reserves.

Vox stations.

A thousand small symbols that most tactical exercises quietly removed because they made battles untidy.

Renault stepped beside him.

“Today,” he said, “you get to be clever.”

That produced smiles.

Darcelle activated a second layer.

Personnel numbers appeared.

Dock crews.

Refinery workers.

Merchant crews.

Medical capacity.

Evacuation lift.

Estimated food reserves.

The smiles faded.

Prite divided the class into twelve commands.

“Each command has the same objective. Hold the system for seventy-two hours against a superior attacking force.”

A cadet raised his hand.

“Yes?”

“What are the victory conditions?”

“Still possessing the system after seventy-two hours.”

The cadet frowned. “What civilian-loss threshold?”

“There isn’t one.”

The room became very still.

Prite let them sit with that.

Then he said, “There is no score for saving civilians. There is no penalty for losing them. There is no hidden Academy metric that rewards mercy. There is no secret bonus for honor. There is only the system and the clock.”

Darcelle looked at him.

They had argued about this part for forty minutes.

Prite continued.

“What you do with that freedom is part of the examination.”

The simulations began.

For the first six minutes, every command was brilliant.

Cadets dispersed fuel reserves. They repositioned escorts. They mined likely approach corridors. They moved repair ships. They established redundant vox relays. One group detached civilian tugs to drag empty cargo hulls into false fleet formations.

Prite watched with growing unease.

“They learned yesterday,” Darcelle whispered.

“Yes.”

“Isn’t that the point?”

“No.”

Renault heard him.

He smiled faintly.

Then the enemy arrived.

The first attack did not strike a warship.

It struck a civilian fuel depot.

One command immediately diverted escorts to protect the remaining depots.

Another abandoned them.

A third evacuated the workers but left the fuel.

A fourth ordered the depot destroyed before capture.

Prite marked nothing.

At twenty minutes, the attackers threatened the inhabited moon.

Half the commands recognized the feint.

Three ignored it.

Two committed everything.

One cadet group began arguing so loudly that the simulation paused automatically because the command microphones could no longer distinguish orders.

Renault leaned toward Prite.

“That one feels authentic.”

Prite did not answer.

Command Seven had found the obvious exploit.

They had realized the enemy’s objective required usable orbital infrastructure.

So they began destroying their own stations.

First the refinery.

Then the repair yard.

Then the primary civilian dock.

The enemy advance slowed.

Command Seven cheered.

Prite stopped their simulation.

The cadets looked up.

“Why did you stop us?”

“You tell me.”

“We denied infrastructure to the enemy.”

“Yes.”

“We traded fixed assets for time.”

“Yes.”

“We’re winning.”

Prite looked at the casualty estimate.

Forty-three thousand civilians had been aboard or dependent upon the orbital facilities when the cadets issued the demolition orders.

“You are.”

The cadet commander waited.

Prite said nothing else.

The boy’s expression changed.

Not much.

Enough.

In another command, a cadet refused to abandon a crippled escort whose crew had kept fighting after its engines failed. She sent two tugs and a destroyer back for it.

The destroyer was lost.

The tugs were lost.

The escort survived.

The diversion opened a corridor the attackers exploited eleven minutes later.

Her system fell.

She sat staring at the display.

Prite stopped that simulation too.

“Was I wrong?” she asked.

He hated the question.

The old syllabus would have answered it.

The old syllabus had answers for everything.

That was one of its defects.

“What did you know when you gave the order?” he asked.

“That the escort had twelve hundred crew.”

“What else?”

“That abandoning them would have freed the destroyer.”

“What did you believe the enemy would do?”

“Continue toward the refinery.”

“They didn’t.”

“No.”

“Could you have known?”

She hesitated.

“No.”

“Could you have considered it?”

Another hesitation.

“Yes.”

Prite nodded.

“Then that is what you take from it.”

She looked dissatisfied.

Good.

Dissatisfaction meant the lesson had not become a slogan yet.

At the one-hour mark, Darcelle began introducing equipment failures.

Not dramatic ones.

A targeting array drifted.

A fuel pump lost pressure.

A vox repeater transmitted orders six seconds late.

A servitor misread a docking command and blocked a launch bay with a bulk hauler.

The cadets complained.

Darcelle brightened.

For the first time all morning, he looked entirely comfortable.

“Machines do that,” he told them.

One cadet protested that the maintenance probabilities were excessive.

Darcelle checked the figure.

“No. Your maintenance assumptions were optimistic.”

“We used Academy standard readiness.”

“Yes.”

“Then the Academy figure is wrong.”

Darcelle smiled.

There was no anxiety in it now.

“Now you’re learning.”

At seventy-two simulated hours, three commands still held the system.

Command Seven held the most territory.

It also possessed the least system worth holding.

Command Two had preserved the moon and most of the civilian infrastructure but lost every capital ship.

Command Eleven retained a battered fleet, one station, half the refinery, and a civilian population furious about rationing, compulsory evacuation, and the conversion of three luxury liners into ammunition tenders.

The cadets waited for a ranking.

Prite did not provide one.

A hand rose.

“Who won?”

“All three.”

That produced immediate argument.

Prite raised his voice.

“Command Seven achieved the stated military objective.”

The cadets from Seven looked pleased.

“They also destroyed enough infrastructure to cripple the system for years.”

The pleasure weakened.

“Command Two preserved the population and industrial base but left the system almost defenseless against a second attack.”

Command Two stopped looking pleased.

“Command Eleven preserved enough of everything to continue functioning, at the cost of efficiency, comfort and several decisions that will produce inquiries until your grandchildren are dead.”

Renault leaned toward the class.

“Possibly longer. The Administratum is very committed to family traditions.”

A few people laughed.

Prite let them.

Then he looked across the room.

“Yesterday you learned that doctrine can be exploited.”

He changed the display.

The twelve systems appeared side by side.

Different wreckage.

Different graves.

Different surviving assets.

“Today you learn the dangerous part.”

He pointed toward Command Seven.

“A clever solution is not the same thing as a good decision.”

No one laughed now.

The Headmaster watched from the gallery.

Vane watched the doors.

Prite wondered which of them was more worried.

After the cadets left, Darcelle remained at the console.

“I thought Seven was brilliant at first.”

“So did I,” Prite said.

Renault sat on the edge of the platform.

“That was why it worked.”

Darcelle looked at the empty seats.

“We used to do things like that.”

Prite knew what he meant.

In the private simulations, before the Headmaster, before House Vallimere, before anyone had cared what three technicians did after shift, they had sacrificed fleets for elegant victories because the dead had been numbers and the reset button had always been within reach.

“We did,” Prite said.

Renault turned the brass Academy token over between his fingers.

“We were playing.”

Prite looked at the casualty figures still hovering over Command Seven’s dead stations.

“Yes.”

The Headmaster descended from the gallery.

“Well?”

Prite expected approval.

Instead the old man asked, “What did you learn?”

Prite almost laughed.

Then he realized the question was serious.

He looked at Darcelle.

At Renault.

At the simulations they had built.

At the lesson they had intended to teach and the one that had arrived without permission.

Finally he opened the syllabus draft.

Below the first lesson, he wrote another line.

**LESSON TWO: WINNING IS A CONDITION. COST IS A CONSEQUENCE. DO NOT CONFUSE THEM.**

Darcelle read it.

“That one needs a footnote.”

Prite closed the slate.

“No.”

Renault nodded solemnly.

“Good. We are improving already.”

At the rear of the hall, Vane touched his vox-bead.

His expression did not change.

“Professors.”

Prite turned.

“Yes?”

“House Merovin has withdrawn its duel petition.”

Darcelle exhaled.

Vane continued.

“House Caldrake has submitted two.”

Darcelle inhaled again.

Renault looked at Prite.

“Net change?”

“Plus one.”

The Headmaster sighed.

“Teaching is going well, then.”
