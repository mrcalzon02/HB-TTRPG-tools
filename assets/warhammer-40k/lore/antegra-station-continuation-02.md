# Antegra Station

## Continuation II — The Courtesy Inspection

*An original Cafarron Corridor chronicle continuation. Warhammer 40,000 and associated marks and setting elements are the property of Games Workshop. This non-commercial fan work is not endorsed by or affiliated with Games Workshop.*

The crowbar arrived before the Mechanicus did.

Governor Talbor Varik considered this a minor triumph of civil administration.

Captain Ardan Krell drove the hooked end into the audit door while two troopers leaned against the frame. The door had been manufactured on Antegra one hundred and thirty-seven years earlier, repaired twenty-six times, blessed nine times, condemned twice and never replaced because replacement required a procurement authorization from an office that had ceased to exist during Varik's first rejuvenat cycle.

It resisted them with institutional confidence.

“Again,” Varik said.

Krell heaved.

Metal screamed. The door opened far enough for a thin man to pass sideways, which was sufficient because most of Antegra's senior clerks had been selected by starvation long before they were selected by examination.

Varik stepped through.

Solvik caught his sleeve.

“Governor.”

Varik looked down at the Commissar-Keeper's hand.

Solvik released him.

“The arriving vessel has identified itself.”

“And?”

“Mechanicus survey tender *Peregrine of Cinders*. Custodian Magos Hester Vhal. Twelve bonded enginseers, forty-four servitors, a thermal works cohort and an armed Skitarii escort.”

“That is an unusual amount of courtesy.”

“They call it a courtesy inspection.”

Varik stared at him.

Krell stopped levering the door.

Some phrases were dangerous because they concealed threats.

Others were dangerous because they did not bother.

“Transmit the manifests,” Varik said.

Inside Central Audit, hundreds of data reliquaries stood in ranks beneath sweating pipes. Incense drifted around them in oily sheets. Scribes occupied sunken desks between the machines, their fingers stained black by carbon paper and cheap recaf. Several had continued working through the alarm because nobody had told them that freezing to death constituted authorized leave.

Master Auditor Selene Marr approached at speed.

She was sixty-three, looked ninety and possessed the expression of a woman who had spent her life discovering that numbers were honest only until people learned to write them down.

“My lord governor.”

“Master Auditor. I require the uncorrected tithe manifests.”

Marr stopped.

Every stylus in the hall seemed to stop with her.

“Which year?” she asked.

“All of them.”

A junior clerk made a small religious sound.

Marr looked toward Solvik, then back to Varik.

“The raw ledgers include discrepancies.”

“I am aware.”

“Significant discrepancies.”

“I am becoming more aware by the minute.”

“Some entries may be interpreted as fraud.”

Varik gestured at the frozen condensation hanging from the ceiling.

“Master Auditor, at this point I am hoping for fraud. Fraud can be arrested. Structural thermodynamic collapse is less cooperative.”

Marr considered this and nodded once.

“Archive Nine.”

She turned to her clerks.

“Open the black ledgers.”

Nobody moved.

Marr raised her voice.

“By order of the governor.”

A clerk near the rear swallowed. “The black ledgers are sealed by the Securities Advisory Board.”

Varik looked at Solvik.

Solvik looked genuinely tired.

“I did not issue that seal.”

“No,” Marr said. “Your predecessor did.”

Solvik's predecessor had been dead for forty-one years.

Varik smiled faintly.

“There is something almost reassuring about a conspiracy with proper archival discipline.”

The first black ledger came out of its vault under armed guard.

Then the second.

Then twenty-three more.

The discrepancies began small.

Two thousand laborers reported at a mine that had employed twenty-six hundred.

Three hundred tonnes of reactor-grade ore listed as transit loss.

A habitation block reported vacant while consuming enough heat and protein ration for eight thousand souls.

Then the numbers grew teeth.

Whole work crews existed twice.

Children had been entered as deceased before birth, then appeared eighteen years later in labor quotas.

Ore consignments had left Antegra aboard ships that had never docked.

Heat allocations had been redirected into sealed sectors whose official population was zero.

Krell stood over Marr's shoulder as she opened one ledger after another.

“Where did the heat go?”

Marr pointed to the allocation sigil.

“Substructure Eighteen.”

Krell frowned. “There is no Substructure Eighteen.”

“There is in the thermal accounts.”

Solvik stepped closer.

“There is no Substructure Eighteen on station schematics.”

Marr gave him the weary look auditors reserve for men who have mistaken a map for reality.

“Then something nonexistent has consumed eleven percent of Antegra's deep-core heat for sixty-eight years.”

Varik said nothing.

A vox chime sounded.

External Dock Three reported hard seal.

The *Peregrine of Cinders* had arrived.

Its first message was not a greeting.

It was a work order.

Krell read it aloud.

“By custodial authority and preexisting emergency compact, Mechanicus personnel will assume temporary control of primary thermal distribution, reactor access, pressure systems and substructure transit until completion of inspection.”

Solvik's face hardened. “Preexisting compact?”

“Date?” Varik asked.

Krell scrolled.

His expression changed.

“Sixty-eight years ago.”

Nobody spoke.

Marr slowly turned back toward the black ledger.

Substructure Eighteen had begun consuming heat sixty-eight years ago.

The Mechanicus compact had been signed sixty-eight years ago.

Varik had been governor then.

He did not remember signing it.

That was not impossible. Two centuries of office contained a great many signatures, and rejuvenat treatment was not kind to memory. But governors survived by distrusting coincidences, particularly coincidences bearing their own seal.

“Show me the authorization.”

Krell handed him the slate.

There it was.

TALBOR VARIK, SOVEREIGN GOVERNOR OF ANTEGRA.

His cipher.

His gene-mark.

His private sign.

Perfect.

Varik studied it for a long time.

“I did not sign this.”

Solvik looked at him carefully. “You are certain?”

“No.”

The answer disturbed Krell more than certainty would have.

Varik returned the slate.

“I am certain that I do not remember signing it. At my age those are different legal categories.”

The station vox clicked.

A woman's voice emerged through the static, dry and metallic beneath a Mechanicus harmonizer.

“Governor Varik. This is Custodian Magos Hester Vhal. We have received your manifests.”

Varik glanced at Krell.

“They transmitted?”

Marr nodded. “To every address ordered.”

“Good.”

Vhal continued.

“Your disclosure satisfies the final condition of dormant compact A-Theta-Seventeen. Mechanicus custodial intervention is now mandatory.”

Varik took the vox handset.

“Magos Vhal, I have no memory of this compact.”

“That circumstance is entered in the compact.”

Silence spread through Central Audit.

Varik's fingers tightened around the handset.

“Explain.”

“I am not authorized to explain over unsecured vox.”

“You have just invoked my own signature against me.”

“Correct.”

“And the compact anticipated that I would not remember it.”

“Correct.”

Varik looked at the black ledger.

“Does your compact concern Substructure Eighteen?”

For the first time, Vhal paused.

“Governor, clear the central lifts.”

“That was not an answer.”

“It was the answer most conducive to your continued biological function.”

Krell muttered, “Mechanicus courtesy.”

Vhal heard him.

“Captain Krell is correct.”

The connection ended.

Varik stared at the dead handset.

“I dislike her already.”

“Then she may be trustworthy,” Solvik said.

Varik looked at him.

The Commissar-Keeper shrugged almost imperceptibly.

“Everyone you liked appears to have falsified the tithe.”

A deep vibration passed through the floor.

Not an explosion.

Machinery.

Something vast had begun moving beneath Central Audit.

Dust fell from the rafters. The scribes looked upward. Somewhere far below, locks that did not appear on any station schematic were opening one after another.

Marr watched a thermal display populate with new lines.

“Governor.”

Varik joined her.

A conduit map was drawing itself across the slate.

It descended beneath the known station.

Past the mines.

Past the old reactor vaults.

Past the geological anchor works.

Into a region officially recorded as solid rock.

Heat flowed there in impossible quantity.

So did power.

And life-support.

Krell whispered, “What in the Emperor's name have we been heating?”

Varik watched a lift indicator illuminate on a control panel that had possessed no such indicator a minute before.

SUBSTRUCTURE XVIII — CUSTODIAL ACCESS RESTORED.

The lift began climbing toward them.

Varik rested one hand on his father's saber.

“Apparently,” he said, “the past.”
