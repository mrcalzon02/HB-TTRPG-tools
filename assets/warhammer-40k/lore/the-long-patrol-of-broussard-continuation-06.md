# The Long Patrol of Broussard

## XVI. THE WEIGHT OF THE ROAD

**Author:** Mrcalzon02 / Christopher Vardeman

At fifty metres, the *Hesperant Vale* stopped being a stranded ship and became a ship that could strand itself somewhere worse.

Captain Broussard did not put that in his report.

The recovery chief wanted the next hundred metres in a single pull. He had a clean line, a warm hauler and a crew who had finally begun to believe in their repairs.

Sella Varn listened without looking up from the vibration slate.

"Thirty metres," she said.

"Fifty."

"Thirty."

"We just did fifty."

"And stopped twice. Your memory has become generous."

The chief appealed to Broussard.

Broussard looked at the line drawn across the ice. The surveyed corridor narrowed beyond the eastern turn. Its margins were marked with drilled poles, not promises. The hauler could pull the freighter through, but only if the outer brace continued to take the load at the angles Sella had certified.

"Thirty," he said.

The chief returned to his hauler muttering something about soldiers who had never earned a living moving heavy things.

Kelm had heard him. "For once, captain, somebody else has the pleasure."

"You're enjoying this?"

"Not yet. Ask when I can see the convoy."

The hauler drew tension. The freighter's damaged quarter shuddered once. Sella's hand rose; the recovery chief cut power before she had to speak. A load trace on her slate settled.

"Again," she said.

The second attempt took. The ship moved across the ice at a speed that would have embarrassed a funeral procession.

Twenty metres passed. Then thirty.

"Stop."

The hauler stopped. The ship did not immediately settle; the tow frame flexed against the load and returned, slow enough to watch.

Sella waited for the return trace before sending fitters beneath the starboard quarter.

Broussard checked the time. The convoy had received three progress reports in the last six hours. None had promised a departure hour. That was becoming an unpopular habit with convoy command.

He intended to keep it.

---

The eastern corridor had to be protected as well as measured.

Broussard sent Chimera One ahead with the survey chief, while Chimera Two remained on the inner flank where its plated vision block and missing track pads would not be asked to negotiate a pressure ridge. The other two vehicles covered the open approaches. Their crews watched for predators and for movement in the ice; the two hazards did not announce themselves in the same way.

At the next drill point a surveyor dropped to one knee and laid his sounder flat.

"Hold the line."

His partner raised a red flag.

The driver of Chimera One stopped before the flag finished moving.

Broussard reached them on foot with Gabriel a few paces behind. A narrow white seam crossed the ice between two marked holes. It had not been there when the route was approved.

"Thickness?" Broussard asked.

"Still sound beneath us. The upper plate is opening." The surveyor pointed east. "Could be ordinary contraction. Could reach the next ridge."

"How long to establish which?"

"An hour. Perhaps two."

Gabriel crouched beside the seam without touching it. "Does the ship get to wait where it is?"

"The ship can wait," the surveyor said. "The hauler is on better ice. We can take the strain off."

"Then that's the question answered."

Broussard ordered the line slackened and a new set of holes drilled outside the suspected fracture. The survey chief made the men work back from their last certified point, not forward toward the interesting crack.

One of the soldiers helping carry probes stumbled when his boot caught a frozen cable. Gabriel caught his harness before he fell across the marked edge. The soldier recovered his feet, flushed beneath his frost hood, and tried to apologize.

"Save it for the man who has to count you," Gabriel said.

The soldier looked at the red line and stopped apologizing.

The survey took eighty-seven minutes. The seam terminated short of the pressure ridge. It was not an open channel beneath the ship's route, but its margin had narrowed the approved corridor by two metres.

The survey chief revised his sketch.

"We can use it. Hauler turns later. Smaller angle."

Sella studied the change. "And the ship?"

"Same line through the narrow section. No lateral correction there."

"Then I can certify the next stage. Not the one after it."

Broussard signed the amended movement order. The old drawing remained attached.

Holt, receiving the change over the hard line, repeated both revision numbers correctly. He no longer needed to be told why the discarded route stayed in the record.

---

On the bridge, the refrigeration warning sounded during a period when the ship was doing nothing.

Kelm silenced it and called cargo control.

The temporary environmental bypass had been designed to carry the holds through a repair, not through an expedition conducted thirty metres at a time. Its duty cycle was approaching the limit Sella had given it.

The cargo master came up with a slate of temperatures and a face that suggested he would rather have brought a corpse.

"Forward bank's holding," he said. "Port bank is climbing. Slow, but climbing."

"Why?" Kelm asked.

"The reserve compressor needs its service. We have the men, not the spare seals."

"Where are they?"

"On the hauler."

Kelm looked at Broussard. The recovery package had brought the seals for its own environmental plant. They fitted the freighter's reserve compressor only because the two machines had been built under the same old Imperial procurement specification. They were not surplus.

The hauler's chief was called to the bridge. He arrived angry and became angrier when he learned why.

"If I give you my reserve seals, I have no spare for the hauler."

"If you don't," said the cargo master, "you may have a perfectly serviceable hauler pulling a ship full of spoiled food."

"How long before spoilage?"

The cargo master gave a range, not a number. The range depended on whether the next movement stage succeeded and whether the port bank could be brought down again before the insulation surrendered its stored cold.

Kelm asked for the actual temperatures. The cargo master showed him.

"Not lost yet," Kelm said.

"No, captain."

Broussard had seen enough supply disputes to know when the correct answer was not his to invent.

"Can the hauler's plant be inspected before we release the seals?"

The chief hesitated. "Yes."

"And can you run it without the reserve if the inspection passes?"

"Until something fails."

"That was already true."

The chief glared at him, then called his fitter.

The inspection passed. Kelm signed the transfer as a debt owed by the *Hesperant Vale* to the recovery detachment. The cargo master signed for the seals. The hauler chief signed a warning that the loss of its reserve reduced the recovery margin.

Nobody crossed out the warning.

Sella would not restart the pull until the compressor service was complete. Kelm did not ask her to. He knew what the ship carried, and he knew that the people eating it later would not care how impressive the recovery had looked.

In the forward holds, the cargo master moved his workers along the temperature lines. The rows of loose produce bins stood where they had stood before. Their restraints were checked, their labels counted and their lids left closed. No one was authorized to rummage through perishable cargo while the bypass was under load.

A hand complained that one bin was heavier than its manifest estimate.

"Then write the measured weight," the cargo master said. "Don't lift it twice to prove you're right."

He entered the discrepancy in the cargo working book. It was one of several that day. The manifest had been prepared before the impact and the subsequent shifting, and nobody aboard possessed a reason to treat a single imperfect weight as extraordinary.

The cargo master moved on to the next row.

---

By the time the refrigeration alarm cleared, the sky had darkened into the blue-grey that High Presidio called afternoon.

Broussard's convoy message came back with a request for an estimated return.

He read it once and handed it to Kelm.

"They'll want a number," Kelm said.

"They have a number. Eighty metres."

"They want a time."

"They can have the time of the next report."

Kelm gave a tired laugh. "You realize they can relieve you?"

"Of the recovery command? Gladly. Of the ice? No."

Kelm folded the message.

"I'll countersign your answer."

Broussard looked at him. "You don't have to."

"I have to explain to my owners why their ship is still here. I would rather explain a delay we chose than a wreck we pretended not to foresee."

They sent a joint report: eighty metres of completed movement; new surface fracture surveyed and route amended; refrigeration bypass serviced with recovery-package stores; restricted thrust unchanged; no reliable arrival estimate. The damaged Chimera remained mobile under limitation. The three dead cargo workers remained recorded as casualties of the isolated service-junction fault.

The report made neither man popular.

Gabriel read the copy at the command table.

"An honest document," he said. "A dangerous habit."

"It has worked so far."

"Has it?"

"We're still alive."

Gabriel glanced toward the ship. "Not all of us."

Broussard did not answer immediately. He thought of Noll, Vale and Dorn, and of the empty places at the work parties. Sella had been forced to borrow fitters from tasks they would have finished sooner if the three men were alive.

"No," he said. "Not all."

Gabriel set the copy down. He did not offer absolution. Broussard had not asked for any.

---

The next pull was made in three stages: twenty metres, forty, then seventy.

Each stage ended with a load inspection. At the second, the recovery chief found that one of the ground pulleys had begun to cut a shallow groove in the ice beneath its anchor plate. The metal was sound; the ground beneath it was not.

He asked to move the anchor.

"Where?" Sella asked.

"Two metres inward."

"That's outside the line you gave me."

"Inside the safe corridor."

"Inside the corridor isn't the same as inside the load angle."

The chief took his pencil and drew the new angle. It added a small sideways component to the starboard brace. Small was not zero. Sella rejected it.

He stared at the drawing, then rubbed out the new anchor.

"We can reset farther forward," he said. "Takes another hour."

"Then it takes another hour."

For a moment Broussard expected the chief to appeal again. Instead the man folded the drawing and went to supervise the reset. The refusal had cost him time, but he had learned what kind of answer would be accepted.

At the final stage, the hauler's tracks ground through a patch of windblown snow. The line held. The freighter passed the narrowest point of the amended corridor and came to rest on the next marked shelf.

The movement total stood at two hundred and ten metres from the original recovery position.

The convoy was still beyond the horizon.

Sella examined the brace trace, made a fitter repeat a fastening check, and certified the completed stage. She did not certify the next one.

Holt entered the distance, time and revision numbers in the bridge record. The fourteen-second gap in the older impact recording remained exactly where it had been. Nothing about a successful tow explained it.

Broussard stood beside the hauler while its crew shut down for the next survey.

The recovery chief joined him.

"Two hundred and ten," he said.

"Yes."

"We might actually get there."

"We might."

"Do you ever say anything cheerful?"

Broussard looked at the repaired frame, the marked ice, the distant freighter and the men gathering their tools for another inspection.

"Nobody fell through."

The chief considered that.

"Fair enough."

Beyond the lamps, the surveyed route ended at another line of poles. Beyond those poles lay ice nobody had yet measured under this load.

Broussard ordered the next watch posted before he went inside.
