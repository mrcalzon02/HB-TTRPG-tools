# The Long Patrol of Broussard

## VII. THE WOUND IN THE HULL

**Author:** Mrcalzon02 / Christopher Vardeman  
**Based on the work of:** *The Long Patrol of Broussard*

As they made their final approach, Broussard opened a vox channel to the crippled freighter.


The response was immediate enough.


Just not from the man he had expected.


The vessel manifest listed a Captain Derrovan Kelm as master and commanding officer, and Broussard had assumed—perhaps foolishly—that the captain of a disabled ship would be the first person interested in speaking with the military detachment sent to save it.


Instead the vox answered with the tinny, slightly over-modulated voice of someone painfully junior.


"Freighter Hesperant Vale, bridge receiving."


Broussard glanced at the manifest.


"Captain Broussard, Imperial Guard convoy recovery detachment. Requesting Captain Kelm."


A pause.


Not a long one.


Just long enough to be noticed.


"Yes, sir. Of course, sir. I'll retrieve the captain immediately."


Retrieve.


Broussard looked toward Gabriel.


The commissar raised one eyebrow.


Neither said anything.


There were perfectly legitimate reasons for a ship's captain not to be standing beside the vox at all times.


There were also perfectly legitimate reasons for a junior officer's voice to tighten when asked where his captain was.


Broussard had learned not to confuse possibility with reassurance.


Still, there was work to do.


Arrangements were made quickly enough.


The freighter's surviving maneuvering systems were insufficient to move the vessel but functional enough to maintain its present balance, and its crew could lower personnel access gantries along the port side where the hull remained intact.


Broussard ordered the Chimeras to establish a loose perimeter while the recovery hauler moved forward.


Only then, as they closed to within a few hundred meters, did he properly see the damage.


The surveillance report had called it catastrophic.


For once, the wording lacked imagination.


The ice had not simply punctured the hull.


It had opened it.


A massive spear of pressure-formed ice had struck the lower forward starboard section at an angle, pierced the outer plating, and then dragged backward as the freighter's momentum carried the vessel over it.


The result was a jagged wound stretching along a substantial portion of the lower hull.


Two full gravitic suspension grids had been torn through.


Armor plating had peeled outward in curled sections of blackened metal.


Structural ribs had been crushed or exposed.


Insulation hung from the wound in frozen sheets.


Somewhere along the tear, an internal conduit had ruptured and frozen coolant had painted part of the hull in pale blue-white streaks.


Broussard stared through the vision block.


"That's not an engine failure."


"No," Gabriel said beside him.


"That's an argument with geography."


"The geography appears to have won."


The closer they came, the more details emerged.


One entire distributor bank had been exposed where the hull had been ripped away.


Broussard recognized enough of the layout from the vessel schematics to understand what he was looking at.


Primary electrical distribution.


Forward lift-grid regulation.


Emergency storage capacitors.


And beneath it all, what remained of the forward power reservoir assembly.


Much of that section was simply gone.


Not absent in the clean sense.


Gone in the explosive sense.


The compartment had been torn open around it, plating warped outward in a broad blackened flower.


Broussard could imagine what had happened.


The ice had punched through the hull.


It had crossed energized systems before any automatic breaker could isolate them.


Metal, ice, conductive slurry and high-voltage equipment had met one another in a very small space.


For perhaps a fraction of a second, the internal electrical system had attempted to behave as designed.


Then several thousand volts of stored power had discovered an entirely new route to ground.


The arc must have been magnificent.


A violent white-blue flash beneath the ship.


Power leaping across fractured bus bars.


Control systems burning out simultaneously.


Capacitors discharging through torn metal.


Then darkness.


After that came the more mundane consequences.


Fire.


Pressure loss.


Coolant rupture.


Grid collapse.


Automatic shutdown.


And several thousand tonnes of freighter suddenly discovering that gravity had been patiently waiting underneath it the entire time.


Only the surviving suspension grids had prevented the vessel from slamming completely onto the ice.


The Hesperant Vale now sat crookedly above the frozen surface, its starboard bow riding low while the functioning grids fought continuously to keep the damaged hull from settling.


Broussard did not like the angle.


He liked even less the idea of trusting those surviving grids for several days.


The recovery hauler rolled past the command Chimera.


Its broad industrial tires and auxiliary gravitic skids carried it directly alongside the damaged section.


Floodlights snapped on.


White illumination swept across the hull.


The wound became enormous.


What had seemed severe from a distance now looked almost surgical.


As though some colossal animal had opened the vessel with a claw.


The hauler's articulated arm rose from its cradle.


Servos groaned.


Hydraulics hissed.


Then the long mechanical limb began to pan slowly across the breach.


Sensor packages mounted near the manipulator head scanned the exposed structure while secondary lamps examined individual sections of torn plating.


The Magos was already moving.


Broussard watched through the viewport as the Mechanicus priest descended from the hauler with two enginseers behind him.


The Magos wore heavy environmental robes beneath the traditional red vestments, giving him the appearance of a walking furnace wrapped in religious fabric.


Several mechanical limbs unfolded from beneath his robes as he approached the ship.


One carried an auspex.


Another held a compact diagnostic probe.


A third seemed to have no immediately obvious purpose beyond making Broussard uncomfortable.


The freighter lowered a personnel gantry.


It unfolded from the side of the hull in stages.


First a narrow access platform.


Then a ladder assembly.


Then a hydraulically lowered ramp that extended until its lower end hovered just above the ice.


The whole structure looked fragile beside the bulk of the ship.


The Magos climbed aboard without hesitation.


One enginseer followed.


The other remained outside beside the recovery hauler, already conversing with the machine through a cable attached directly to its diagnostic port.


Broussard suspected the man was technically speaking with the vehicle more politely than most officers spoke to their soldiers.


The Magos would have a long walk ahead of him.


Commercial freighters were built around cargo.


Human convenience came somewhat later.


The external access gantry opened onto the mid-level service corridor, which meant reaching the damaged engineering decks required passing inward through the crew section, descending two primary stairwells, crossing the central machinery spine, and then navigating the maintenance passages around the forward power complex.


The vessel schematic resembled tangled intestines.


Corridors wrapped around storage compartments.


Maintenance shafts cut vertically between decks.


Engineering access ways appeared to have been added whenever someone discovered a new machine that required servicing.


There were probably sections of the ship where two bulkheads had been constructed years apart by people who had never met and disagreed philosophically about where a corridor should exist.


The Magos disappeared inside.


Broussard checked the vox.


"Recovery One, confirm Magos aboard."


"Confirmed."


"External scan?"


"Running now."


"Any immediate structural concern?"


A pause.


"Define immediate."


Broussard closed his eyes briefly.


He hated engineers.


"Will the ship fall over in the next ten minutes?"


"Unlikely."


"Good."


Gabriel gave him a sideways glance.


"High standards."


"I've learned to set achievable objectives."


Outside, the four Chimeras moved into their assigned positions.


Voss took the forward arc.


Orro moved south.


Marr swung north.


Broussard kept his own vehicle near the recovery hauler and the freighter's personnel access point.


Weapons remained ready.


Not aimed.


There was a difference.


The crew of the freighter began appearing along the side gantry.


One at first.


Then three.


Then several more.


They wore insulated commercial void coats stained with grease, smoke and coolant.


Some carried tool bags.


One had a bandage wrapped around his forehead.


Another walked with one arm held stiffly against his chest.


They looked tired.


That was expected.


They also looked nervous.


Less expected.


Broussard climbed out of the Chimera.


The cold struck immediately.


High Presidio had a way of making the inside of an armored vehicle seem luxurious the moment one stepped outside it.


Wind tore at his coat.


Snow moved sideways across the ice.


The damaged freighter towered above him.


Up close, the surviving gravitic grids produced a low pulsing vibration that he could feel through his boots.


Not steady.


Pulsing.


Compensating.


Correcting.


Working harder than they should.


He disliked that too.


Gabriel descended behind him.


The commissar landed on the ice and adjusted his greatcoat.


Broussard glanced over.


"Still like the coat?"


"Very much."


"Good."


They approached the gantry.


A crewman hurried down to meet them.


Young.


Possibly the same junior officer who had answered the vox.


He saluted incorrectly.


Broussard ignored it.


"Captain Broussard?"


"Yes."


"Second Bridge Officer Meren Holt, sir."


There it was.


Second Bridge Officer.


Young enough that his face had not entirely decided whether it wanted to look adult.


Broussard nodded.


"Where's Captain Kelm?"


Holt hesitated.


Again.


The same pause as on the vox.


Small.


But now Broussard could see the man's face when it happened.


"The captain is... occupied, sir."


Broussard felt Gabriel become still beside him.


"Occupied with what?"


"Damage coordination."


Broussard looked toward the freighter.


Then back at Holt.


"Your main engineerium is open to the weather, two suspension grids are destroyed, half your forward power distribution is missing, and Imperial Guard recovery personnel have just arrived under independent command authority."


He kept his voice friendly.


That usually made questions harder to evade.


"I would imagine damage coordination currently involves me."


Holt swallowed.


"Yes, sir."


"So."


Broussard waited.


The wind moved snow between them.


Behind Holt, several freighter crew members had become noticeably interested in nearby machinery.


Finally the junior officer said:


"I'll take you to him."


Broussard nodded.


"Excellent."


He turned toward the Chimera.


"Gabriel?"


The commissar was already looking at him.


"I'll accompany you."


Of course he would.


Broussard had expected that.


He keyed his squad vox.


"Command entering the vessel. Voss assumes perimeter authority. Nobody moves outside assigned sectors without clearing it first."


Acknowledgments returned.


He looked once more at the massive tear in the hull.


The recovery arm continued scanning.


Its lights moved slowly across burned conduits and exposed machinery.


Somewhere deep inside, the Magos was already crawling toward the engineerium.


Somewhere else aboard, a captain had apparently been too occupied to answer his own bridge.


Broussard climbed the gantry.


Gabriel followed.


Second Officer Holt led them upward.


The access hatch opened.


Warm air spilled out.


Not properly warm.


Shipboard emergency warm.


The smell came with it.


Burned insulation.


Electrical smoke.


Coolant.


Hot metal.


And something else beneath it.


Faint.


Organic.


Broussard paused at the threshold.


Gabriel noticed.


"What?"


Broussard breathed in again.


The smell was already fading beneath the ventilation system.


"Nothing."


He stepped inside.


The hatch closed behind them.


It was possible the freighter had merely suffered exactly the disaster described in the report.


A navigational failure.


An ice strike.


An electrical cascade.


A crippled ship waiting for rescue.


That would have been unfortunate.


But ordinary.


Broussard had spent enough years in uniform to know that ordinary disasters generally did not make junior bridge officers hesitate when asked where their captain was.


And they certainly did not make an entire ship's crew avoid looking at the officers who had come to save them.
