# Scion of the Warp-Born Blood — The Toll at the Inner Gate

**Based on the work of:** *Scion of the Warp-Born Blood*  
**Original story by:** Christopher Vardeman / Mrcalzon02  
**Continuation:** The Toll at the Inner Gate

---

The first indication of trouble was not a distress call. It was a blank space in a list.

Mulvane had been waiting in the shielded antechamber of a Mechanicus drydock, where the walls were too thick for ordinary voices to carry and the air tasted faintly of boiled copper. He had been told to expect a tithe carrier requiring assistance on the inward passage. The manifest on the table named its cargo, its captain, the weight of its engines, and the fee that House Drenal would collect for the use of his sight. Beside that entry there ought to have been the names of the other vessels waiting at the outer rendezvous.

There were three names. A fourth line had been scraped clean.

He looked at the clerk. The clerk was not a member of the House. He wore the drydock's grey, and his thumb bore a small burn where he had been too slow moving a sealing iron.

"Was there a fourth?"

"Yes, lord."

"Where is it?"

The clerk studied the table, as if the missing ship might be concealed under the lacquer. "Not in the current assignment."

That was a careful answer. Mulvane had been raised among careful answers. They were useful because they identified the places where a person had decided not to lie.

He asked for the earlier manifest.

The clerk's hand stopped just short of the drawer. "I would need authorization."

"Then request it."

"From the House?"

"From whoever removed the line."

The man bowed and went away. Mulvane returned to the sheet. A tithe carrier could wait a few hours without its grain becoming less edible. The other entries belonged to a pilgrim transport and a repair tender, both under routine restrictions. Their captains would resent the delay and pay the necessary fees. The fourth vessel, whatever it had been, had disappeared from the arrangement entirely.

Beyond the antechamber, machinery groaned against the drydock's restraints. The sound came through the floor rather than the walls. Mulvane had learned, as a child, to distinguish noises that promised pain from noises that merely accompanied it. This one belonged to a cargo cradle under load.

The clerk returned with an older sheet, its corner already cut to show that it was superseded. He put it down without looking at Mulvane.

The erased vessel was a hospital lighter.

It had carried patients from a fever-struck transport to a receiving station whose berths were already full. The lighter's own Navigator had suffered an injury during the previous translation, and the ship had missed its allotted approach. Its life-support stores were counted in hours rather than days. It had not vanished from the system. It had been removed from the list of vessels eligible for House assistance because the contracted tithe carrier held the next reserved passage.

Mulvane read the time of the lighter's last signal. Then he read it again.

"Who made the change?"

"The allocation office, lord."

"On whose instruction?"

"The seal is valid."

"That was not my question."

The clerk's burned thumb pressed against his other palm. "The instruction came through House Drenal."

Mulvane folded the older manifest beneath the newer one. He did not ask the clerk to break the seal. He had enough information to know that the order was real, and not enough to know whether the people who had issued it understood the condition of the vessel they had displaced.

He had been trained to endure pain. He had not been trained to mistake a schedule for the thing it described.

---

The House's liaison answered on the second summons. His image appeared in the small brass frame above the table, grainy at the edges where the shielding interfered with the signal. Mulvane knew the voice. It had approved his movements for years, though he could not have said what the man's face looked like without the frame.

"The tithe carrier is ready," the liaison said. "You are late."

"There is a hospital lighter outside the assigned passage."

"There is no hospital lighter on your manifest."

"There was."

A pause. Not surprise. The man had expected him to find it.

"The receiving station refused priority clearance. The vessel has no standing under our agreement."

"How many patients?"

"That is not material to your commission."

"How many?"

"Seven hundred and fourteen registered. Their attendants and ship's company are additional."

Mulvane had seen the great ships carry ten thousand souls into a dock with no more ceremony than a cargo tally. Seven hundred and fourteen was a small number by the standards of the Imperium. It was also enough people to fill the ward where he had once been made to watch his failed cousins breathe through tubes.

"The lighter cannot remain outside the approach," he said.

"Then its owners should have contracted earlier."

"Did they know the Navigator would be injured?"

"Do not make a moral argument out of a commercial failure, Seventh Scion. You have been given a passage and a fee."

Mulvane considered the two sheets. He knew the House's contracts. They had been read to him when he was young, repeated during his training, and tested in him until he could recall their exceptions while bleeding. The reservation protected a client's order in the queue. It did not require him to navigate into a hazardous approach if the route had become unsafe.

"There is a hazard," he said.

The liaison's expression hardened. "State it."

"Uncontrolled traffic near the outer rendezvous. A vessel with failing life support and an impaired Navigator. If its captain attempts an unaided translation to save the patients, the approach becomes unsafe for every vessel in the queue."

"You are speculating."

"Yes. I have not yet examined the lighter. Send me its current signals and I will tell you whether the speculation is justified."

The liaison's gaze moved away from the frame. Someone beside him spoke too softly for Mulvane to hear. The man returned his attention.

"You will complete the contracted passage."

"I intend to."

"First."

"After I determine whether the route remains safe."

The transmission ended.

Mulvane sat very still. He had not refused the House. A refusal would have been simpler to understand and much harder to survive. He had asked a question that its own procedures permitted him to ask. The distinction was narrow enough to hurt.

He remembered the rite at fourteen: the chamber, the binding cloth, the command to look. Pain had become direction only because he had continued looking after every sensible part of his body begged him to turn away.

There was no light in the antechamber worth looking at. He waited for the signals.

---

The lighter's transmissions arrived in fragments. A receiving station had forwarded the last reliable reports, then a shipmaster's plea recorded through a failing transmitter. The master did not ask for a miracle. She asked for an approach slot, a qualified guide, and a place to discharge her patients. The infirmary had lost one refrigeration loop. Two compartments had been sealed to preserve air. The ship had enough stores to wait, but not enough to wait indefinitely, and the captain had begun reducing power to the ward lights.

The House's liaison had not been wrong about the contract. Nor had the lighter's master been wrong to demand a place in the queue. Both were operating inside systems that had been built to allocate scarce assistance. Neither could alter the other's authority.

Mulvane requested a direct connection.

The shipmaster appeared with a strip of dressing beneath one ear and the exhaustion of a person who had stopped trying to conceal it. Behind her, a man was carrying a folded stretcher past the bridge hatch.

"I have no money for a Drenal," she said before he could speak.

"I have not asked for any."

"They told us your House had declined."

"It has."

She watched him without answering. He could see the calculation in her face. A powerful stranger was offering attention that the allocation office had denied. That might be rescue. It might be the beginning of a worse bill.

"Tell me what your Navigator can do."

"Nothing useful. He can hear us. He cannot keep his eye open without convulsing."

"Has he been examined?"

"By our medicae officer. We have no replacement."

"Then do not put him back in the chair."

"We were considering a short translation."

"Do not."

The word came out harder than Mulvane intended. The shipmaster stiffened.

He moderated his voice. "I know what happens when a wounded Navigator tries to follow the beacon this close to Terra. Your vessel may survive the attempt. The people inside it may not."

"What would you have me do?"

"Hold your present position. Send me your power and atmosphere figures. Keep your engines available for a conventional rendezvous."

"The station says it cannot receive us."

"I have not yet spoken to the station."

She gave a short, bitter laugh. "You think that will help?"

"No. I think it will tell us which problem is real."

For the first time she looked directly at him rather than at the emblem beside his name.

"How long?"

"I cannot promise a time until I have the figures."

She nodded once. That was the answer a captain could use. Mulvane cut the connection and ordered the drydock to prepare a transfer cutter. The clerk with the burned thumb asked whether the House had approved.

"No," Mulvane said. "The examination has not been completed."

The clerk looked at the waiting cutter order. He understood that it was not quite an answer, and also that it was enough to begin moving men and fuel. He stamped the requisition.

---

The receiving station had room for the patients, though not in its official wards.

That fact took forty minutes to establish and another twenty to make useful. The station's superintendent had refused the lighter because its medical deck was at capacity. The freight quarantine hold, on the other hand, had been cleared two days earlier and had its own air and water systems. It was not certified to receive living patients. Converting it would require medicae personnel, portable cots, an inspection, and a signature from someone prepared to accept responsibility for using a freight compartment as a ward.

Mulvane did not order the superintendent to break her rules. She was protecting people already inside the station, and a careless arrival could make their condition worse.

"How many of your patients would have to be moved?" he asked.

"Forty-three," she said. "And I will not put them beside incoming fever cases."

"Then keep them where they are. Use the quarantine hold for the lighter."

"Who pays for the conversion?"

The question was not callous. Without a charge code, the superintendent could not obtain the extra filters, sterilizing crews, and medicae staff. The work would begin with willing hands and end when someone refused to release the supplies.

Mulvane asked for the conversion estimate. The figure was smaller than a single day's fee for his own service.

He had no independent fortune to offer. His House provided his chambers, his treatments, his attendants, his regalia, and the vessels that carried him. Even the account in his name was a House account, and he had never considered what it meant to spend it without instruction.

"Charge it to my service allocation," he said.

The superintendent stared at him. "That allocation is for your maintenance."

"Then it will maintain something."

She was not amused. She asked for a signed authorization, and he gave one. She warned him that the charge would be examined. He knew.

A second problem remained. The lighter was too far from the station for a quick conventional transfer. The cutter could reach it, but towing a damaged hospital vessel through crowded approach traffic would consume hours. The original reservation had been built around the tithe carrier's mass and route. If Mulvane took the lighter first, the tithe carrier would miss its slot and the delay would propagate through a convoy whose grain was already promised to receivers below.

He studied the trajectories and made the calculation himself. The cutter could carry him to the lighter. He could guide the lighter to a safe point outside the station's traffic control boundary. From there, local pilots could bring it in under ordinary engines. The tithe carrier could retain most of its reserved passage, provided the two operations did not overlap.

The difficulty was not distance. It was the beacon.

He would have to open his sight twice in a period that his House physicians ordinarily kept clear after one inner-system passage. The first opening would be brief. The second, with a heavy tithe carrier and its convoy, would not.

He had endured worse. That did not mean there was no cost.

---

The transfer cutter was little more than a sealed compartment wrapped around engines. Its pilot had flown Navigators before and did not try to speak to Mulvane during the journey. The man's silence was a professional courtesy. Mulvane appreciated it.

At the lighter's airlock, a medicae orderly met him with a mask and a warning about fever exposure. Mulvane accepted the mask. He had spent a lifetime under chemical discipline, but he was not immune to infection, and the House would not forgive him for becoming useless through carelessness.

The shipmaster waited on the bridge. She had found time to change the dressing beneath her ear. Her injured Navigator lay in a curtained compartment nearby, conscious but unable to bear the light of the bridge displays.

Mulvane visited him first.

The man was older than he had expected, with the pale, thin skin of someone who had lived for years beneath shielding. He turned his head at Mulvane's approach.

"House Drenal," he whispered.

"Yes."

"Don't let them open me again."

"I will not."

The man's fingers loosened on the blanket. Mulvane did not ask him what he had seen. A Navigator could spend the rest of his life trying to describe a moment that had nearly destroyed him, and the description would not necessarily help another Navigator survive it.

On the bridge, Mulvane examined the controls. The lighter's navigation station was crude but functional. He asked for a clean feed from the ship's position instruments, a shielded chair, and a crew member to watch the physical readings while he worked. The shipmaster took that position herself.

"You have done this before?" she asked.

"Often."

"Twice in one shift?"

He looked at her. She had noticed the second assignment on his service slate.

"Not willingly."

She considered that answer and gave no speech of gratitude. She ordered the crew to prepare for translation.

Mulvane settled into the chair and loosened the bindings over his third eye. He had known the Astronomican as a wound before he had known it as a guide. Its presence near Terra was not a lamp shining down a road. It was pressure, heat, and the sense of being observed by something too vast to notice an individual observer. The trained mind could take bearings from that immensity. The untrained mind could drown in it.

He opened his eye.

Pain entered him without transition. It did not arrive as a blade or a blow; those had edges and endings. This was an entire direction of existence made unbearable. For an instant he saw nothing of the lighter, the captain, or the little bridge. There was only the blazing reference by which he had learned to distinguish one current of the Immaterium from another.

He did not fight the light. He measured against it.

The lighter moved. Its engines answered late, then correctly. Mulvane felt the ship's mass as a resistance in the course, a slow reluctance that could not be hurried without risking the hull. He held the line, adjusted, and gave the shipmaster three brief corrections. She repeated them to her helmsman without explanation. She had no need to know what he saw. She needed the numbers and the moment to use them.

The passage was short. It felt longer than the years between his rite and his first vessel.

When he shut his eye, the bridge returned in pieces. The captain was still beside him. One of the displays had cracked under a pressure change. The ship had reached its assigned safe point. Local pilots were already answering the station's signal.

He found blood on the cloth in his hand.

"Are you hurt?" the shipmaster asked.

"Yes."

"Can you stand?"

"Not yet."

She ordered the bridge cleared except for the helmsman. Mulvane sat until his fingers stopped shaking enough to fasten the bindings again. He had promised the House one passage. He had given another ship the first of his sight. The difference was now written in his body.

---

The tithe carrier's captain had been waiting through every minute of the lighter's approach. His first message was a complaint about contractual priority. His second was an amended complaint acknowledging that the lighter had reached the station. The third requested a new departure time.

Mulvane sent him one.

The captain did not thank him. He had a ship full of grain, a schedule of receivers, and a crew whose hours were being consumed by a dispute they had not caused. Mulvane preferred his anger to a performance of gratitude. It was honest and it belonged to the man who would have to explain the delay.

The House liaison contacted him as he returned to the cutter.

"You have made a charge against your maintenance account."

"Yes."

"You have incurred a deviation."

"Yes."

"You have used your sight outside the contracted order."

"The lighter was a hazard to the approach."

"The lighter is now inside the receiving boundary. The hazard is gone."

"Then the contracted passage may proceed."

The liaison's mouth tightened. Mulvane knew the House would find a way to describe the day's events in a ledger. A deviation could become a disciplinary hearing, an adjustment to his allocation, a mark against future assignments, or nothing at all. The uncertainty was itself a form of control. He had lived inside it long enough to know that.

"You will present yourself for examination when the tithe carrier is delivered."

"Of course."

"And the next time you discover an unauthorized vessel, you will report it before taking action."

"I reported it."

"You know what I mean."

Mulvane did. He had been trained to understand what people meant when their words were less precise than their authority.

The liaison ended the call. The cutter's pilot did not look at Mulvane. He had heard enough to know that a Navigator's rank did not necessarily make him free.

The second passage began with a tremor in Mulvane's left hand. He hid it under the chair's restraint. The tithe carrier was heavier than the lighter, and its engines took longer to answer each correction. Its captain had demanded the earliest possible slot and now obeyed every instruction without argument. A practical man, he understood that the safest way to recover lost time was not to lose more of it.

Mulvane opened his eye again.

The Astronomican was no kinder because he had already suffered it once. The brightness came through his mind with the indifferent force of weather. He held his course by disciplines that had been drilled into him before he understood their purpose: breath, count, image, correction, rest. He had been made for this. The thought had comforted him when he was young. Now it was simply true, and truth did not lessen the pain.

The carrier completed its approach. Its grain would reach the receivers later than promised, but it would reach them. The captain confirmed the handover and sent the expected certificate of service. Mulvane signed it with his right hand because his left would not hold the stylus.

No one on the bridge remarked upon the change.

---

The examination took place in a room without windows. A House physician measured the response of Mulvane's pupils, the tremor in his hand, the time it took him to answer simple questions, and the way his third eye reacted when a shielded test lamp was uncovered. The physician had examined him after dozens of passages. He did not pretend the readings were normal.

"Two openings," he said.

"Yes."

"Without the recovery interval."

"Yes."

The physician wrote for some time. He did not ask why. He was not a judge, and Mulvane had learned to value the rare people who understood the limits of their own office.

"How long until the tremor resolves?"

"If it resolves as it has before, several days. There may be headaches. No third opening until I have tested you again."

"That will affect the manifest."

"The manifest is not a nervous system."

Mulvane almost smiled. The physician had said something similar years earlier, when a House officer had wanted him returned to service before his fever broke. The officer had won that argument. The physician had been correct anyway.

The House liaison entered after the examination. He carried a new sheet, bearing Mulvane's name and the charges against his service allocation. The quarantine hold conversion had been paid. The lighter's passage was entered as an uncontracted act. The tithe carrier's certificate was accepted, but the deviation remained under review.

"Your account will carry the cost," the liaison said.

"That is what I authorized."

"The House may also recover damages if the tithe carrier's delay produces a claim."

"Has it?"

"Not yet."

"Then the claim is not yet a debt."

The liaison watched him. Mulvane knew that he was pushing at the edge of what a Seventh Scion might say without being called insolent. He did not push further.

The man put down a second document. It was the physician's restriction, already countersigned. For the next several days, Mulvane would not be assigned another inner passage. Another Drenal would take the work. Mulvane's absence would be recorded as a temporary reduction in utility.

He read the line twice. The House had spent decades training him to withstand the light, and a single additional passage had made him less useful for a time. The cost was real. He could feel it in his fingers.

"Was the hospital lighter received?" he asked.

The liaison glanced at the sheet. "All living patients were transferred. The station has requested reimbursement for its additional staff."

"How many died before transfer?"

"Three are listed. The medical report is not final."

Mulvane did not know their names. He did not know whether an earlier slot would have saved them. He would not pretend that reaching the station had made the passage a triumph.

The liaison collected the papers. "You have made yourself expensive."

"I was expensive already."

For a moment the man seemed about to rebuke him. Instead he left.

Mulvane remained at the table until the physician returned with a cup of water and a bitter draught. He swallowed both. The physician waited for him to finish and took the empty cup away.

---

On the following watch, the clerk from the drydock brought a corrected manifest to Mulvane's recovery chamber. The man's thumb was bandaged now. The tithe carrier appeared in its proper place. The pilgrim transport and repair tender had been reassigned. The hospital lighter had been added to the record with its actual arrival time and a note directing any further claims to House Drenal's allocation office.

There was no blank line.

"Will the House remove it again?" the clerk asked.

Mulvane looked at the new sheet. He could not answer for the House. He had been born into it, raised by it, used by it, and made valuable by it. He understood its habits, but not all of its intentions.

"The station has a copy," he said. "So does the shipmaster. The carrier's captain has the revised schedule."

The clerk nodded. Those were ordinary records held by people with ordinary reasons to keep them. They did not guarantee justice. They made disappearance more difficult.

"Thank you, lord."

"For the manifest?"

"For asking."

The clerk left before Mulvane could answer.

Alone, Mulvane lifted his left hand. The tremor remained. It was small enough that a stranger might not have noticed and large enough that he could not ignore it. He thought of the injured Navigator on the lighter, of the shipmaster who had not begged for mercy, and of the three people who had died despite the passage. He thought, too, of the tithe carrier's captain, whose grain had arrived late because Mulvane had chosen to spend time that was not wholly his to spend.

He did not discover a new freedom in any of this. House Drenal still held his name, his body, and the bloodline it had already taken from him. His next commission would come when the physician permitted it. The Astronomican would still burn. The manifest would still arrive with its prices and its priorities.

But he had seen a blank space and asked what belonged there.

For now, that was the whole of the difference.

Outside the recovery chamber, the drydock machinery continued its work. Cargo moved, ships waited, clerks argued, and another Navigator somewhere beyond the shielding prepared to look into the light. Mulvane closed his ordinary eyes and rested, because the next passage would require him to see.
