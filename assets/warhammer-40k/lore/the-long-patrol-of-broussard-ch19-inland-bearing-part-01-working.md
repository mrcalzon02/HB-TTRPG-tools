# The Long Patrol of Broussard — XIX: The Inland Bearing (working insert, part 1)

**Placement:** After the existing Chapter XIX watch report. Noncanonical until integrated with the main working manuscript and continuity catalog.

The survey chief had promised to check the lateral bearing before another pull. By morning he was standing over the figures with his injured hand freshly dressed and his good one pressed flat against the table.

The anchor was sound at rest. Under a sideways test, the bearing wandered outside the allowance on the provisional sheet. It had not failed. That was the difficulty: the metal still looked trustworthy to anyone who had not read the numbers.

The hauler chief studied the sheet. "We've pulled against worse."

"Then you've been fortunate," Sella Varn said.

"Fortunate enough to move a freighter four hundred metres."

"Four hundred and four," Holt said from the doorway.

Nobody thanked him.

The survey chief drew a line through the proposed bearing, leaving the failed figures beside it. His assistant, who had taken the last two soundings while the chief's hand shook too much to hold the probe, laid down another drawing.

"There's a second position," the assistant said. "Farther inland. Same route, different angle."

The shelf had been sounded the previous afternoon. Its cross-bearing had not been tested under load.

"So we move my machine, take the strain, and go," the hauler chief said.

"No," Broussard replied. "We move it unloaded, prove the anchor, and decide whether we go."

"That is the second time you've made one job sound like three."

"They are three."

The assistant measured the distance between marks. His chief watched, then handed him the pencil without comment.

---

The relocation consumed the middle of the watch.

The hauler moved inland under its own power, the cable slack. Hessel walked ahead with two marker men, checking that the machine stayed on certified ice. He had complained yesterday about being assigned as a signpost. A signpost that had to walk was, in his opinion, a more elaborate insult.

At the new position Sella refused to attach the freighter. The proof line went instead to a deadweight sled.

"If it holds that, it will hold the ship?" the hauler chief asked.

"If it holds that, we will know it held that."

The first proof stopped at the second load increment. The lateral gauge rose sharply. The anchor did not move, but a thin ridge of frost cracked away from one edge of its plate.

Gabriel, stationed with the outer observer, heard the halt over the hard line. Far beyond the markers, a dark patch appeared in the ice. Through the field glasses it proved to be grit trapped in an old pressure seam. Neither man reported an animal where none could be identified.

Hessel checked the ice beneath the broken frost with the hand sounder. Its measured depth held. He waited for the survey assistant's second reading before signalling that the marker ground remained sound.
