# House Vallimere's Sons

## Continuation V — The Minutes Between Orders

The first evidence that the pilot was working arrived in a stack of beautifully written reports.

Prite disliked them immediately.

Thirty-one exercises had been completed since the review board approved the new assessment method. Cadets were documenting the information available to them, the alternatives they considered and the reasons for their decisions. Their instructors were documenting their judgments in turn. Appeals had not stopped, but they had become specific. The curriculum office had begun referring to this as an improvement in institutional confidence.

Renault read the summary over breakfast.

"Nobody has lost a refinery for an improper reason this week."

"Excellent," Darcelle said.

"Several have lost them for very proper reasons."

Prite turned the page.

The casualty figures were not, by themselves, extraordinary. A difficult simulation ought to be capable of killing people. The Academy's previous exercises had been built around victory conditions that encouraged officers to spend simulated lives as though they were ammunition. Their own pilot had begun correcting that habit.

Something else had changed.

Orders were arriving later.

At first he thought the times had been copied from the wrong clock. Then he found the same pattern in four different examination records. A cadet who had once issued a questionable order in ninety seconds was now issuing a defensible one in six minutes. Another had spent eleven minutes documenting why a convoy should turn away from an ambush, and lost the convoy while the explanation was still being composed.

The reports were excellent.

The ships were dead.

Darcelle leaned over his shoulder. His breakfast went untouched.

"That's not an interface fault."

"You haven't looked."

"I've looked at the timing tables. The machine took their orders when it received them."

"Then why are they waiting?"

Renault set down his cup.

"Because we taught them that somebody will read what they thought."

Prite looked at the page again. He had been pleased when the first appeal established that an officer's decision should be judged against the information available at the time. The distinction mattered. Cadet Commander Solren had made a costly decision, and the Academy had finally learned how to examine it without pretending the dead were merely a failed arithmetic exercise.

The cadets had learned something too.

They had learned that a good explanation could survive a bad outcome.

Prite gathered the reports.

"Simulation Hall Four."

Renault looked mournfully at the remaining pastry.

"I assume this is why we aren't allowed to have mornings."

---

The exercise in Hall Four was already under way when they arrived.

Three cadet command teams occupied the stations. Each controlled a convoy approaching a contested orbital anchorage. The convoy carried replacement water processors, field medicae supplies and the personnel needed to install them. A damaged escort had fallen behind. An enemy raider was somewhere beyond the outer sensor net. The anchorage commander had warned that the harbor could not remain open indefinitely.

None of this was unusual. The Academy had used variations on the problem for years.

The unusual part was how little happened.

On the central display, Command Four's convoy continued toward the anchorage at reduced speed. Its escort was transmitting an intermittent drive warning. A navigation buoy had gone dark. The cadet commander had three reasonable choices and insufficient evidence to know which was best.

He had not chosen any of them.

Prite watched the order queue. A proposed turn appeared, remained for twenty seconds, then vanished. A request for additional sensor confirmation replaced it. That, too, vanished. The cadet entered a note explaining why he could not yet establish whether the buoy failure was hostile action or maintenance decay.

The convoy moved another twenty thousand kilometers.

"He's thinking," the observing instructor said.

"Yes," Prite answered.

The instructor sounded relieved. "The pilot is encouraging deliberate command."

Darcelle pointed to the escort's drive temperature.

"What's that doing?"

The instructor glanced at the display.

"Rising."

"Is anyone doing anything about it?"

"Command Four has requested an engineering estimate."

"From whom?"

"The escort."

Darcelle waited.

The estimate came back. The escort's engineering officer recommended reducing load or accepting a risk of drive seizure.

The cadet added the report to his assessment record. He requested a second estimate from the convoy's senior engineer.

Prite felt the familiar desire to reach across the console and press a button on someone else's behalf. He resisted it. That was not teaching.

The outer harbor sent a final traffic advisory. The convoy had a narrow window to enter before defensive mines were armed across the approach. The cadet read it. He marked it acknowledged.

He requested confirmation that the mine deployment would proceed on schedule.

Renault folded his arms.

"Perhaps the mines will wait for the paperwork."

The observing instructor gave him a disapproving look. Renault did not pursue the joke.

A raider contact appeared at the edge of the sensor net. It might have been a hostile ship. It might have been a decoy. The convoy had time to turn, accelerate or detach its damaged escort.

Command Four requested identification.

The raider fired.

By the time the cadet issued his first movement order, the damaged escort had lost propulsion. The convoy turned too late to reach the harbor before the mines armed. Its supply ships survived the initial attack but were stranded outside the defenses with a wounded escort and diminishing fuel.

The simulation ended twenty-six minutes later.

Command Four's report contained six pages of reasoning and a correct account of every uncertainty he had faced.

Prite looked at the casualty estimate.

"Who taught him to do that?"

The instructor stiffened.

"To exercise caution?"

"To wait until the situation stopped being uncertain."

The man opened his mouth, then closed it.

Prite knew he had been unfair. The instructor had not ordered the cadet to wait. He had rewarded the conduct the assessment process made easiest to defend.

That was worse than a single bad instruction.

---

The cadet came to the debrief expecting an argument about the raider.

Prite asked about the buoy.

The cadet frowned.

"Sir?"

"When it went dark, what did you think?"

"Possible hostile interference. Possible equipment failure."

"Reasonable. What did you do?"

"Requested confirmation."

"And while you waited?"

The cadet glanced at the display.

"Maintained course."

"Why?"

"The harbor approach was still open. I didn't want to abandon the mission on uncertain evidence."

"Also reasonable. How long could you afford to wait?"

The cadet hesitated.

Prite did not help him.

"I didn't calculate a specific limit, sir."

"Why not?"

"I was gathering enough information to make a defensible decision."

The word hung between them.

Defensible.

Prite remembered Solren sitting alone in the appeal chamber with a casualty list in front of her. She had not asked to be defended from consequences. She had asked that the Academy judge the decision she actually made, rather than the decision an instructor wished she had made after the result was known.

The cadet before him had taken that protection and made it into a method of postponing risk.

Prite did not blame him. The Academy had made a career of teaching people to optimize whatever it measured.

"Your decision to keep moving was a decision," he said.

"Yes, sir."

"Then why isn't it in your record?"

The cadet looked down.

Prite brought up the order log. The convoy had maintained course for eleven minutes while its commander asked questions. During those minutes it had passed the last useful turn point, lost the opportunity to detach the escort under harbor protection, and approached the raider's weapons envelope.

"You recorded six things you weren't sure about," Prite said. "You didn't record what waiting would cost."

The cadet stared at the screen.

"I thought the correct procedure was to establish certainty."

"No."

Prite stopped. He had nearly turned the debrief into a lecture about the Academy's own failings. The cadet had a narrower problem.

"What could you have done with the information you had?"

"Turned away."

"At a cost."

"Missed the harbor window. Delayed the processors."

"Yes."

"Or detached the escort and accelerated."

"At a cost."

"Yes, sir."

"Or continued."

The cadet looked at the dead escort.

"At a cost."

Prite nodded.

The cadet swallowed.

"Will I fail?"

"That depends on whether you understood the time limit you were under."

"I didn't."

"Then you have identified something worth learning."

The instructor at the rear of the room made a note. Prite wondered whether it would be called a lesson or a procedural deficiency. It would probably become both.

---

Darcelle spent the afternoon in the maintenance gallery beneath the hall.

He had insisted on inspecting the timing records before anyone changed the assessment language. Prite understood why. A policy based on inaccurate clocks was worse than a bad policy because it could be wrong with great confidence.

The gallery smelled of warm insulation and old cleaning solvent. Darcelle knew the machines by the pitch of their fans. He had spent years hiding from the Academy inside their maintenance spaces. Now he had an official key, two technicians assigned to help him, and an instructor's seal authorizing access to records he had once stolen.

He found the discrepancy after forty minutes.

"Here."

Prite looked at the screen.

There were two clocks. One marked when a cadet drafted an order. The other marked when the simulation accepted it. The difference had always existed. In ordinary exercises it was trivial: a commander might draft a course change, inspect it, then issue it seconds later.

Under the pilot, cadets were drafting orders and holding them while they wrote their reasoning. The evaluation record preserved the proposed order as evidence of an alternative considered. It did not show, on the instructor's summary page, how long that alternative remained unissued.

The information had not been lost. It had been placed where nobody assessing command behavior was looking.

"Can you expose the interval?" Prite asked.

"Already can. It's in the raw log."

"On the review display?"

"Not without changing the report layout."

Renault appeared at the gallery stairs carrying the last of the day's coffee.

"How many pages?"

Darcelle did not look up.

"One column."

"That seems survivable."

"It has to show what was possible when the order was drafted and what remained possible when it was issued."

"Two columns, then."

Darcelle gave him a look.

Renault set the coffee down and stepped aside.

The two assigned technicians were watching. Darcelle noticed them and, for an instant, looked like the man who had once expected every uniform in the Academy to be a death sentence.

Then he turned the display toward them.

"Help me check the clock offsets. Don't assume the draft timestamp is the decision timestamp. A cadet can think about an order for as long as he likes. We need to know when the ship was actually told to move."

The senior technician nodded and began tracing the synchronization chain.

Prite watched Darcelle work. His confidence had not appeared all at once. It came when the problem was something he could touch, test and prove. The difference was that he no longer waited for permission to explain it.

They ran the first comparison against Command Four.

The cadet had drafted the escort-detachment order three minutes before the raider fired.

He had never issued it.

Prite studied the unexecuted line.

There was no way to know whether the order would have saved the escort. That was not the point. The order had been possible, then it had not been. The report had treated both moments as though they were the same.

"Don't score the draft as action," Prite said.

Darcelle nodded.

"And don't score hesitation as cowardice without the context."

"I know."

They both did.

That made the work harder.

---

The curriculum office objected before dinner.

Not to the new column. It objected to the proposed use of it.

"You're introducing a timing penalty," the senior officer said.

"No," Darcelle answered.

"Your own proposal says the duration of an unissued order will be considered."

"Yes."

"Then cadets will be punished for deliberation."

"Only if deliberation has a cost they failed to consider."

The officer glanced at Prite.

Prite kept quiet. Darcelle had built the evidence. He deserved to explain it.

The officer tapped the amended assessment form.

"We have just established a right of appeal against hindsight judgments. Are you now proposing that instructors fail cadets for not acting quickly enough?"

Darcelle looked at the page, then at the officer.

"Not quickly enough for what?"

"The tactical situation."

"Exactly. The situation. Not a stopwatch."

Renault, seated beside him, slid a set of logs across the table.

"Command Four had a damaged escort, a closing harbor, and an unidentified contact. He could wait. He did wait. Waiting changed his options."

The officer examined the logs.

"The contact could have been harmless."

"Yes," Renault said.

"Then an early turn might have wasted fuel and missed the harbor."

"Yes."

"And a detached escort might have been lost unnecessarily."

"Yes."

The officer frowned. "You agree with every objection."

"Because they're good objections."

Prite leaned forward.

"We're not asking you to reward action. We're asking you to recognize inaction as a choice. The cadet should have been able to say: I can wait four minutes for a better sensor return, but after that the harbor window closes, so I will turn whether the return comes or not. He didn't establish that limit. Neither did the assessment."

The officer read the draft again.

"How do you prevent instructors from inventing a deadline afterward?"

Darcelle was ready.

"Preserve the scenario's original time windows. No new deadlines after the run. The raw logs are sealed when the exercise ends. If the scenario didn't contain a meaningful limit, the evaluator cannot pretend one existed."

That was the part Prite liked best. The machinery would not tell the cadet which choice was correct. It would prevent an instructor from rewriting the conditions under which the choice was made.

The officer considered the screen.

"And existing assessments?"

"Unchanged," Darcelle said. "This is a pilot correction. We do not retroactively fail cadets for a criterion we failed to teach."

The officer nodded slowly.

"One trial class. Observe before amendment."

Prite had expected an argument lasting until midnight.

Renault looked almost disappointed.

The officer gathered the pages.

"I should warn you that this will generate more reports."

"We've noticed," Prite said.

---

They did not rerun Command Four's battle.

That would have allowed the cadets to fight an enemy they already knew.

Instead Darcelle built a new exercise from the same class of constraints. A hospital convoy needed to cross a disputed approach. Its escort could protect it against one known threat but not two. A relief tug was available for a limited interval. Sensor reports arrived late, and some were wrong. The harbor commander had authority to close the route if a mine warning became credible.

The scenario was not a trap designed to prove that fast orders were good.

In one branch, an early turn would waste the only safe fuel reserve. In another, a hasty detachment would strand the escort beyond rescue. The enemy was not obliged to attack where the instructor wanted the cadet to look.

Prite watched the trial class from the gallery.

The cadet at Command Two received an uncertain mine warning. She requested a second report. Then she asked the tug how long it could remain available.

"Six minutes," the tug answered.

She ordered the convoy to reduce speed and the escort to prepare a towing connection. She did not yet commit either vessel to the tug.

The observing instructor marked the action.

The cadet turned to her navigation officer.

"If the second report isn't here in four, we take the outer route. If the tug loses availability first, we turn now."

Prite looked at Darcelle.

Darcelle was watching the clock.

At three minutes the second report arrived. It contradicted the first, but its source was less reliable. The cadet had to choose whether to believe the warning or the reassurance.

She took the outer route.

The convoy reached the receiving harbor late. Its water processors would miss the first repair shift. A second enemy contact struck the escort on the longer approach and damaged its weapon mount. The cadet did not win cleanly.

But she had preserved the tug, kept the hospital convoy together, and made her choice while the alternatives still existed.

At the debrief she was angry about the damaged escort.

"Would you do it again?" Renault asked.

"With what I knew then? Yes."

"Would you wait four minutes again?"

"Maybe three. The tug's estimate was optimistic."

"Why?"

"Because the drive temperature was rising. I should have asked for the current reading, not the rated endurance."

Darcelle made a note.

Prite asked what she thought of the mine warning.

"I still don't know whether it was real, sir."

"Good."

She looked surprised.

"You aren't required to know things the exercise never told you."

The cadet considered that. Then she looked at the casualty estimate again.

"Can I see the escort's engineering log?"

Darcelle brought it up.

They spent another twenty minutes examining the damage. No one awarded her a victory speech. She left with a problem she understood more clearly and a ship she had not entirely saved.

That was an improvement.

---

The Headmaster visited their workroom after the trial.

Vane arrived first, inspected the door, and stood where he could see both corridor approaches. There had been no new duel petitions that day. Prite was learning not to mistake the absence of news for the end of danger.

The Headmaster placed the trial results on the table.

"The curriculum office approves the new timing display."

Darcelle exhaled.

"With conditions," the Headmaster added.

Of course.

"The trial must run through the remainder of the term. Instructors will be required to identify the opportunity cost of delay using evidence available during the exercise. No fixed speed bonus. No retrospective deadline. Existing appeal rights remain."

Prite nodded.

"That's sensible."

The Headmaster studied him.

"I am pleased to hear you say so. It happens infrequently."

Renault took the papers.

"What about the cadets already examined?"

"No changes to their marks. Their records will be retained for the pilot evaluation."

"Good," Darcelle said.

The Headmaster looked at him.

"Your timing work made the difference."

Darcelle glanced at the others, uncomfortable with the direct praise.

"The clocks were already there."

"Someone had to notice what the Academy was doing with them."

For a moment Darcelle looked down at his hands. Prite remembered him in the garden, before the Headmaster revealed the truth about the Three Admirals, frightened that every new discovery would bring a firing squad. He was still frightened of some things. He had not stopped being useful while frightened.

Vane's vox clicked softly. He listened, gave a quiet acknowledgment and returned his attention to the corridor.

The Headmaster rose.

"One other matter. The fleet instruction office wants to know whether the pilot is producing better officers."

Prite looked at the assessment stack.

"We don't know yet."

"Precisely. Please keep saying that."

After they left, Renault found the draft syllabus beneath the day's reports.

"Third lesson?"

Prite looked at the blank space.

The first lesson had challenged doctrine. The second had forced cadets to count the cost of victory. He could have written another maxim. The Academy would have printed it on a wall, and somebody would eventually use it to justify a decision the maxim was never meant to cover.

He wrote a question instead.

**What changes while you wait?**

Darcelle read it.

"That isn't an answer."

"No."

Renault leaned over the table.

"Curriculum will hate it."

Prite left the question where it was.

Beyond the workroom, the Academy's simulation halls continued running. Cadets were making decisions, delaying them, changing them, regretting them. Some would lose ships for good reasons. Some would save ships by luck. The records would not tell the professors everything.

They would, at least, stop pretending that time had stood still while an officer decided what to do.
