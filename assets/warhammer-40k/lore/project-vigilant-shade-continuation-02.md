# Project VIGILANT SHADE

## Installment VIII — The Cost of the Perimeter

By morning the southern evacuation road had become a narrow river of people, freight, and military vehicles, all of them moving at different speeds and none of them capable of stopping without trapping the rest.

General Snadler Helgren studied the traffic plot from Highmount. The first company had reached the estate. The second had reached the outer district and was being fed forward in sections. Its ammunition carriers were still behind a column of civilian haulers. The third company had not been called from its transport-security assignments; removing those men would leave three other districts without guards.

His operations major set down the first casualty return.

“Eleven confirmed military and Arbites dead. Nineteen wounded received by the southern collection point. Missing personnel not yet reconciled.”

“Those are not final figures.”

“No, sir.”

“Then the heading must say so.”

The major changed it. Helgren watched him do it. He had seen reports acquire the authority of a final count merely because a clerk had copied a provisional number into a cleaner document.

The eastern depot requested another fuel release. The evacuation officer wanted all available carriers for civilians. The engineers needed one heavy hauler to move demolition equipment, and the medicae superintendent wanted the same vehicle for the wounded. Every request was defensible. The same machine could not serve three masters at once.

“Release the fuel for two carriers,” Helgren said. “One to the engineers, one to medical evacuation. The infantry will make do with the transport they have. Put the third-company call on conditional readiness, not movement.”

“The prefect will object to military fuel going to the hospital.”

“The hospital is receiving our wounded and his citizens.”

A fresh image arrived from the estate. The northern warehouse still stood, but the culvert demolition had broken the old drainage wall. Water was spreading into the lower stores. On the far side of the flooded apron, something large moved between the freight sheds.

Helgren enlarged the image. The shape vanished behind a roof.

“Tell Trellis I want a line he can hold,” he said, “not a line he can draw.”

---

Captain Irvine Trellis had drawn three lines anyway.

The first marked the warehouse windows and their fields of fire. The second followed the eastern freight lane, where the newly arrived soldiers could manoeuvre. The third, in a different colour, was the southern road. That line was not a defence position. It was the route by which everyone else might survive.

The engineer sergeant laid a drainage plan across the table. The drawing was twenty-six years old. Two culverts had been added since its last revision, according to the maintenance officer, but only one had been entered on the estate copy.

“The western branch empties beneath the pump house,” the sergeant said. “If we seal it, the water backs into the freight basement.”

“If we leave it open?”

“Something small enough can come through. Or the big ones can widen it.”

Yarrick leaned over the plan. His red eye followed the service channels to the pump-house foundations. He had seen Orks turn scrap and confined spaces into advantages before. He could not know what lay beyond this particular wall.

“Watch the outlet. Do not destroy the pump until you know what it keeps dry.”

The maintenance officer looked relieved. “The lower residential quarter takes its drainage through that station. If the pumps stop, we will flood occupied cellars.”

Trellis pointed to the second line. “One squad on the pump house. A reserve behind the eastern junction. No charge without the engineer sergeant’s clearance. Can you keep a service path open?”

“For now,” the sergeant said. “But I need two men and a cutting set. The shutter blast broke the inspection rail.”

Trellis assigned the men from his own limited reserve. The company commander objected, quietly and reasonably. He had fewer rifles than the map suggested and no guarantee of ammunition beyond the next delivery.

“I know,” Trellis said. “I am buying us the ability to keep the road open.”

They agreed on a timed withdrawal point if the pump house became untenable. The order went into the military log with the engineer’s objection and the maintenance officer’s warning. No one would later be permitted to call it a cost-free choice.

The first test came before the ink had dried. A lookout reported movement at the western outlet: two small Orks, a third carrying a reel of cable, and a larger figure too broad to pass beneath the arch. The soldiers fired on the first two. The third disappeared into the channel with the reel.

The engineer sergeant asked permission to seal the opening.

“Not yet,” Trellis said. “We still have men inspecting the pump-room side.”

The sergeant swore, then raised the inspection team himself. They were already retreating.

---

Waldorf gave his statement while someone else was eating breakfast beside the casualty tent.

The Highmount recorder wanted a clean sequence: the impossible claimant, the first Ork, the tunnel, the military response. Waldorf supplied what he had seen and separated it from what he had been told. The recorder asked twice whether he believed the man was Sebastian Yarrick.

“I believe he killed an Ork in front of me,” Waldorf said. “I believe the codes passed the checks I was told about. I did not perform those checks.”

“Do you doubt the identification?”

“I am not qualified to certify it.”

The recorder stopped pressing. Waldorf could hear artillery somewhere beyond the estate, too far away to distinguish practice from a separate emergency. He was grateful that the recorder did not ask him to identify that sound as well.

Henshaw brought the duplicate evidence ledger. His forearm was freshly dressed and his handwriting, when he tried to add a notation, had become nearly illegible.

“Leave it,” Waldorf told him. “You can read the entries back to me.”

The shield from the pipe casualty had been transferred to a numbered salvage enclosure. The casualty clerk had signed for it, but the pipe itself had not been recovered. A soldier thought it had gone into the northern warehouse. Another remembered it beside a loader. Neither had seen it placed in custody. Waldorf recorded both recollections without choosing between them.

Carver arrived with the south-gate permit book. A dozen pages had been soaked through, but the supervisor’s emergency authorization remained legible. Beside it Carver had kept a separate tally of the vehicles he waved through after the gate opened.

“Two machine-shop buses are still missing from the muster return,” Carver said.

“Missing from the road or missing from the paperwork?”

“We don't know.”

Waldorf put that sentence in the ledger. Then he compared Carver’s gate tally with the first military evacuation return. The figures did not match. One count included empty vehicles sent back for another load; the other counted only vehicles carrying people.

He marked the distinction rather than subtracting one number from the other.

A field clerk delivered a sealed canvas pouch containing metal fragments collected from the pump-house approach. The clerk had a location, collection time, and two witnesses. One fragment was a clamp with a flattened maker’s panel and a stamped pair of alignment dots. Another was a length of cable termination fitted with an unusually clean crimp.

“Ork scrap?” Henshaw asked.

“Probably,” Waldorf said. “But the word ‘probably’ is not a manufacturer.”

The fragments could have been stolen from ordinary machinery. They could have come from a warehouse, a salvage yard, or the industrial complex beneath the estate. They did not tell him which. He sealed the pouch as a battlefield collection, not as evidence recovered from the original tunnel.

That distinction cost him another line of handwriting. It might someday be worth considerably more.

---

The Orks came through the pump house just after the next ammunition delivery.

They had not found a secret passage. They had found an inspection hatch whose fasteners had been neglected for years. The smaller creatures broke the weakened hinge and dragged the cable reel inside. Behind them came an Ork with a welding visor tied across its face and a crude cutting torch that spat bright green sparks against the pipework.

The first soldier to see it shouted a warning. The second fired through the open hatch. The torch struck the floor, burning a line across a hose. Water sprayed into the electrical cabinet.

The pumps stopped.

For a moment the station was almost quiet. Then the emergency alarm began sounding through the residential quarter, and a maintenance worker on the upper platform fell while trying to reach the isolation switch.

Trellis received three reports at once: the pump house was compromised, the lower cellars were flooding, and the eastern reserve had been reduced by the men assigned to keep the service path open.

Yarrick did not demand command. He studied the layout while Trellis listened to the engineer sergeant.

“Can you isolate the damaged cabinet?”

“If the upper switch is still intact. Someone has to reach it.”

“Can you do it from the roof?”

“Not with the ladder we have.”

The company commander offered a fire team to clear the lower room. Yarrick shook his head.

“They will be shooting into a room full of pipes they need afterward. Take the upper platform. Cut off the torch bearer and make it come to you.”

The commander looked to Trellis. Trellis gave the order. A pair of soldiers crossed from the adjacent maintenance roof under covering fire, carrying a portable ladder and a coil of safety line. The first made the platform. The second was hit in the thigh as he climbed and nearly pulled both men down.

They got the line around a roof anchor. The maintenance worker, conscious but unable to stand, was hauled away from the switch. The first soldier reached the isolator and pulled it.

The alarm changed pitch. One bank of pumps remained dead; another began turning on the backup supply. Water continued to rise in the lower quarter, but more slowly.

Inside the pump house, the Ork with the visor pushed through a service door and met the fire team at the landing. It was larger than the hatch had suggested, its cutting torch dragging sparks across the floor. The soldiers killed it with concentrated fire at close range. One of them lost two fingers when the torch tank burst against the wall.

No one celebrated the kill. The maintenance officer was already calculating how long the surviving pumps could run before their bearings overheated.

Yarrick watched the injured soldier being carried out. The man was young, furious, and apologizing for dropping his weapon.

“Keep your hand above your heart,” Yarrick told him. “The medics will take the rest.”

The soldier stared at the greatcoat and the claw. “Commissar?”

“Do as you're told.”

The man obeyed. Trellis heard the exchange and said nothing about identity. He sent the casualty number to Highmount and ordered the damaged pump bank marked unavailable.

---

At the southern road, Carver found the first missing machine-shop bus behind a stalled grain hauler.

It had not vanished. Its driver had turned back to collect workers from the late shift, then become trapped when a carrier tried to reverse through the junction. The passengers had spent forty minutes watching soldiers and civilians argue over which vehicle could move first.

Carver walked the line, found the carrier’s crew chief, and persuaded him to bring his vehicle forward by a few metres. It was enough to free the bus. The crew chief demanded the name of whoever had ordered civilian traffic into a military lane.

Carver gave him the gate supervisor’s signed authorization. He did not pretend it was an elegant answer.

The second bus was still unaccounted for. The machine-shop foreman supplied a list of the people expected aboard, but admitted that the shift roster had been altered after the evacuation order. Two workers might have left on foot. Three others had been reassigned to the drainage pumps and had not signed out.

Waldorf sent Henshaw to the southern collection point with the names. He did not send him into the flooded quarter with a damaged arm.

The medicae superintendent accepted the list and returned an unwelcome fact: the collection point had only enough dressings for the present wounded if no further casualties arrived. A promised supply vehicle was still on the outer road.

A woman waiting beside the casualty tent asked Waldorf whether her husband was among the missing. He had no answer. He took the man’s name and the shop where he worked. She did not thank him, and he did not expect her to.

The first bus left with its passengers counted. The second remained missing.

At the pump house, the maintenance officer confirmed that three workers were still inside the quarter, sheltering above the flood line. Trellis approved a rescue by the same service route the engineers had kept open. The men who went in were tired, and one had to surrender his dry ammunition to a replacement before leaving. They returned with all three workers, soaked and shivering, while the lower streets filled another hand’s breadth.

The rescue was real. So was the cost of the pump bank that would not start again.

---

By late afternoon Highmount had a perimeter map that matched more of the ground than the first one had.

The north warehouse was still contested. The eastern lane remained held by the first company and elements of the second. The pump-house roof was secure, but the lower machinery room was unusable until the engineers could inspect it. The southern road remained open in one direction under emergency authority. Two drainage crossings were watched; a third had been found beneath a workshop whose owners insisted it had been sealed years ago.

Helgren ordered the third company held at readiness while its transport-security relief was assembled. He would not empty the outer districts to make the estate look secure on a staff chart.

The casualty return was worse than the morning’s. It distinguished confirmed dead, medically received wounded, and unlocated personnel. The categories were still incomplete. Helgren signed the report as provisional and sent a separate request for additional field dressings and fuel.

A junior officer pointed to the recovered pump-house fragments on the incident list.

“Factory fittings, sir. That suggests organized supply.”

“It suggests fittings,” Helgren said. “Find the factory before you write the rest.”

---

At the estate, Waldorf laid the sealed pouch beside the copied witness statement. The alignment dots on the clamp interested him less than the recorded place where it had been found. There were other industrial yards on New Presidio. If the same fittings appeared in legitimate stores, their maker’s marks might be ordinary. If they did not, the absence would become another question.

He requested a comparison against the estate’s maintenance and freight inventories, through the Arbites evidence office. He did not ask the estate to open its private records to him on the strength of a battlefield guess.

Trellis brought the evening report to Yarrick. The old Commissar read it without sitting down.

“Their attacks have thinned,” Trellis said.

“They are not gone.”

“No.”

Yarrick looked toward the northern roofs. He had wanted an army. What he had received was a company, part of another, exhausted police, a handful of engineers and civilians who still had to be carried past the danger. They had held a line. They had not contained the source.

Trellis waited while he finished the report.

“You have given us useful advice,” the captain said. “Highmount will record it as such.”

“And my name?”

“Still under review.”

Yarrick handed the slate back. He disliked the answer. He also understood the officer who had given it. He had demanded that men act on evidence rather than legend. He could not reasonably demand that they make a legend their evidence.

“Then keep the wounded moving,” he said. “And find out where the smaller ones are going.”

Outside, a freight hauler crossed the southern gate with its lamps covered against the rain. Behind it another vehicle waited, and behind that another. The evacuation continued through a city whose official maps had only begun to admit where the fighting was.

Below the estate, the dark service channels had not fallen silent.
