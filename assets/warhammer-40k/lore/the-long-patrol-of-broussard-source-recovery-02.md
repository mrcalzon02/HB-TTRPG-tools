# The Long Patrol of Broussard

## Source Recovery 02 — II. ORDERS FROM THE SAINT MALDERSON

*Verbatim recovery from the surviving Google working draft, reconciled 2026-09-20. This file preserves source prose rather than reconstructing it. Recovery continues sequentially from the same document before any new Broussard continuation is permitted.*

It had been like this for days.

Quiet.

Not peaceful. Broussard had served long enough to know there was a difference.

Peace was something politicians announced after battles had merely moved elsewhere. Quiet was what happened when the universe appeared to be considering its options.

They patrolled through blowing snow and over endless ice, crawling along beside the great commercial tankers while the horizon remained stubbornly empty. Their most serious engagements had involved blasting shifted ice formations away from routes where heavily burdened freighters could not quite make clearance, or persuading local wildlife that sheltering beneath a multi-thousand-tonne gravitic tanker was an extremely temporary lifestyle choice.

It should have been comfortable duty.

That was precisely what bothered him.

Somewhere in Broussard's long service, experience had transformed ordinary caution into a permanent low-grade paranoia. It was not cowardice. Cowardice was being frightened of something that might happen.

Experience was knowing that it eventually would.

There was, he remembered, an ancient Terran principle concerning such matters.

Murphy's Law.

Everything that could go wrong would go wrong.

Broussard considered this unnecessarily optimistic.

In the Imperial Guard, things that could not reasonably go wrong frequently did so anyway, usually because someone had forgotten to submit the correct requisition form.

He had seen the principle demonstrated at almost every muster he had ever attended.

There was always one man who had misplaced his socks.

One whose boots had mysteriously vanished.

One whose rifle power pack had ceased to exist precisely when the regiment was forming for inspection.

He particularly remembered a trooper named Hessel who had once lost the charging pack for his lascarbine immediately before parade.

Hessel had solved the problem by inserting an empty lho-stick carton into the weapon's magazine well.

From several meters away, if one did not look very closely, the silhouette was convincing enough.

It had even remained in place during parade rest.

Marching had been more complicated.

Shouldering arms had become an act of theological faith.

Hessel had survived the inspection by maintaining the rigid expression of a man who knew that one incorrect movement would cause a brightly colored tobacco packet to fall from his rifle directly in front of the regimental commissar.

Broussard had never been entirely certain whether that incident proved the Emperor protected fools or merely had a sense of humor.

Either explanation was uncomfortable.

So when the end-of-shift vox check arrived, Broussard was already expecting something unpleasant.

The message began normally enough.

Convoy position.

Fuel status.

Vehicle readiness.

Temperature warnings.

Estimated movement for the evening cycle.

Routine logistical chatter passed across the command frequency while his relief crew began preparing to take over.

Then another voice entered the channel.

Lieutenant-Major Ruschin.

Broussard immediately disliked this.

Senior officers did not personally contact junior convoy commanders to congratulate them on uneventful patrols.

They contacted them because something had happened.

Or was about to happen.

Or, worst of all, because someone had developed an idea.

The vox crackled.

"Captain Broussard. By order of convoy command, you are to report immediately to the command vessel Saint Malderson. Present yourself directly to the convoy commander."

Broussard stared at the vox unit.

There were sentences capable of making a man's stomach drop.

Your wife would like a word.

The commissar has requested your presence.

The Mechanicus says the reactor noise is probably normal.

"Report directly to the convoy commander" ranked comfortably among them.

He keyed the transmitter.

"Orders acknowledged."

The response came immediately.

"Transfer your patrol authority to the incoming shift. Transportation clearance has already been issued. Command expects you aboard within the hour."

Of course they had already issued clearance.

Someone had been planning this.

That made it worse.

Broussard handed over responsibility for the patrol with appropriate ceremony, which in operational conditions meant speaking approximately forty words, signing three electronic forms, and confirming twice that everyone understood who was responsible if something enormous came through the ice.

Then he ordered his Chimera toward the head of the convoy.

The Saint Malderson waited there.

Calling it a ship was technically correct in much the same way that calling a hive city a building was technically correct.

Officially, the Saint Malderson was a Bastion-class command skimmer, part of the Imperium's wet-navy answer to the peculiar environmental circumstances of High Presidio.

Practically speaking, it was what happened when an Imperial engineer looked at an ice freighter and asked:

What if it were also a fortress?

The original hull had once been a massive commercial transport.

Then someone had armored it.

Then someone had armed it.

Then someone had decided it needed command facilities.

Then maintenance bays.

Then vehicle storage.

Then aviation support.

Then more armor because all of those things were expensive.

By the time the redesign was complete, very little remained of the original freighter except the basic shape and perhaps several embarrassed structural members buried deep within the hull.

The Saint Malderson was designed to travel at the head of major winter convoys.

It could carry Chimeras.

It could carry tanks.

It could transport engineering vehicles, recovery crawlers, fuelers, ammunition carriers, mobile kitchens, medical sections, and whatever other assorted mechanical organisms the Imperial Guard required to move several thousand people across a frozen ocean without immediately starving, freezing, breaking down, or shooting one another.

Its lower hull contained cavernous vehicle decks large enough to swallow entire mechanized companies.

Those decks became storage and refit facilities during the off-season.

They also functioned as emergency workshops.

Machine shops.

Parts depots.

Fuel stations.

And temporary homes for every broken vehicle unfortunate enough to suffer mechanical failure several hundred kilometers from land.

If a Chimera threw a track, the Saint Malderson could repair it.

If a tank lost an engine, the Saint Malderson could replace it.

If an infantry company ran out of ammunition, the Saint Malderson could resupply it.

If an officer misplaced the correct form authorizing ammunition distribution, the Saint Malderson could ensure the company remained completely defenseless until the paperwork was located.

There were traditions to maintain.

Above the vehicle decks rose workshops, warehouses, fuel reservoirs, crew quarters, officers' barracks, communications centers, tactical rooms, medical stations, kitchens, chapels, administrative offices, navigation suites, and enough bureaucratic infrastructure to sustain an organization responsible for coordinating more than a hundred mechanized escort elements spread across kilometers of moving convoy.

Its upper decks supported aircraft operations.

Small atmospheric flyers and utility craft could land upon reinforced pads along the superstructure, while enclosed hangars protected machines from the brutal winter storms.

Gun emplacements studded the hull.

Autocannons.

Heavy bolters.

Missile mounts.

Naval las-batteries adapted for surface engagement.

Several weapons were intended for hostile vehicles.

Most were intended for wildlife.

A disturbing number were intended for wildlife large enough that Broussard preferred not to imagine the engineering calculations involved.

The entire vessel was effectively a moving Imperial settlement.

A fortress.

A supply depot.

A repair yard.

An airfield.

A naval command center.

A barracks.

A fuel station.

A magazine.

And, because this was the Imperium, somewhere aboard there was almost certainly a chapel larger than necessary.

The vessel moved with deceptive gentleness across the ice.

Massive gravitic projectors carried most of its weight, allowing the hull to coast just above the frozen surface while enormous stabilizers kept the structure level.

From a distance it seemed almost serene.

Up close, the scale became oppressive.

The rear of the vessel opened into gigantic boarding apertures.

Multiple reinforced loading ramps extended from the stern, broad enough for Chimeras and armored carriers to drive directly into the maintenance decks without the ship coming to a complete stop.

Convoy vehicles could approach from behind, match speed, climb the ramps, refuel, repair, resupply, and emerge again.

It was an enormous mechanical mouth perpetually swallowing Imperial machinery and vomiting it back into the snow slightly less broken than before.

But the stern was not what drew Broussard's attention.

The prow did.

The Saint Malderson possessed a bow that would not have looked entirely inappropriate mounted upon a Dauntless-class light cruiser.

It rose above the ice in a massive armored wedge, reinforced by kilometers of internal ribbing and faced with layers of ceramite and hardened plasteel.

Integrated heating grids ran beneath the outer plating.

The purpose was not to break through the frozen ocean.

That would have been catastrophically stupid.

The ice beneath them might be dozens of meters thick in places, but beneath that lay kilometers of extremely cold water containing creatures with a demonstrated interest in eating ships.

Nobody wanted to create additional openings.

Instead, the prow acted as a gigantic plow.

Winter winds and ocean movement could pile broken ice into ridges many meters high. Pressure formations accumulated across trade routes, creating jagged barricades capable of damaging gravitic vessels passing too close above them.

The Saint Malderson simply moved them.

The heated prow weakened frozen formations while the armored wedge shouldered them aside through brute force.

Ice cliffs shattered.

Pressure ridges collapsed.

Massive slabs weighing hundreds of tonnes rolled away from the vessel as it advanced.

Behind it remained a rough but navigable corridor across which the convoy could pass.

It was not elegant engineering.

Imperial engineering rarely aspired to elegance when sufficient mass could accomplish the same objective.

Broussard watched the vessel grow steadily larger through the Chimera's forward vision block.

The command ship towered above everything around it.

Searchlights swept across the ice.

Escort vehicles moved around the hull like insects around some migrating metal beast.

Steam drifted from the heated prow where accumulated snow struck hot plating and vanished.

Aircraft navigation lights blinked high overhead.

Broussard's driver whistled softly.

"Big bastard."

Broussard folded his arms.

"Technical designation."

"Sir?"

"That is the official naval classification. Bastion-class Big Bastard."

The driver glanced back.

"Thought Bastion-class was the classification."

"That's what they tell civilians."

They approached the stern.

One of the boarding ramps lowered toward them, its armored surface descending until it hovered just above the ice.

Guidance lights illuminated the route.

A deck controller appeared over the vox.

"Escort Three-Seven, maintain fourteen kilometers per hour. Align center ramp. Do not brake during transition."

Broussard looked toward his driver.

"Do not brake."

"I heard him, sir."

"I'm reinforcing the instruction."

"Comforting."

The Chimera climbed.

For several seconds the vehicle crossed the gap between open ice and shipboard deck while the Saint Malderson continued moving beneath them.

Then the tracks struck metal.

The sound changed instantly.

Snow hiss became industrial thunder.

The Chimera rolled into the vessel's lower maintenance bay and entered an entirely different world.

Thousands of lumen strips illuminated cavernous spaces filled with machinery.

Crane systems moved overhead.

Servitors dragged ammunition pallets.

Enginseers clustered around damaged vehicles.

Mechanics shouted beneath the screaming whine of tools.

Fuel lines descended from ceiling gantries.

Armored vehicles sat in repair cradles with their engine compartments torn open like carcasses upon butcher's tables.

The air smelled of promethium, lubricants, hot metal, incense, sweat, and the faint electrical bitterness of abused machinery.

Someone waved the Chimera toward an assigned holding position.

Broussard climbed out.

A naval rating was already waiting for him.

"Captain Broussard?"

"Yes."

"Command deck. Follow me."

There was no explanation.

Naturally.

Explanations were for people who had enough rank to demand them.

They crossed the maintenance deck and entered one of the vessel's internal transit lifts.

The doors closed.

The lift began climbing.

Broussard watched deck numbers flash past.

Vehicle deck.

Supply deck.

Engineering.

Crew habitation.

Operations support.

Officer country.

Command.

Too high.

He frowned.

The rating remained perfectly expressionless.

Broussard considered asking what this was about.

Then decided against it.

If the rating knew, he probably had orders not to say.

If he did not know, asking would accomplish nothing.

And if he knew and had not been told not to say, then whatever was happening had already become far more interesting than Broussard wanted.

The lift stopped.

The doors opened onto a wide corridor lined with naval personnel, convoy officers, communications staff, and armed sentries.

This was not ordinary command traffic.

Too many people were moving with purpose.

Too many voices were low.

Too many doors were guarded.

Broussard's unease hardened.

Something had happened.

He followed the rating toward the forward command section.

At the end of the corridor stood two storm-troopers in full armor beside an armored hatch.

One checked Broussard's identification.

The other checked it again.

Then they opened the door.

"Captain Broussard reporting as ordered," he said.

Inside waited the convoy command staff.

Maps covered the walls.

Tactical displays glowed across the central strategium table.

Several officers stood around it.

And at the far end of the room waited the convoy commander.

Broussard stepped inside.

The hatch sealed behind him.

There was a particular sound made by heavy armored doors closing.

Broussard had always found it considerably more ominous when one was standing on the wrong side of them.

The convoy commander looked up.

"Captain Broussard."

"Sir."

"We have a problem."

There it was.

Murphy had arrived.
