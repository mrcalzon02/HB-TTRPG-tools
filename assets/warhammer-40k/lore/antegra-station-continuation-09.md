# Antegra Station

## Continuation IX — The Recognition

*An original Cafarron Corridor chronicle continuation. Warhammer 40,000 and associated marks and setting elements are the property of Games Workshop. This non-commercial fan work is not endorsed by or affiliated with Games Workshop.*

For eleven seconds, nobody on Antegra Station said anything.

This was unusual enough to qualify as an emergency in its own right.

The unknown contact remained on the tactical display as a wound in the mathematics. Every augur returned information. None agreed on what information had been returned.

Captain Ardan Krell stood over the plot with both hands braced against the brass rim.

"Range?"

The augur lieutenant swallowed.

"Four hundred thousand kilometres."

A second servitor clicked.

"Nine million."

A third began reciting a distance in negative numbers.

Krell looked at the lieutenant.

"Pick one."

"Sir?"

"We are a military organization. We require a wrong answer promptly."

"Four hundred thousand kilometres."

"Excellent. Mark it provisional."

Senior Auditor Selene Marr glanced up from the coherence display.

"Everything is provisional now."

"That should make you feel at home."

Marr ignored him.

Talbor Varik watched the thing that had arrived without translating through any lawful boundary of the system.

He had no office.

No title.

No legal authority.

According to the records presently saving eight million people from becoming the nervous system of a dead civilization, he had never governed Antegra at all.

The station nevertheless waited for him to speak.

Imperial institutions possessed many strengths.

Consistency was not among them.

"What is it doing?" he asked.

Krell enlarged the tactical return.

"Nothing."

Legate-Inquisitor Aveline Rhyse said, "Nothing is not reassuring."

"It is when compared with shooting."

"Captain."

"Legate."

The contact changed.

Not position.

Not velocity.

Its relationship to the station changed.

Every auspex icon representing Antegra vanished.

Then reappeared on the opposite side of the contact.

Krell's face hardened.

"Did we move?"

"No, sir."

"Did it move?"

"No, sir."

"Then what moved?"

The augur lieutenant stared at the instruments.

"The coordinate system."

Marr stopped typing.

"That is discourteous."

Rhyse looked toward Witness Lysa Nineteen.

The blindfolded child had gone completely still.

"Lysa."

No answer.

Rhyse knelt beside her.

"What do you see?"

"It doesn't see."

"The contact?"

Lysa shook her head.

"It recognizes."

Varik felt the seven moons behind his eyes.

They had dimmed when the thing arrived.

Now one disappeared.

Not destroyed.

Forgotten.

The memory of it simply ceased to exist.

Varik staggered.

Krell caught his arm.

"Talbor?"

Varik stared at him.

The use of his given name frightened him more than the contact.

"One of the moons is gone."

Rhyse rose.

"From the vision?"

"From the memory."

Marr checked the coherence map.

Millions of citizen threads were still frozen.

Then a whole district vanished from the mnemonic visualization.

Habitation Crown Four.

Three hundred and eleven thousand citizens.

Marr went pale.

"They are still physically present."

Krell checked the station census.

"Are they?"

The official population count had dropped by three hundred and eleven thousand.

The corrective error had already made the census unreliable.

This was different.

The records did not contradict one another.

They agreed perfectly.

Crown Four had never existed.

Varik looked toward Marr.

"Put it back."

"I can't."

"You invented seven departments before breakfast."

"Inventing things is easier than proving something existed after reality has filed an objection."

Solvik drew his bolt pistol.

No one commented.

There were circumstances in which a firearm was merely emotional support.

---

The first message arrived at 06:31.

It did not use vox.

It appeared in the station's oldest tithe ledger.

Marr had the book open because she was still dismantling Varik's gubernatorial lineage when letters began surfacing through two centuries of ink.

Not new ink.

Old ink.

Words that had apparently always been there.

She read them once.

Then again.

Varik leaned over her shoulder.

WHERE IS THE REST OF YOU?

Krell frowned.

"That seems almost conversational."

Rhyse said, "Do not answer it."

A second line appeared.

YOU WERE LARGER BEFORE.

Lysa whimpered.

The city-memory recoiled.

Another moon disappeared.

Varik understood.

"It isn't talking to us."

Rhyse looked at him.

"No."

"It is talking to the city."

"Yes."

The next words formed slowly.

WHY DID YOU CUT YOURSELF INTO ANIMALS?

Nobody in Central Audit moved.

Krell finally said, "I object to the noun."

Marr looked at him.

"On theological grounds?"

"Professional ones."

Solvik said, "It may be accurate."

Krell turned.

"I preferred you before this morning."

"You did not."

"That is true."

The ledger page blackened.

Not burned.

Every written mark simply thickened until the parchment became a rectangle of ink.

Then the page tore itself free and folded inward without being touched.

Rhyse seized it with a silver forceps.

The parchment collapsed into a point.

Then vanished.

Marr stared at the empty binding.

"That volume is part of the permanent tithe record."

Rhyse looked at her.

"Senior Auditor, something has entered the system by altering the local meaning of distance and is currently communicating through retroactive ink."

"Yes."

"And you are concerned about the ledger?"

"It was indexed."

Varik almost laughed.

Almost.

Then Crown Four called Central Audit.

---

The vox operator was crying.

"Central, this is Crown Four Administration. We have a records failure."

Marr took the channel.

"Define failure."

"Our residents are disappearing from the registry."

"They remain physically present?"

"Yes."

"Names?"

"That is the problem."

The operator breathed hard.

"I know there are people standing in front of me. I know I work with them. I know one of them is my sister."

A pause.

"I cannot remember her name."

Marr closed her eyes.

Varik took the vox.

"Listen to me. Stop using the registry."

"Who is this?"

The question hurt more than it should have.

"Nobody important."

Krell looked at him.

Varik continued.

"Write names by hand. On walls if necessary. Give every person a witness. Two if you can. Speak names aloud. Tell stories. Bad ones are acceptable. Especially bad ones."

Rhyse understood first.

"Personal memory."

Varik nodded.

"The city survives by being remembered. Whatever this is attacks recognition."

Marr seized another vox channel.

"All departments. Suspend electronic identity reconciliation. Establish manual witness rolls. Every citizen is to identify two others from direct memory. No genealogical databases. No centralized concordance."

Krell said, "You are rebuilding the network."

"Small networks."

"The protocol was designed to fragment them."

"Then we fragment deliberately. Cells of three. No central index."

Varik looked at her.

"A station of eight million people held together by gossip."

Marr's mouth tightened.

"Humanity has used worse infrastructure."

Solvik holstered his pistol.

"Frequently."

Within minutes, Antegra began remembering itself by hand.

Dockworkers chalked names onto pressure doors.

Families wrote one another onto ration wrappers.

Naval ratings recited squad rosters without consulting slates.

Children were told the names of grandparents they had never met and instructed to repeat them.

Priests opened parish books and discovered half their congregations had disappeared from the pages, so they began ringing bells and shouting names from pulpits.

In Habitation Crown Four, a woman wrote her sister's name across both forearms.

The letters remained.

The official census continued to insist neither woman existed.

For once, the people trusted handwriting over government.

This represented progress.

---

At 06:52, the contact reacted.

The impossible return expanded across every tactical display.

Krell ordered the station to battle readiness.

Rhyse ordered him to hold fire.

Solvik asked whether those orders contradicted one another sufficiently to satisfy the emergency protocol.

Nobody answered him.

The contact produced no weapon signature.

Instead, Antegra lost another moon.

Then another.

Only three remained in Varik's memory.

He fell to one knee.

The white city inside him was changing.

Buildings were vanishing.

Streets ended in blankness.

Crowds of remembered figures ran through plazas that no longer connected to anything.

They were afraid.

Not of death.

Varik finally understood that much.

Death had happened to them already.

They were afraid of being made never to have existed.

Lysa screamed.

Rhyse caught her.

The child's blindfold had become wet with blood.

"It's asking," Lysa gasped.

"What?"

"Why they hid."

Varik looked toward the tactical display.

"Can it hear you?"

Lysa shook her head violently.

"It doesn't hear. It notices."

"Can we make it notice something else?"

Rhyse's eyes narrowed.

"Talbor."

"The city hid by becoming incomplete. We made ourselves incomplete to hide it again."

Marr understood.

"You want to give it a false whole."

"I want to give it something recognizable."

Krell stared at them.

"What does that mean in language used by people who have recently slept?"

Varik looked at the station records.

At the contradictory census.

At his erased governorship.

At two centuries of tithe fraud.

At eight million people presently preserving one another with chalk, paper and stubbornness.

Then he looked at Marr.

"Can you make Antegra look like one person?"

Marr did not answer immediately.

This was how Varik knew the request was unreasonable.

Finally she said, "Legally?"

"Does that matter?"

"Not anymore."

"Good."

Marr sat down.

Her fingers moved across the keys.

"Give me twenty minutes."

---

The fiction was magnificent.

Marr collapsed every remaining station account into a single legal identity.

Property ownership, labor obligations, ration entitlement, debt, medical liability, docking fees, burial rights, tax arrears and eleven active criminal indictments were reassigned to one person.

Talbor Varik.

Krell watched the numbers accumulate.

"You are making him personally liable for the station."

"Yes."

"All of it?"

"Yes."

"The dockyard owes the Mechanicus seventeen thousand labor-hours."

"He owes them now."

"There are six million outstanding ration chits."

"His."

"Habitation Ring Two has an unpaid ventilation levy from forty-three years ago."

"Also his."

Varik leaned over Marr's shoulder.

"I object."

"You are not governor. Your objection has no standing."

Krell turned away so nobody would see him smile.

The station census changed.

Population: 1.

Marr looked at the result.

"There."

Rhyse said, "This is absurd."

"Yes."

"It may also be heretical."

"Almost certainly."

"Continue."

The contact stopped erasing Crown Four.

Every auspex aboard Antegra went dark.

Varik felt the remaining three moons turn toward him.

The dead city did the same.

For one impossible instant, eight million living people and an extinct civilization were administratively represented by a single middle-aged man who had technically never held public office.

The Imperium had produced stranger paperwork.

Not much stranger.

The contact recognized him.

The tactical displays returned.

A shape appeared.

Not a ship.

A silhouette defined by missing stars.

Long.

Thin.

Jointed in places geometry did not require joints.

Around it moved thousands of smaller absences like attendants orbiting a corpse.

Lysa whispered one word.

"Collector."

Rhyse looked down at her.

"Is that its name?"

"No."

"What is it?"

"What it does."

The contact transmitted again.

This time the message appeared everywhere a human being had written Talbor Varik's name.

On walls.

On ration wrappers.

On forearms.

On prayer books.

Beside each handwritten name, a second line appeared in characters no one had written.

YOU ARE NOT THEM.

Varik stared at the words.

Then smiled.

Rhyse saw it.

"Why are you smiling?"

"Because it believes us."

The smile vanished.

A third line appeared.

THEN RETURN WHAT YOU STOLE.

The remaining three moons inside Varik's memory went dark.

Below Central Audit, Substructure XVIII opened every sealed door at once.

Something in the impossible corridor began moving upward.

Not toward the Collector.

Toward Talbor Varik.

And for the first time that morning, the dead city did not feel afraid.

It felt ashamed.
