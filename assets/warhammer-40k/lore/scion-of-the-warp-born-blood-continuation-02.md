# Scion of the Warp-Born Blood — The Corrected Line

**Based on the work of:** *Scion of the Warp-Born Blood*  
**Original story by:** Christopher Vardeman / Mrcalzon02  
**Continuation:** The Corrected Line

---

The physician had left three marks on Mulvane's examination slate. One measured the tremor in his left hand. One marked the time between a question and the movement of his eyes toward its answer. The third had been made after the physician covered the test lamp and asked whether the light had gone away.

Mulvane had said yes.

The physician had written something down anyway.

It was the second morning of his restriction. His left hand would hold a cup if supported from below, but not a stylus. The headache had become a pressure behind his eyes that he could almost ignore until he tried to read.

"The allocation office wants you listed as reserve," the physician said.

"The tithe carrier's passage was completed."

"There are other carriers."

"Am I fit?"

"You can stand and answer questions. You cannot open your eye for another inner passage. I have written both conclusions, since your House appears to require assistance distinguishing them."

Mulvane nearly smiled. It hurt.

A summons arrived before the physician had finished. House Drenal required him at an allocation review in the middle watch.

"Not on your feet," the physician said.

"I can attend seated."

"They can bring the hearing here."

Mulvane did not possess the authority to demand that. The physician did. He attached the restriction to the summons and sent it back.

---

The review came to the recovery chamber.

Two allocation officers brought the old and new manifests, the carrier's contract, a recorder, and the liaison who had instructed Mulvane to complete the contracted passage first. There were no ceremonial robes. A dispute over a ship's place in a queue required timestamps more urgently than ceremony.

The senior officer placed the superseded manifest before Mulvane.

"Did you know this document was no longer operative?"

"Yes."

"Did you obtain it without permission?"

"I asked the clerk to request permission. He returned with the sheet."

"Did you promise to protect him?"

"No."

The liaison said, "You delayed a contracted vessel on the strength of a possible hazard."

"I requested the lighter's current signals because its Navigator was injured and its captain had considered an unaided translation. A possible hazard is what an examination is for."

"Your assessment is disputed."

"Then examine it."

The senior officer called up the signal sequence. The hospital lighter's first request described the injured Navigator and its diminishing reserves. A later message proposed a desperate translation if no assistance came.

"The second message arrived after you delayed the carrier," the liaison observed.

"Yes. It confirmed the concern. It did not cause the initial examination."

Mulvane watched the senior officer note the distinction. House Drenal sold its Navigators' judgment as part of the contract. If the House declared that judgment irrelevant whenever it interfered with the queue, it would be making a promise its clients could eventually test.

"Did you order the lighter's captain to disobey her command?" the officer asked.

"I told her not to put an injured Navigator back in the chair. I requested figures and recommended holding position for a conventional rendezvous."

"She was free to refuse?"

"She was the shipmaster."

The liaison said, "She was desperate."

"Yes," Mulvane answered. "That does not make her a passenger."

The recorder marked the exchange. No one called it a victory.

---

The receiving station's invoice arrived during the hearing. The superintendent had certified filters, sterilization and portable cots, but rejected a contractor's second cleaning crew that had never arrived and a charge for opening a compartment already cleared two days before the lighter's approach.

The liaison studied the total.

"She is recovering the whole ward budget through one emergency."

"She has rejected the unsupported charges," Mulvane said.

"How would you know?"

"The rejection is signed."

The senior officer read it and crossed two items off the disputed total.

"Even the supported expense was not authorized by the House."

"I charged the conversion to my service account."

"That is not the same thing."

"No."

Mulvane knew that the balance could remain against him for years. House Drenal did not give him a purse from which to pay a hospital bill. It controlled his assignments, accommodation, medicine and the accounting of his usefulness. An expense assigned to him was another lever in hands that already held most of the others.

The liaison produced a notice from the tithe carrier's factor. The owners reserved a claim for spoiled grain and a missed unloading window.

"Has a loss been established?" Mulvane asked.

"The factor alleges one."

"The carrier's discharge acknowledgment recorded no spoilage."

"A later survey may."

"Then examine the survey."

The senior officer wrote again.

"A reserved claim is not a debt yet," he said. "Nor does its failure, if it fails, absolve you of the unauthorized expenditure."

"I understand."

Mulvane was not seeking absolution. He was trying to keep distinct things distinct, because the House was powerful enough to make a vague accusation endure longer than a precise one.

---

The preliminary mortality report had also been corrected.

The House liaison's earlier account grouped three patients as having died before transfer. The receiving station now recorded one death before final approach, one during preparation of the quarantine hold, and one pronounced after transfer. The names were withheld while the station established who was entitled to receive them.

"Does this tell us whether an earlier passage would have saved them?" Mulvane asked.

The physician, still present under his own authority, read the times.

"No."

"Does it prove the delay was harmless?"

"No."

The senior officer closed the report.

"This is not a mortality inquiry."

"Then do not use the three deaths as though their meaning has already been decided."

The officer considered that. "The sequence will be amended. Cause remains outside this review."

It was less than Mulvane wanted and more than he had expected. The dead had not become proof that he was right. They had not become proof that the House was right either. They were three people whose deaths remained real while the institutions around them argued over what could properly be inferred.

When the officers left, the physician tested Mulvane's hand again.

"Still not cleared," he said.

"I know."

"Knowing is not the same as obeying."

Mulvane looked at him. The physician returned the gaze without ceremony.

"I will obey."

---

The clerk with the bandaged thumb came to collect the table seals.

"Did they question you?" Mulvane asked.

"Yesterday, lord."

"About the old manifest?"

"About the authorization. I kept the request."

"Good."

The clerk stacked the slates in the recorder's order. He had been moved from the priority desk while the review proceeded. The new assignment carried the same ration allotment and fewer opportunities for advancement.

"They called it separation of duties," he said.

Mulvane knew the phrase. It could describe a genuine precaution and still impose a cost.

"I did not ask you to lose your place."

"No, lord."

"Would you have brought the sheet if you had known?"

The clerk stopped stacking. He considered the question long enough that Mulvane regretted asking it.

"I had a proper request," he said. "I would have brought it."

Mulvane did not ask him to say anything braver.

After the clerk left, the lighter's shipmaster sent a message to the allocation office with Mulvane copied. She wanted confirmation that the station's conversion bill would not be transferred to her vessel as a navigation charge. Her owners had received a provisional account. She had no authority to accept new debt on behalf of the patients.

Mulvane asked another clerk for the passage acknowledgment.

"Can she be charged under a contract?" he asked.

"There is no signed lighter contract, lord. But the station names House Drenal as guarantor. A guarantor may seek recovery."

"From the vessel?"

"From whoever accepted the service."

The acknowledgment identified Mulvane's service allocation as the authorization. He asked that a copy be sent to the shipmaster. The clerk checked the seal and confirmed it was released for ordinary circulation.

"That will not settle her bill," the clerk said.

"No."

"It may complicate the House review."

"It is still the correct record."

The message went out. It did not promise the shipmaster protection. It gave her a document with which to argue against a debt she had not agreed to bear.

---

At the next examination Mulvane could write three words without his left hand striking the table. The physician did not clear him. His visual response still lagged, and the afterimage of the test lamp remained when the cover fell.

The senior allocation officer visited alone afterward.

"The carrier's factor has submitted a survey," he said. "It was taken after discharge. We have requested the earlier temperature records. The review remains open."

Mulvane nodded.

"The station's supported expenses remain against your service account. The duplicate charges have been removed."

"And the clerk?"

"His reassignment is under review."

"His request was authorized."

"That is in the file."

The officer laid down another paper. The receiving station had proposed that future emergency allocation changes preserve an acknowledgment of the original priority and the authority responsible for altering it.

"Will the House agree?" Mulvane asked.

"We have not decided. There are legitimate reasons to restrict a change's details. Medical privacy. Client security. Hazards that cannot be disclosed before examination. A record intended to prevent one omission can create another delay."

Mulvane considered the answer. The officer was not merely defending a bad system. He knew which problems a replacement might create.

"Then perhaps the reason need not be public," Mulvane said. "Only that a reason exists, and who authorized the change."

"Perhaps. You are not being asked to draft the rule."

"No."

"Rest."

The officer departed with the file. Mulvane remained with the reduced invoice, the unresolved claim and the physician's restriction. The clerk had lost his desk. The shipmaster was defending her vessel. The station was caring for patients who needed more than a safe arrival. Three people were dead, and their corrected times had not explained why.

He had made a line difficult to erase. The line now ran through other people's work.

Outside his chamber another manifest was being prepared for a Navigator fit to open his eye. Mulvane was not that Navigator today.

For once, the fact had been entered correctly.
