# The Long Patrol of Broussard

## XI. THE THIRD MAN

**Author:** Mrcalzon02 / Christopher Vardeman  
**Based on the work of:** *The Long Patrol of Broussard*

*An original non-commercial Warhammer 40,000 fan chronicle. Warhammer 40,000 and associated marks and setting elements are the property of Games Workshop. This fan work is not endorsed by or affiliated with Games Workshop.*

The forward cargo spine was colder than the rest of the ship.

Not immediately.

At first it was merely less warm.

Then the air changed by degrees as the search party moved deeper into the damaged bow of the *Hesperant Vale*, past bulkheads where emergency lumen strips worked only in alternating sections and ventilation fans had been reduced to a tired mechanical mutter.

The freighter had been designed to move food.

A great deal of food.

That fact became less abstract with every hatch they passed.

The forward holds were not rooms in any ordinary sense. They were agricultural caverns divided by industrial bulkheads and internal baffles, enormous spaces intended to swallow hundreds of tonnes of produce at a time and later surrender it to conveyor systems at destination.

The air smelled accordingly.

Cold earth.

Onions.

Bruised fruit.

Vegetable rot where refrigeration had failed.

Metal.

Lubricant.

Coolant.

And beneath all of it, the old stale breath of a ship running too many emergency systems at once.

Broussard walked first.

Gabriel followed a pace behind him.

Sella Varn came next with a lamp clipped to one shoulder, a heavy tool bag hanging from the other, and the expression of a woman who had already decided that whatever they found would somehow become her maintenance problem.

Two Guardsmen brought up the rear.

Broussard had selected men who could follow instructions.

This excluded more people than he preferred to admit.

They left hard-line vox repeaters at each major junction exactly as planned.

Every turn was marked.

Every hatch was checked before passing through it.

Every time the deck changed angle, Sella stopped to look at the bulkheads before continuing.

Captain Kelm accompanied them as far as the cargo spine.

Broussard had meant what he said.

Kelm would come that far.

No farther.

The captain objected once.

Not loudly.

Not theatrically.

That was almost worse.

"They're my people."

"I know."

"I know these holds."

"Sella knows them too."

Kelm looked at her.

Sella did not appear eager to become part of an argument between two captains.

She solved the problem by examining a wall panel with tremendous interest.

Kelm looked back at Broussard.

"If they're hurt—"

"Then I need you somewhere the rescue team can bring them back to."

Kelm's jaw tightened.

Broussard lowered his voice.

"You're the captain of eighty-seven people."

"Eighty-four, if those three are—"

He stopped.

Nobody finished the sentence for him.

Broussard waited a moment.

Then said:

"Eighty-seven until I know otherwise."

Kelm looked at him for several seconds.

Finally he nodded.

"Fine."

He stayed at the cargo spine.

Broussard continued forward.

The first bin access corridor ran almost forty meters between two enormous produce compartments.

The scale was wrong for people.

Everything was too tall.

The walls rose above them in reinforced vertical plates interrupted by maintenance galleries and inspection ports. Somewhere behind the left-hand bulkhead lay several hundred tonnes of root vegetables. Behind the right, winter fruit and hard squash.

One did not often feel threatened by cabbage.

High Presidio was an educational place.

Sella pointed upward toward a row of pressure indicators.

"Bin Four is holding."

"Meaning?"

"Meaning it hasn't emptied itself into the corridor."

"Good."

"Probably."

Broussard looked at her.

Sella shrugged.

"Instrumentation took a beating."

"Of course it did."

They moved on.

The forward lighting grew worse.

At the next junction, one entire run of lumens was dead.

Sella produced a handheld work lamp and swept it over the walls.

No deformation.

No spilled cargo.

No broken hatch.

Nothing.

"Jerrik's team should have passed this way," she said.

Broussard crouched beside the deck.

Boot marks crossed through a layer of dust and powdered insulation.

Three sets.

Then more.

Old traffic.

Recent traffic.

He could not tell which was which.

Sella could.

She pointed.

"That tread."

Broussard followed the line with his eyes.

Three sets of prints moved forward.

None returned.

Gabriel noticed that at the same time.

He said nothing.

There was increasingly little need for the two men to announce the obvious to one another.

They moved deeper.

The next section had suffered more obvious electrical damage.

Several access panels had been removed or blown loose.

A conduit housing along the ceiling was scorched.

One junction box had been wrapped in an emergency insulating blanket and marked with a hand-painted warning sigil.

Sella paused beside it.

"That wasn't here before."

"Before the crash?"

"Before last shift."

Broussard looked at the blanket.

"Live?"

"Was."

"Now?"

She checked with a probe.

"Dead."

"Good."

"Don't get sentimental."

They continued.

The smell reached them before the bodies did.

Broussard stopped.

Sella stopped so abruptly behind Gabriel that the tool bag struck her hip.

The Guardsmen froze.

For several seconds nobody spoke.

The odor was faint.

Then the ventilation cycled.

A sluggish fan somewhere forward changed pitch.

The smell rolled back toward them.

Burned insulation.

Hot copper.

Ozone.

And something else.

Something greasy.

Sweet.

Wrong.

Broussard knew that smell.

Every soldier did eventually.

Some learned it from flamers.

Some from vehicle fires.

Some from artillery strikes where a body had been too close to the wrong piece of machinery when the world became electrical.

Burned flesh had a particular way of announcing itself.

Not loudly.

Not dramatically.

It simply entered the air and made every animal part of the human mind insist that one should not breathe too deeply.

Sella's face changed.

"Emperor."

Gabriel's expression flattened.

No smile now.

Broussard raised one hand.

"Nobody touches anything."

The two Guardsmen stopped exactly where they were.

Good men.

Sella whispered:

"Jerrik."

"Maybe."

She looked at him.

Broussard did not soften it.

"Maybe."

The smell was stronger ahead.

So was the ozone.

That mattered.

Burned bodies meant death.

Ozone meant electricity.

Electricity meant the thing that killed them might still be interested in repeating itself.

Broussard crouched beside the wall.

"Sella."

She was already pulling a diagnostic probe from her bag.

"Checking."

"No heroics."

"I've got a probe."

"People manage heroics with less."

She gave him a look.

Then extended the probe ahead of them.

The instrument chirped.

Once.

Then again.

Its warning rune flickered amber.

Sella stopped.

"Field leakage."

"How bad?"

"Bad enough."

"Useful."

"No."

"I meant the warning."

"Oh."

They advanced one pace at a time.

Broussard saw the first body at the bend.

A man lay facedown across the edge of an opened decking panel.

One arm extended into the shallow service recess below.

His work coat was blackened along the shoulder and side.

The fingers of his outstretched hand were burned dark.

Sella made a sound in her throat.

Not a word.

Broussard caught the back of her coat before she could move forward.

She turned on him sharply.

Then understood.

"Don't."

"That's Jerrik."

"I know."

"That's Jerrik."

"I heard you."

She stared at him.

Broussard kept his grip.

"You smell the ozone?"

Her face tightened.

"Yes."

"Then he is dead until we prove the corridor isn't."

That stopped her.

Not emotionally.

Practically.

Sometimes practical people could be saved by giving them a problem larger than grief.

Gabriel moved beside Broussard.

"There."

A second body was partially hidden beyond the first.

On its back.

One hand locked around the shoulder of the dead man in front of him.

The other curled toward the edge of the service trench.

Another meter farther on lay the third.

He had fallen sideways.

His boots were tangled beneath him.

One hand still clutched a length of fabric from the second man's coat.

Three bodies.

Close together.

Too close.

Broussard felt the shape of it before anyone explained.

One man went down.

Second man found him.

Reached.

Third found the second.

Reached.

A perfectly ordinary human disaster.

No enemy required.

No malice.

Only the ancient conviction that when another human being collapses, one should try to help.

The ship made a small clicking sound.

Sella's eyes widened.

"Back."

Broussard did not ask why.

"Back."

They moved.

Fast.

Not running.

Running killed people in damaged industrial spaces.

They retreated six meters around the bend.

Sella shoved the probe toward the deck.

The warning rune went from amber to red.

Then the corridor flashed.

White-blue light snapped across the passage.

The sound was not a bang.

It was a violent electrical crack, hard enough that Broussard felt it in his teeth.

A jagged arc jumped from the exposed service trench to a damaged cable deck along the opposite wall.

For less than a second the entire corridor became daylight.

Then darkness returned.

The smell of ozone became overwhelming.

One of the Guardsmen swore.

Sella did not.

She was staring at the gap.

"Intermittent feed."

"From where?"

"Don't know."

"Can you kill it?"

"Yes."

She paused.

"Probably."

Broussard looked at her.

Sella glared.

"Do you want the honest version or the reassuring version?"

"Honest."

"Then probably."

"Good."

Gabriel glanced toward him.

"You have strange standards."

"They remain achievable."

The commissar almost smiled.

Almost.

Sella knelt beside the wall panel behind them and opened it.

Inside, cables disappeared into the damaged forward distribution network.

She began tracing labels.

Some had burned away.

Others had been replaced by handwritten markings.

One had apparently been labeled by a man with very strong feelings about penmanship and very little time.

Sella read silently.

Then swore.

"Emergency cross-feed."

"Meaning?"

"When the primary trunk failed, they rerouted part of the forward service grid through an auxiliary line."

"That sounds reasonable."

"It was."

"And now?"

"The insulation somewhere between here and the next junction is gone. Probably collision damage. Maybe heat. Maybe a fast repair that didn't hold."

Broussard looked toward the bodies.

"So the decking is live."

"Sometimes."

"Wonderful."

"That's the problem."

He keyed the hard-line vox.

"Cargo spine, this is Broussard."

Static.

Then Kelm.

"Here."

"We found them."

Silence.

Broussard let it sit.

Kelm asked:

"Alive?"

"No."

Another silence.

Longer.

Sella stopped moving for half a heartbeat.

Then resumed tracing the power route.

Kelm's voice returned quieter.

"Cause?"

"Electrical."

"Where?"

"Service corridor between forward bins. Intermittent arc across an open deck junction."

Kelm swore once.

Not loudly.

Broussard continued.

"Nobody else comes forward."

"Understood."

"Get the Magos looking at the forward auxiliary cross-feed from your side. We need it dead and physically isolated before recovery."

"Understood."

A pause.

Then:

"Names?"

Broussard looked down the corridor.

"Jerrik is first. I can't confirm the other two from here."

Sella said:

"Edren's the second."

Her voice was flat.

Broussard looked at her.

She pointed toward the boot.

"Red repair stitching. His."

"And the third?"

She stared.

"Pallas."

"Certain?"

"Yes."

Broussard keyed the vox again.

"All three confirmed."

There was no answer for several seconds.

Then Kelm said:

"Understood."

The channel closed.

Gabriel remained still beside Broussard.

His eyes were on the bodies.

"Chain reaction."

"Yes."

"First one touches the fault."

"Or falls into it."

"Second reaches for him."

"Yes."

"Third reaches for the second."

Broussard nodded.

Gabriel exhaled through his nose.

"Compassion. Repeated three times."

"Don't make it philosophical."

"I wasn't."

Broussard looked at him.

Gabriel's face was tired.

"That's what makes it worse."

Sella continued working.

Her hands had become extremely precise.

Broussard recognized that too.

There were people who shook when frightened.

People who became angry.

People who froze.

And people whose hands became perfect because perfection was the only thing keeping the rest of them together.

Sella belonged to the last group.

"Found the feed," she said.

"Can you isolate it?"

"Local breaker won't matter. The cross-feed bypasses it."

"Of course it does."

"I need the Magos to kill the auxiliary line upstream."

"How long?"

"If he agrees with me?"

"Yes."

"Five minutes."

"And if he doesn't?"

"Longer."

Gabriel said:

"I can assist with doctrinal persuasion."

Sella looked at the commissar.

For perhaps the first time since Broussard had met her, she smiled without humor.

"That might save time."

The Magos came over the hard line less than a minute later.

His voice arrived layered with static and machine harmonics.

"Forward auxiliary discontinuity confirmed."

Sella answered before Broussard could.

"Kill cross-feed A-Seventeen through C-Nine."

"That will remove illumination and environmental circulation from two forward hold sectors."

"There are three dead men lying across the fault."

A pause.

"Clarification accepted."

The lights went out.

Completely.

For several seconds only their portable lamps remained.

The ventilation died.

The corridor became astonishingly quiet.

Broussard heard the ship creak.

He heard Sella breathing.

He heard one Guardsman shift his weight.

Then the Magos spoke.

"Cross-feed isolated."

Sella tested.

Amber.

Then nothing.

She tested again.

Nothing.

"Again," Broussard said.

She did.

Nothing.

"Again."

Sella looked at him.

He waited.

She tested a third time.

Nothing.

"Dead."

Broussard nodded.

"Now we recover them."

They approached carefully.

Even with the circuit isolated, nobody stepped over the exposed trench.

Sella laid insulating mats across the deck first.

The first body was Jerrik Noll.

There was no question.

His face was turned partly toward them now that they were close.

His eyes were open.

The left side of his coat had burned into the fabric beneath it.

One hand remained inside the service recess where the damaged conductor had apparently arced across exposed metal.

Edren Vale lay immediately behind him.

His right hand had locked around Jerrik's shoulder.

The contact point had burned through both coats.

He had likely found Jerrik collapsed and done exactly what any decent shipmate would do.

He had tried to pull him away.

Pallas Dorn had found them both.

The third man had grabbed Edren.

The current had found him too.

Sella crouched beside Pallas without touching him.

For a long moment she said nothing.

Broussard gave her the time.

Not much.

There was work.

But some.

Finally she said:

"Idiots."

Gabriel looked at her.

Sella's jaw tightened.

"All three of them."

Nobody corrected her.

"Jerrik knew electrical."

Her voice shook once.

Then stopped.

"He should've known better."

Broussard said:

"He may never have seen the fault."

She looked at him.

"The second two did."

"Did they?"

Sella stared at Edren's hand on Jerrik's shoulder.

No one spoke.

Broussard continued quietly.

"They saw a man down."

That was all.

Sella looked away.

Gabriel said nothing.

There was nothing useful for a Commissar to add.

The Imperium had regulations for electrical safety.

It had training catechisms.

Isolation procedures.

Warning sigils.

Required insulating equipment.

Forms for incident review.

Forms for equipment loss.

Forms for death notifications.

Probably a form somewhere for recording that the first two forms had been completed correctly.

None of them altered the oldest human instinct in the corridor.

Someone falls.

You reach for them.

Broussard keyed the vox.

"Cargo spine."

Kelm answered immediately.

"Here."

"Fault is isolated. We have all three."

A long pause.

Then:

"Bring them home."

Broussard looked at the bodies.

Home.

There were worse words for a ship.

"We will."

The recovery took time.

They used insulated drag sheets and did not touch bare metal.

Sella supervised every connection herself.

The two Guardsmen worked without complaint.

Gabriel carried Pallas.

Broussard took Edren with one of the soldiers.

Jerrik came last.

The dead were heavier than the living in ways that had nothing to do with mass.

They had no instinct to help.

No balance.

No small corrections.

No willingness.

Only weight.

By the time the search party reached the cargo spine, Kelm was waiting.

He did not step forward immediately.

He looked at the three covered forms.

Then at Sella.

She shook her head once.

Kelm closed his eyes.

For perhaps two seconds.

No more.

Then he became captain again.

"Medicae bay."

Broussard said:

"They were electrocuted. Junction short. Intermittent arc across the corridor."

Kelm opened his eyes.

"Accident?"

"Everything I saw says accident."

Gabriel added:

"One goes down. Second reaches for him. Third reaches for the second."

Kelm looked at the covered bodies.

His mouth tightened.

"That sounds like them."

Sella made a small noise that might have been a laugh if anyone had been cruel enough to call it one.

Kelm looked toward her.

"You all right?"

"No."

"Good."

She frowned at him.

He nodded toward the bodies.

"I'd be worried if you were."

Sella looked down.

Broussard let the silence stand.

There would be reports.

There would be examinations.

The Magos would inspect the failed junction.

The ship's maintenance history would acquire another ugly paragraph.

Three families would eventually receive notices written by people who had never smelled the corridor where their relatives died.

That was the Imperial way.

Distance made tragedy administratively manageable.

Broussard looked back toward the darkened forward passage.

The search had answered one question.

Jerrik Noll, Edren Vale and Pallas Dorn had not vanished.

They had died in a damaged ship doing ordinary work.

One by electricity.

Two by trying to help.

It was horrible.

It was also, in a strange and unwelcome way, reassuring.

Because horrible things did not have to be mysteries.

Sometimes a damaged cable was only a damaged cable.

Sometimes three dead men were not evidence of conspiracy, xenos intrusion, sabotage or heresy.

Sometimes the universe was perfectly capable of killing people without ideological assistance.

Broussard had always considered that one of its more discouraging efficiencies.

Gabriel came to stand beside him.

"Feel better?"

Broussard considered the question.

"No."

"Neither do I."

"But I understand this."

Gabriel nodded.

Behind them, Kelm was giving quiet instructions for the bodies.

Sella had already turned back toward the forward schematic.

Broussard noticed.

"Where are you going?"

"Back to the junction."

"No."

She looked at him.

"The line is dead."

"The line is dead because a Magos and three layers of emergency power routing currently agree that it is dead."

Sella stared.

Broussard pointed toward the medicae team.

"You are going to drink something hot, sit down for fifteen minutes and let someone else confirm that the thing which killed three of your friends remains dead."

"I don't need—"

"That was not the opening bid."

Sella stopped.

Kelm, despite everything, made a tired sound through his nose.

Gabriel's mouth twitched.

Sella looked from one captain to the other.

Then at the commissar.

"This is conspiracy."

"Yes," Gabriel said. "An extremely well-documented one."

For the first time since they found Jerrik, Sella's face almost became human again.

Almost.

She picked up her tool bag.

"Fifteen minutes."

"Twenty."

"You said fifteen."

"I revised the order after resistance."

She glared at him.

Broussard waited.

Sella walked toward the crew spaces.

Kelm watched her go.

Then looked at Broussard.

"Thank you."

"Don't."

"For what?"

"Making me admit I did something compassionate. It damages discipline."

Kelm almost smiled.

Then his gaze returned to the dead.

The smile disappeared.

Broussard looked once more toward the forward holds.

The immediate mystery had been solved.

Three missing men.

Three bodies.

A shorted junction.

A chain of attempted rescues that had become a chain of deaths.

Simple.

Ugly.

Understandable.

And yet, somewhere farther back in the ship's memory, fourteen seconds were still missing.

Broussard had not forgotten them.
