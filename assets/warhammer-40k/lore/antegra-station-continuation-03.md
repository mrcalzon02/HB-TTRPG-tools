# Antegra Station

## Continuation III — The Department of Necessary Omissions

*An original Cafarron Corridor chronicle continuation. Warhammer 40,000 and associated marks and setting elements are the property of Games Workshop. This non-commercial fan work is not endorsed by or affiliated with Games Workshop.*

The lift arrived empty.

This disappointed Captain Ardan Krell, who had arranged six troopers behind overturned audit desks on the reasonable assumption that anything climbing out of a nonexistent substructure would have the decency to be armed.

Governor Talbor Varik considered the empty lift worse.

Doors opened upon a cage of black iron large enough for twenty men. Its floor was polished by generations of boots. A brass plate above the controls bore the double-headed eagle, the cog of Mars, and a third seal that had been attacked with a chisel until only three letters remained.

D.N.O.

Master Auditor Selene Marr stared at it.

“Do you recognize that?” Varik asked.

“No.”

Commissar-Keeper Dren Solvik did.

Varik watched the recognition pass across his face and disappear behind professional discipline.

“Commissar.”

Solvik sighed.

“The Department of Necessary Omissions.”

Krell looked at him. “That is a real department?”

“Officially?” Solvik said. “No.”

“Unofficially?”

“Especially no.”

Marr rubbed her eyes.

Varik felt, not for the first time, that the Imperium had perfected a form of government in which absurdity ceased to be comic through sheer administrative mass.

The station vox clicked.

Custodian Magos Hester Vhal spoke through the ceiling grille.

“Governor Varik. Do not enter the lift until my party reaches Central Audit.”

Varik stepped into it.

Krell followed immediately.

Solvik swore with admirable restraint and joined them.

Marr remained outside.

Varik looked at her.

“I am an auditor, my lord.”

“Yes.”

“I possess a stylus.”

“Yes.”

“You possess soldiers.”

“Also yes.”

“I believe this is an appropriate moment for professional specialization.”

Varik nodded. “Secure the ledgers.”

The lift doors closed.

Vhal's voice returned.

“You were instructed to wait.”

“I have spent two hundred years waiting for Imperial departments,” Varik said. “They have rarely improved with anticipation.”

The lift descended.

There were no floor indicators beneath Central Audit. Instead, numbers appeared one by one on a brass counter.

Minus one.

Minus two.

Minus five.

Minus twelve.

Krell checked his laspistol.

Solvik examined the walls.

The cage was old. Not neglected. Old.

Every bolt had been maintained. Every cable had been greased. Incense residue stained the upper vents. Somebody had serviced this lift for decades while the station above insisted it did not exist.

At minus eighteen the cage stopped.

The doors opened onto warmth.

Antegra was a cold station. Even its prosperous levels were cold by the standards of civilized worlds, and nobody born there trusted a room in which breath did not show.

Substructure Eighteen was warm enough to be offensive.

A broad corridor stretched away beneath amber lumen globes. Pipes ran along the walls in immaculate ranks. The deck plates were clean. Shrine alcoves held fresh candles. Air recyclers hummed without coughing.

Krell stared.

“We have habitation blocks freezing three levels above the core.”

Solvik touched one of the pipes.

“Warm-water return.”

“Eleven percent,” Varik said.

For sixty-eight years.

The corridor ended at a checkpoint.

Two servitors waited there.

They were human enough to be unpleasant and mechanical enough to make the distinction irrelevant. Each wore a faded blue tabard bearing the mutilated D.N.O. seal.

One raised its head.

“State authority.”

“Talbor Varik. Governor of Antegra.”

The servitor's lenses flickered.

“Authority recognized.”

A pause.

“Memory status?”

Varik went still.

Solvik's hand moved toward his bolt pistol.

“Clarify,” Varik said.

“Memory status.”

“I remember being governor.”

“Insufficient response.”

Krell whispered, “I can shoot it.”

Varik raised a finger.

The servitor repeated itself.

“Memory status.”

Varik thought of the compact bearing his seal.

He thought of rejuvenat treatments, missing years, and the peculiar sensation of encountering his own handwriting as evidence against himself.

“Compromised,” he said.

The checkpoint doors unlocked.

Krell did not lower his weapon.

“I liked it better when doors simply refused to open.”

Beyond lay an office.

Not a laboratory.

Not a reactor vault.

An office.

Hundreds of desks filled a chamber large enough to house a cathedral nave. Filing cabinets rose in armored stacks. Cogitator banks occupied recessed chapels. Pneumatic message tubes crossed the ceiling like brass arteries.

Most of the desks were empty.

Not abandoned.

Empty.

Work lamps still burned. Ink bottles had been capped. Chairs were pushed neatly beneath desks.

A clock on the far wall kept perfect station time.

Krell looked around.

“What was this?”

Solvik answered quietly.

“A records ministry.”

Varik walked to the nearest desk.

A nameplate remained.

ADEPT JUNIOR GHELAS VORN
DEPARTMENT OF NECESSARY OMISSIONS
POPULATION RECONCILIATION DIVISION

A ledger lay beneath it.

Varik opened the cover.

The first page carried a date sixty-eight years old.

The second carried a date from yesterday.

His eyes narrowed.

“Krell.”

The captain leaned over his shoulder.

Rows of numbers filled the page.

Births.

Deaths.

Transfers.

Labor allocations.

Protein consumption.

Thermal draw.

The real population of Antegra.

Not the tithe population.

Not the council population.

The real one.

Every concealed child.

Every unofficial worker.

Every dead citizen whose ration identity had been sold onward.

Every habitation block erased from official census.

Every discrepancy Master Auditor Marr had spent her career finding from the wrong side of the wall.

Krell turned several pages.

“These are current.”

“Yes.”

“Who has been writing them?”

A pneumatic tube clattered overhead.

A brass capsule dropped into a basket beside them.

Nobody moved.

Krell drew his pistol.

Varik opened the capsule.

Inside was a narrow strip of paper.

GOVERNOR PRESENT.

CUSTODIAN PARTY DELAYED.

COMMISSAR PRESENT.

CAPTAIN PRESENT.

MARR REMAINS ABOVE.

Varik read the final line twice.

PROCEED TO INTERVIEW CHAMBER FOUR.

Solvik stared at the ceiling.

“We are being observed.”

Krell looked toward the empty desks.

“That was becoming difficult to miss.”

A green lamp illuminated above a door at the far side of the hall.

INTERVIEW CHAMBER IV.

Varik folded the message and placed it in his coat.

“Governor,” Solvik said, “we should wait for the Mechanicus.”

“Why?”

“They know what this place is.”

“That is precisely why I would like five minutes without them.”

They crossed the office.

Along the way, Varik noticed the portraits.

Governors of Antegra.

His predecessors lined one wall beneath tarnished brass plaques.

The portraits ended with his own.

Not the current official portrait.

An older one.

Varik stood younger beneath the station seal, perhaps one hundred and thirty years of age. His hair had still been mostly black. His left eye was natural. He wore the mourning sash for his first wife.

Beneath it was a plaque.

TALBOR VARIK
SOVEREIGN GOVERNOR
FOUNDING CUSTODIAN — D.N.O. ANTEGRA OFFICE

Krell read it.

Then read it again.

“My lord.”

“Yes.”

“You founded this place.”

“So it would appear.”

Solvik studied the portrait.

“Sixty-eight years ago.”

Varik touched the plaque.

The brass was polished.

Someone maintained that too.

Interview Chamber Four opened before they reached it.

Inside stood a table and four chairs.

One chair was occupied.

The man seated there wore the gray robes of a senior Administratum clerk. He was extremely old. Tubes entered his wrists. A spinal brace held him upright. One side of his face had collapsed inward with age despite repeated rejuvenat work.

His eyes were clear.

“Governor Varik,” he said.

Varik stopped.

The old man smiled.

“You took longer than expected.”

“Who are you?”

“Senior Reconciliator Halbrecht Senn.”

The name meant nothing.

Senn saw that.

His smile faded slightly.

“Yes,” he said. “They said that would happen.”

“Who said?”

“You did.”

Krell's pistol came up.

Senn ignored it.

“You established this office sixty-eight years ago after the Ordo investigation.”

Varik felt something cold beneath the warmth of the room.

“What investigation?”

Senn looked at Solvik.

The Commissar-Keeper had gone pale.

“Dren?” Varik asked.

Solvik said nothing.

Senn folded his hands.

“The investigation into why Antegra's population could not be allowed to appear in the Imperial tithe.”

Krell laughed once.

There was no humor in it.

“We have spent three days trying to expose the people hiding the population.”

“Yes,” Senn said.

“And now you are telling us the governor ordered it hidden?”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

Senn looked at Varik.

“Because the fraud was the quarantine.”

Silence.

Varik sat opposite him.

“Explain.”

Senn reached beneath the table and produced a red file.

The seal upon it had been broken before.

By Varik.

His signature crossed the old wax.

Senn placed the file between them.

“Sixty-eight years ago, miners opened a pre-Imperial vault beneath the southern anchor works. Nothing emerged. No creature. No plague. No machine army.”

Krell relaxed by a fraction.

Senn noticed.

“That was the problem.”

He opened the file.

Photographs lay inside.

Men standing before a black doorway cut into rock.

Men carrying crates.

Men smiling.

Families.

Children.

A schoolroom.

Varik frowned.

“What am I looking at?”

“The first contaminated cohort.”

“They look healthy.”

“They were.”

“Mutated?”

“No.”

“Psykers?”

“No.”

“Xenos infection?”

“Not biologically.”

Varik's patience thinned.

“Then contaminated by what?”

Senn turned the next photograph.

It showed a page of handwriting.

Varik recognized the script.

His own.

Senn said, “Memory.”

The station seemed suddenly quieter.

“Everyone who entered the vault began remembering things that had never happened to them. Languages they had never learned. Places they had never visited. People dead thousands of years before their birth.”

Krell said, “Possession.”

“That was the first theory.”

“And the second?” Solvik asked.

Senn looked at him.

“You know the second.”

Solvik's jaw tightened.

Varik turned.

“Dren.”

The Commissar-Keeper finally met his eyes.

“My predecessor was assigned to the investigation.”

“You knew.”

“I knew there had been an investigation. I did not know the findings. The file transferred under commissariat seal with instructions not to open unless Substructure Eighteen reactivated.”

“And you never thought to mention that?”

“I receive many sealed instructions.”

Varik stared at him.

Solvik added, “Most are less interesting.”

Senn coughed.

The sound rattled through his implants.

“The memories spread through conversation. Not always. Not predictably. Families acquired them. Work crews. Children born years after the vault was sealed described the same places.”

Varik looked again at the photographs.

“What places?”

Senn hesitated.

Then pushed another sheet across the table.

A star chart.

Krell leaned closer.

“That is not the Cafarron Corridor.”

“No.”

“Where is it?”

“We do not know.”

Varik studied the annotations.

Several stars had Imperial catalog numbers.

Their positions were wrong.

Not slightly wrong.

Wrong by millennia of stellar drift.

“These observations are ancient.”

“Yes.”

“How ancient?”

Senn looked tired.

“Some predate the Imperium.”

The vox grille above them clicked.

Magos Vhal's voice filled the chamber.

“Reconciliator Senn. You are exceeding disclosure protocol.”

Senn looked upward.

“I am dying.”

“That condition does not amend protocol.”

“It has improved my opinion of protocol considerably.”

Varik almost smiled.

Almost.

Vhal continued.

“Governor Varik, remain where you are.”

Varik ignored her.

“Why hide the population?” he asked Senn.

“Because the Administratum counts people.”

“I am aware.”

“No, Governor. It counts them, moves them, levies them, drafts them, marries their labor to distant obligations. Antegra's infected population could not be allowed into ordinary Imperial circulation.”

Krell understood first.

“The tithe.”

Senn nodded.

“Every falsified worker was a person kept here. Every invented death prevented a transfer. Every missing regiment levy kept a cohort from boarding a transport. Every fraudulent census was a quarantine wall made of paper.”

Varik stared at the red file.

“How many?”

Senn did not answer.

“How many people carry these memories?”

“Today?”

“Yes.”

Senn looked toward the ceiling as if consulting figures only he could see.

“Approximately thirty-eight percent of Antegra's actual population.”

Krell lowered his pistol.

Varik felt the station above him in a new way.

Millions of voices.

Workers.

Children.

Clerks.

Miners.

People who had been hidden not because the council wanted to steal from the Imperium, but because sixty-eight years earlier Talbor Varik had apparently concluded that giving them to the Imperium would be worse.

“And the missing memories?” Varik asked.

Senn looked at him with something close to pity.

“You ordered them removed.”

“From me.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“Because you were infected.”

The door behind them locked.

Krell spun.

Solvik drew his bolt pistol.

Varik did neither.

He looked down at his hands.

There was an old scar across his right palm.

He had never remembered receiving it.

Suddenly he remembered black stone beneath his fingers.

A door without hinges.

A sky with too many moons.

A woman speaking a language he had never learned.

And something beyond the door remembering him back.

Varik inhaled sharply.

Senn closed his eyes.

“Oh,” the old reconciliator said.

The warmth of Substructure Eighteen no longer felt comforting.

Senn reached for the emergency vox.

“Magos.”

Vhal answered immediately.

“I observed it.”

Senn looked at Varik.

“So did I.”

Krell stepped between them.

“What happened?”

Senn's voice was very quiet.

“The quarantine just recognized its governor.”
