# THE EUROPAN CREWMAN’S PRIMER

Conduct, Readiness, and Survival in the Submariner’s Trade

Issued for circulation aboard commercial, Coalition, research, transport, salvage, and independently operated vessels beneath the Europan ice.

## FOREWORD

There are easier ways to earn a living than serving aboard a submarine on Europa.

There are warmer ways. Safer ways. Ways that do not place several thousand tonnes of steel between crushing pressure on one side and a temperamental reactor on the other. Ways that do not require a person to share narrow corridors with armed security officers, exhausted engineers, overworked medics, unstable assistants, and whatever has begun scratching at the outer hull.

Nevertheless, you have chosen the submariner’s trade.

Perhaps you came for the marks. Perhaps you are running from debts, authorities, relatives, former employers, or all four at once. Perhaps you genuinely believe there is something noble in carrying supplies between the last lights of human civilization. Whatever brought you below the ice, your survival now depends upon the people beside you—and theirs depends upon you.

A submarine is not merely a vehicle. It is a sealed and interdependent community operating inside an environment that is instantly lethal to human life. Every hatch, junction box, oxygen tank, ammunition crate, medical cabinet, and pressure seal exists because someone may need it during the worst minute of their life.

Competence matters. Discipline matters. Restraint matters.

Most importantly, cooperation matters.

This primer does not demand perfection. Every veteran was once an inexperienced hand, and every capable officer has made mistakes they would prefer omitted from the official record. What is required is that each crew member make a reasonable effort to learn, communicate, conserve resources, follow lawful orders, and avoid placing the vessel in unnecessary danger.

Europa is already trying to kill us.

The crew need not assist it.

## REGARD EVERY CONTROL AS LOADED

A newly assigned crew member will encounter switches, levers, consoles, detonators, discharge controls, reactor panels, weapon interfaces, ballast controls, docking systems, and emergency devices whose purpose may not be immediately obvious.

Do not operate unfamiliar equipment merely to discover what it does.

A switch aboard a Europan submarine may control a lamp. It may also discharge an electrical coil through a diver working outside the hull. A control at the helm may alter ballast, disengage autopilot, open a docking hatch, change the vessel’s depth, or send the submarine directly into rock.

The consequences of careless experimentation include, but are not limited to:

- Reactor instability or meltdown.

- Accidental discharge of nuclear or conventional ordnance.

- Electrocution of personnel outside the vessel.

- Uncontrolled flooding or ballast failure.

- Collision with terrain, wreckage, ice, or hostile fauna.

- Destruction of a shuttle attempting to dock.

- Loss of weapons required later in the voyage.

- Sudden and entirely preventable death.

Before operating unfamiliar equipment, ask a qualified crew member what it controls. Observe how the system is used. Consult available labels, diagrams, manuals, or status displays.

Curiosity is valuable. Uncontrolled curiosity aboard a pressure vessel is not.

When in doubt, leave the control untouched until you understand it.

## REPORT ABOARD READY TO LEARN

No captain expects a new submariner to know every vessel, every junction route, or every emergency procedure. Submarines differ. Captains differ. Some engineers have rewired their vessels so thoroughly that not even the original shipyard could explain them.

What the crew may reasonably expect is that you understand the fundamental controls of your own body and profession.

Before joining an active voyage, learn how to:

- Move through compartments and climb ladders.

- Open, close, and secure hatches.

- Pick up, equip, store, and transfer items.

- Use the vessel’s inventory and item-finding systems.

- Wear a diving suit and exchange oxygen tanks.

- Operate the basic tools assigned to your profession.

- Communicate over radio and local voice channels.

- Recognize flooding, fire, low oxygen, and reactor alarms.

- Identify the principal workspaces aboard a submarine.

The middle of a hull breach is not the appropriate time to ask how to equip a welding tool. A reactor fire is not the ideal setting for a detailed explanation of electrical repair. A medic treating three casualties cannot be expected to abandon them because someone has misplaced the screwdriver already attached to their tool belt.

Ask questions, but exercise judgment regarding when and how you ask them.

Questions concerning an unfamiliar submarine, an unusual system, a mission objective, or a genuine shortage are entirely reasonable. Experienced crew members should answer them when circumstances permit. No one benefits when a new hand remains silent, guesses incorrectly, and creates a larger emergency.

Before reporting an item missing, use the item finder and search the appropriate storage compartments. Before requesting personal instruction during a crisis, determine whether the matter can wait until the vessel is stable.

Self-sufficiency does not mean knowing everything. It means attempting the obvious solutions before transferring the entire burden to someone else.

New personnel should be taught.

New personnel must also make an honest effort to learn.

## THE GENERAL CREWMAN

The Assistant, the Apprentice, and the Person Somehow Doing Everyone Else’s Job

If you are joining a submarine for the first time and cannot yet meet the basic requirements of a specialist position, you should strongly consider signing aboard as an assistant.

This is not an insult.

It is not an admission that you know nothing.

It is not an announcement that you are useless, expendable, or expected to spend the voyage sitting inside a storage cabinet until something needs testing.

It means you are entering the crew as a general submariner rather than falsely claiming expertise in medicine, engineering, security, or navigation.

That is sensible.

It is certainly more sensible than signing aboard as a medical officer because you once successfully applied an adhesive bandage, or becoming a reactor engineer because you have seen electricity and found the experience memorable.

The assistant is the submarine’s flexible crewman: a worker who can support every department, learn several trades, fill sudden gaps, and take responsibility for the dozens of important tasks that specialists are too busy, injured, flooded, or on fire to perform.

A new assistant is effectively saying:

“I do not yet know everything, but I am willing to work, learn, carry things, follow instructions, and refrain from lying about my qualifications.”

On Europa, this places you ahead of an alarming percentage of the labor market.

## YOU ARE NOT THE LEFTOVER ROLE

Specialist positions aboard a submarine are limited.

There may already be a captain, doctor, engineer, mechanic, and security officer. This does not mean the important jobs have been claimed and you have arrived too late to contribute.

It means the obvious jobs have been claimed.

The less obvious jobs remain everywhere.

Somebody still needs to:

- Carry ammunition to the guns.

- Bring welding fuel to the mechanic.

- Bring electrical components to the engineer.

- Drag wounded personnel to medical.

- Refill oxygen tanks.

- Retrieve tools.

- Move supplies.

- Watch the rear of the away team.

- Operate an unattended pump.

- Extinguish a fire.

- Report the creature entering the ballast tank.

- Explain why there is a creature entering the ballast tank.

- Help remove the creature from the ballast tank.

- Prevent someone from putting another creature in the ballast tank to see whether they become friends.

The assistant is not what remains after every useful role has been selected.

The assistant is the role that remains useful after every specialist becomes occupied.

A submarine with no assistants may still operate.

It will simply do so more slowly, less efficiently, and with every specialist periodically abandoning critical work to search for a screwdriver.

## THE ASSISTANT’S TRUE SPECIALTY

An assistant is often described as lacking a specialty.

This is inaccurate.

The assistant’s specialty is whatever has become a problem in the last thirty seconds.

A specialist usually owns a clearly defined responsibility.

The engineer manages the reactor and grid.

The mechanic maintains the hull and machinery.

The doctor treats the injured.

Security shoots creatures while attempting not to shoot the people standing in front of the creatures.

The captain points the submarine in the direction least likely to contain a wall.

The assistant’s responsibilities are more flexible:

- If something needs carrying, carry it.

- If something needs fetching, fetch it.

- If someone needs help, help them.

- If a compartment is flooding, find a pump or a welding tool.

- If a gun is empty, bring ammunition.

- If someone is bleeding, move them toward the person wearing the medical uniform.

- If the person wearing the medical uniform is the one bleeding, move them anyway.

- If the captain asks where the away team is, provide a more useful answer than “away.”

The assistant goes where the submarine’s current emergency has created a labor shortage.

This is why a veteran assistant may be more useful than a mediocre specialist.

The specialist knows one department.

The veteran assistant knows where every department keeps the things it claims nobody else is allowed to touch.

## YOU ARE NOT USELESS

New assistants occasionally believe that they are unimportant because no machinery has their name written above it.

Do not make this mistake.

The reactor has an engineer.

Medical has a doctor.

The helm has a captain.

The assistant has the entire remaining submarine.

You are not ballast with pockets.

You are not an emergency ration for the mudraptors.

You are not the crew member automatically selected to touch every suspicious artifact because “the specialists are too valuable.”

You are certainly not required to wear a clown mask, although historical precedent suggests one may eventually appear in your locker whether you requested it or not.

A competent assistant reduces the amount of time specialists spend doing work that does not require specialist training.

Consider the following:

A mechanic is welding a major hull rupture. Their welding tank empties.

Without an assistant, the mechanic stops repairing the hull, runs across the submarine, searches for fuel, returns, and discovers that the sea has used the delay to purchase the compartment.

With an assistant, a fresh tank arrives before the first one empties.

The mechanic keeps welding.

The hull remains intact.

The crew survives.

The assistant did not perform the dramatic repair.

They made the dramatic repair possible.

This distinction describes a great deal of real work.

## THE MULTIPLIER OF PEOPLE WHO ALREADY LOOK BUSY

A heavily experienced assistant can multiply the effectiveness of every department aboard the vessel.

Not literally.

If your assistant begins physically multiplying, notify medical and do not allow either copy near the ballast controls.

Operationally, however, a capable assistant enables specialists to remain at their stations.

The engineer stays at the reactor because the assistant brings replacement components.

The mechanic keeps sealing breaches because the assistant refills welding tanks.

The medic continues treating critical casualties because the assistant carries patients into medical.

Security keeps firing because the assistant reloads the weapon.

The captain remains at the helm because the assistant checks whether the away team has returned.

This is the assistant’s real value:

They prevent skilled personnel from spending half the emergency running errands.

A veteran general crewman may walk into a damaged compartment, identify what the specialist is about to need, retrieve it, and return before being asked.

This is not telepathy.

It is experience, observation, and familiarity with the recurring habits of professionals who always discover they need another component when the nearest cabinet is three rooms away.

## THE SACRED DUTY OF FETCHING THINGS

Fetching supplies is sometimes dismissed as unimportant work.

It becomes considerably more important when the item being fetched is:

- The last welding tank.

- A replacement fuel rod.

- The blood pack required by a dying crew member.

- Ammunition for the only gun facing the threat.

- A diving suit for the person trapped inside a flooding compartment.

- A fire extinguisher.

- The captain’s emergency revolver.

- The engineer who knows why the reactor has begun making that noise.

A good assistant learns where supplies are stored.

A great assistant learns where supplies are actually stored after the crew has finished placing them anywhere except their designated storage.

The labels may say:

- Welding equipment.

- Medical supplies.

- Electrical components.

- Ammunition.

- Diving equipment.

The truth may be:

- Welding equipment in medical.

- Medical supplies in the captain’s cabin.

- Electrical components in a toolbox carried by an unconscious mechanic.

- Ammunition scattered across three compartments.

- Diving equipment worn by someone who went to the station bar.

Finding things is a legitimate maritime skill.

The assistant who knows where the replacement fuel rods are may briefly become the most important person on the submarine.

Enjoy the moment.

Then bring the rods to engineering.

## LEARN THE BOAT BEFORE THE BOAT LEARNS YOU

The assistant position is one of the best ways to learn the layout of a submarine.

Specialists frequently remain near their assigned departments.

The assistant travels everywhere.

Use this opportunity to learn:

- Where the reactor is.

- Where the replacement fuel is.

- Where the engine is.

- Where the pumps are.

- Which rooms are ballast tanks.

- Where oxygen is generated.

- Where medical stores its supplies.

- Where security stores weapons.

- Where ammunition is supposed to be stored.

- Where ammunition has actually been stored.

- Where the airlocks are.

- Which airlock someone has left open.

- Where the fabricator and deconstructor are.

- Which doors lead toward safety.

- Which doors lead into a flooded ballast tank.

- Which controls should never be operated merely because they are red and pleasingly illuminated.

An assistant who learns the submarine can respond quickly.

An assistant who does not may spend the emergency running through the same corridor repeatedly while asking where engineering is.

The third time you pass the reactor, consider the possibility that you have found it.

## THE ASSISTANT’S UNIVERSAL RESPONSE KIT

A general crewman should gradually learn how to use the basic tools found throughout the vessel.

A practical assistant may carry or know how to obtain:

- A wrench.

- A screwdriver.

- A welding tool.

- A spare welding tank.

- A fire extinguisher.

- A diving mask.

- A basic medical item.

- A flashlight.

- A radio headset.

- A tool belt.

- Enough empty storage space to carry something useful.

Do not carry every tool aboard the submarine.

The objective is to remain prepared, not to become a mobile evidence locker.

An assistant carrying three welding tools, two crowbars, four masks, every screwdriver aboard, nine bandages, an empty shotgun, two fuel rods, and a musical instrument has not created an effective loadout.

They have become a loot container capable of speech.

Take what you need for the work you are performing.

Return what you no longer require.

Leave enough equipment for the other people attempting to keep the vessel alive.

## ASK WHAT NEEDS DOING

When unsure how to contribute, ask the department heads.

Useful questions include:

- “Engineering, do you need components?”

- “Mechanics, where is the worst breach?”

- “Medical, do you need casualties moved?”

- “Security, which gun needs ammunition?”

- “Captain, where do you need another pair of hands?”

- “Away team, do you need a porter or lookout?”

- “Who used the last welding tank?”

- “Why is there water coming through the ceiling?”

- “Was the ceiling always supposed to do that?”

Ask once.

Listen to the answer.

Do the work.

Do not stand beside a mechanic actively welding the ocean out of the compartment and repeatedly ask whether they need help while making no effort to retrieve the empty tank at their feet.

If everyone is too busy to answer, look for obvious work:

- Refill tanks.

- Return tools.

- Recharge batteries.

- Carry supplies.

- Move casualties.

- Watch the rear.

- Secure loose equipment.

- Close doors behind you.

- Report anything unusual.

- Report anything normal that has become unusual.

- Report anything that has teeth and was not listed on the crew roster.

## DO NOT HELP SO HARD THAT YOU BECOME THE PROBLEM

There is a difference between helping and inserting yourself into every operation.

Do not crowd specialists while they work.

Do not seize controls from someone who knows how to use them.

Do not enter a reactor room merely because the engineer is inside and appears lonely.

Do not open a sealed compartment because someone might need assistance unless you have first determined why it was sealed.

Do not drag a casualty away from medical while the doctor is treating them.

Do not reload a weapon with ammunition you found in an unlabeled crate without confirming what it contains.

Do not repair junction boxes in a random order because they all look equally broken.

Ask where help is needed.

If no answer is possible, choose work whose benefit is obvious and whose risks you understand.

A good assistant reduces chaos.

An assistant who helps indiscriminately may merely distribute chaos more evenly across the submarine.

## SHADOWING SPECIALISTS WITHOUT BECOMING THEIR SHADOW

One of the assistant’s most valuable duties is learning from experienced personnel.

Ask permission to observe when circumstances permit.

A willing engineer may show you:

- Reactor operation.

- Electrical repair.

- Junction routing.

- Component fabrication.

- Power-grid management.

A willing mechanic may show you:

- Hull repair.

- Pump operation.

- Engine maintenance.

- Fabrication.

- Ballast systems.

A willing medic may show you:

- Basic first aid.

- Blood replacement.

- Burn treatment.

- Oxygen-deprivation treatment.

- Poison recognition.

A willing security officer may show you:

- Weapon handling.

- Ammunition types.

- Firing-line discipline.

- Prisoner restraint.

- Boarding procedures.

A willing captain may show you:

- Navigation.

- Sonar interpretation.

- Mission planning.

- Crew coordination.

- The private art of staring at a sonar contact and pretending to know what it is.

Observe.

Ask useful questions.

Do not interrupt constantly.

Do not stand so close that the specialist cannot reach the machinery.

Do not imitate a procedure you only half understood during an emergency.

Learning requires exposure.

It also requires patience.

## BECOMING DANGEROUSLY COMPETENT

An assistant should not remain permanently dependent.

Over time, learn to perform basic tasks across several departments.

A veteran assistant may be able to:

- Seal a hull breach.

- Replace a damaged electrical component.

- Refill and transport oxygen or welding tanks.

- Administer basic first aid.

- Operate a gun under supervision.

- Assist with reactor refueling.

- Fabricate common supplies.

- Repair simple machinery.

- Navigate familiar compartments in darkness.

- Prepare away-team equipment.

- Identify common biological threats.

- Recognize when something should be left entirely to a specialist.

The final skill is among the most important.

A little knowledge is useful.

A little knowledge combined with confidence, a crowbar, and access to the reactor controls is how incident reports become training materials.

Become competent.

Do not become convinced that competence in one emergency has made you master of every department.

## ASSISTANTS DURING FLOODING

When flooding occurs, assistants are among the most useful personnel aboard.

Your tasks may include:

- Retrieving welding tools.

- Bringing spare welding tanks.

- Operating pumps.

- Closing hatches.

- Moving supplies out of the water.

- Dragging unconscious personnel to safety.

- Carrying diving suits to trapped crew members.

- Reporting the location and severity of breaches.

- Watching for creatures entering through the damaged hull.

Do not crowd the mechanic directly against the rupture.

Do not stand between the welding tool and the damaged hull.

Do not open doors into flooded compartments without considering where the water will go.

Do not remove your diving suit simply because the room appears dry while the pressure alarm continues screaming.

If a compartment is fully flooded and the breach cannot be reached safely, assist with containment.

Close doors.

Preserve dry compartments.

Move casualties.

Keep repair supplies moving.

The ocean does not need help spreading through the vessel.

## ASSISTANTS DURING COMBAT

During combat, an assistant should determine what the security and gunnery teams require.

Common tasks include:

- Carrying ammunition.

- Reloading unattended weapon loaders.

- Reporting threats.

- Watching corridors for boarders.

- Moving wounded personnel.

- Retrieving weapons for authorized crew.

- Securing loose supplies.

- Repairing damage behind the firing line.

- Maintaining oxygen and welding supplies.

Do not stand in front of armed security personnel.

Do not run across an active firing line.

Do not pick up an unfamiliar heavy weapon and discharge it into a crowded compartment because the enemy appeared briefly visible.

Do not assume that wearing a security helmet has granted you tactical authority.

If armed, obey firing-line discipline.

If unarmed, remain useful without becoming scenery between the shooter and the target.

## ASSISTANTS IN MEDICAL

The medical department may require help during mass-casualty events.

An assistant may:

- Carry injured personnel into medical.

- Move stable patients away from treatment areas.

- Retrieve blood packs, bandages, oxygen, or antidotes.

- Monitor unconscious personnel.

- Report symptoms accurately.

- Clean blood and hazards after treatment.

- Restrain violent patients when directed.

Do not administer unfamiliar drugs because the label sounds encouraging.

Do not distribute narcotics to improve morale.

Do not remove medicine from medical storage without telling the medical officer.

Do not stand over the doctor repeatedly asking whether the patient will survive.

The doctor is attempting to answer that question with their hands.

If instructed to bring a specific medicine, bring that medicine.

If you cannot find it, say so.

Do not substitute another syringe because both contain liquid.

## ASSISTANTS IN ENGINEERING

Engineering often requires components, fuel, tools, and someone willing to enter unpleasant compartments.

An assistant may:

- Carry replacement electrical components.

- Retrieve fuel rods.

- Move discharged rods into safe storage.

- Transport batteries.

- Report damaged junction boxes.

- Assist with rewiring under supervision.

- Operate emergency power systems when instructed.

- Watch for flooding while the engineer works.

Do not insert fuel into the reactor without authorization.

Do not remove fuel from the reactor to see whether the engineer notices.

Do not rewire systems according to personal artistic preference.

Do not touch exposed electrical equipment while standing in water.

If the engineer tells you to remain clear of a panel, remain clear.

Electricity does not distinguish between curiosity and sabotage.

## ASSISTANTS ON AWAY MISSIONS

An away team benefits greatly from a competent assistant.

You may serve as:

- Porter.

- Lookout.

- Rear guard.

- Salvage carrier.

- Oxygen monitor.

- Tool carrier.

- Casualty handler.

- Specimen carrier.

- The person who remembers which direction leads back to the submarine.

Carry useful supplies without taking every item aboard.

Maintain communication.

Watch oxygen.

Do not wander away because you saw something interesting.

Do not touch alien artifacts without instruction.

Do not carry an unknown egg, growth, parasite, or severed creature part back to the submarine inside your clothing.

If the away-team leader says retreat, retreat.

You may ask why after reaching the airlock.

## THE ASSISTANT AND THE CLOWN QUESTION

The assistant’s profession has acquired a complicated relationship with clown attire.

A clown suit does not automatically make a crew member incompetent.

A bicycle horn does not automatically make a person dangerous.

Humor may improve morale during long voyages.

The difficulty begins when comedy obstructs work, wastes supplies, conceals sabotage, interferes with communication, or places the vessel at risk.

A harmless joke ends when the emergency begins.

A competent clown still closes hatches, carries ammunition, assists the wounded, obeys evacuation orders, and refrains from honking over critical radio traffic.

A dangerous fool does not become harmless merely because their misconduct is described as a prank.

Judge conduct by its consequences.

Do not persecute a crew member for clothing, religious symbolism, unusual speech, or an irritating choice of musical instrument.

Do intervene when any crew member:

- Damages critical systems.

- Wastes irreplaceable supplies.

- Conceals hazards.

- Interferes with medical treatment.

- Blocks emergency access.

- Sabotages communication.

- Endangers the crew while claiming comedic intent.

The issue is not whether someone is funny.

The issue is whether the submarine remains operational after the joke.

## WHEN ALL SPECIALIST ROLES ARE TAKEN

Do not treat a full specialist roster as a reason to leave or refuse work.

A second engineer may still be useful.

A second mechanic may still be useful.

A second medic may still be useful.

A second security officer may still be useful.

However, if you do not possess the qualifications to fill those roles, joining as an assistant allows you to contribute without misrepresenting your experience.

An assistant can train toward a specialist role during the voyage.

A captain may later promote or reassign you based upon demonstrated competence.

Many respected engineers, mechanics, medics, officers, and captains began as general crew.

Some began as clowns.

History is untidy.

## FINDING YOUR FUTURE SPECIALTY

Use assistant service to discover the work you understand and enjoy.

You may find that you prefer:

- Engineering and electrical systems.

- Mechanical repair and fabrication.

- Medical treatment.

- Security and weapons.

- Navigation and command.

- Salvage and exploration.

- Logistics and supply management.

- Remaining a general crewman because every department keeps creating work for you.

Follow experienced personnel.

Practice basic tasks.

Ask for supervised responsibility.

Learn the terminology.

Observe emergencies.

Record mistakes.

Do not claim a specialty before you can perform its essential duties.

A uniform is not a qualification.

Neither is confidence.

## THE VETERAN ASSISTANT

A veteran assistant may become one of the most trusted people aboard.

They know the vessel.

They know the crew.

They know where supplies are hidden.

They know which doors stick, which pumps fail, which junction box sparks, which medic forgets to eat, which security officer forgets to reload, and which engineer has concealed emergency fuel inside a cabinet labeled “mops.”

They may not formally command a department.

Nevertheless, when emergencies occur, people look for them.

A veteran assistant often becomes:

- The captain’s runner.

- The engineer’s extra hands.

- The mechanic’s supply line.

- The medic’s stretcher team.

- Security’s ammunition carrier.

- The away team’s most experienced generalist.

- The person everyone asks when an item is missing.

This trust must be earned.

It is earned by being useful, honest, dependable, restrained, and willing to work without requiring constant praise.

## THE ASSISTANT’S PRIVATE SUPERPOWER

Specialists are often identified by their stations.

The engineer is at the reactor.

The medic is in medical.

The mechanic is near the breach.

The security officer is near the weapon.

The captain is at the helm.

The assistant may be anywhere.

This makes the assistant uniquely capable of seeing the whole submarine.

You may notice:

- A supply shortage before the department does.

- A damaged hatch nobody has reported.

- A missing crew member.

- A weapon that has not been reloaded.

- A fire beginning in an unattended compartment.

- A patient deteriorating outside medical.

- A ballast tank behaving strangely.

- A suspicious person carrying equipment in the wrong direction.

- A creature moving through a corridor where no creature was scheduled.

Report what you see.

A specialist may understand their department better than you.

You may understand what is happening between departments better than anyone.

## FINAL RULE OF THE GENERAL CREWMAN

Be useful.

Be willing to learn.

Do not pretend to know what you do not know.

Do not remain idle while obvious work exists.

Do not create emergencies to make yourself feel important.

Carry what must be carried.

Fetch what must be fetched.

Help those who need help.

Learn the vessel.

Learn the crew.

Learn when to act.

Learn when to ask.

Learn when to step aside.

The assistant is not the least important person aboard.

The least important person aboard is the one who refuses to contribute while everyone else is trying to survive.
