## NARCOTICS AND CONTROLLED MEDICATION

The submarine’s medical cabinet may contain a staggering variety of narcotics, stimulants, sedatives, poisons, antidotes, antibiotics, anti-radiation treatments, and experimental compounds.
Access to these substances is granted because the medical officer may need them to save lives.
It is not granted so the infirmary can become the liveliest social compartment on the vessel.
The medical officer must control access to high-risk medication. Do not distribute narcotics merely because a crew member asks. Do not leave dangerous drugs scattered across the deck. Do not hand a crate of morphine to an assistant because they promise to “be responsible.”
A medication can be appropriate in one dose and lethal in another.
Before administering a controlled drug, consider:
* The patient’s condition.
* The seriousness of their pain.
* Other drugs already administered.
* The likelihood of overdose.
* The possibility of addiction or withdrawal.
* The supply remaining aboard.
* Whether a safer treatment is available.
Do not repeatedly medicate a patient without checking what they have already received. In an emergency involving several medics, communicate doses clearly.
A patient should not receive three separate injections from three separate medical personnel because none of them spoke to one another.
Mark treated casualties when practical. Report significant medication over the radio or through established medical channels.
“Patient stabilized with morphine” is useful.
“I gave them something” is not.
