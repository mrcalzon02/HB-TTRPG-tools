## IX. WHY CARTELS RECOGNIZE UNION 208

Criminal organizations do not generally recognize laws because they are impressed by their moral clarity.
They recognize consequences.
A cartel may ignore a court ruling.
It may bribe an inspector.
It may assassinate an administrator.
It may threaten a captain.
It cannot easily manufacture experienced submariners, reactor technicians, pressure-hull welders, surgeons, and cargo pilots on demand.
Criminal stations are often more dependent upon maritime labor than lawful ones.
They cannot openly purchase certain goods.
They rely upon smugglers.
They need pilots familiar with hidden routes.
They require mechanics willing to work on modified vessels.
They require medical officers who ask few questions.
They require merchants willing to enter dangerous territory.
A cartel that violates Union 208 rules may discover that all of these people have independently decided to be elsewhere.
Even pirates observe certain union protections.
They may seize cargo.
They may demand ransom.
They may rob a vessel.
But many will release ordinary crew members, honor medical neutrality, and avoid interfering with union relief shipments.
This restraint should not be mistaken for kindness.
It is informed self-preservation.
A pirate captain may steal ten crates of ammunition.
They will hesitate before creating a labor dispute that prevents every friendly station from selling them food.
