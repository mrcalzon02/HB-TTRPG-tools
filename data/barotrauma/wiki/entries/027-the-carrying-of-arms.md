## THE CARRYING OF ARMS

A firearm aboard a submarine is not a symbol of rank, authority, or personal importance. It is a tool entrusted to a crew member for the defence of the vessel and the lives aboard it.
Security personnel, armed divers, and away-team members must understand that possessing a weapon does not require its immediate use. The ability to fire is not the same as having a safe opportunity to fire.
A single crawler outside the hull may not justify a nuclear railgun shell. A crew member wearing a clown suit is not automatically a hostile combatant. An argument in the reactor compartment is rarely improved by launching a grenade into it. A mudraptor surrounded by fleeing crew requires a clear line of fire, not indiscriminate panic.
Before firing, identify:
1. The threat.
2. What lies between you and the threat.
3. What lies behind the threat.
4. Which weapon is appropriate.
5. Whether friendly personnel are within or near the line of fire.
6. Whether the ammunition may be required against a greater threat later.
The use of force should always be proportionate to the danger.
When dealing with disorder among the crew, begin with clear instructions. Use restraint, stun weapons, handcuffs, or other nonlethal measures when circumstances allow. Lethal force is reserved for immediate and serious threats to life, the vessel, or the mission.
Security officers are expected to protect the crew, not terrorize them.
They are not permitted to manufacture confrontations, demand personal obedience beyond their assigned authority, or punish imagined disrespect. They do not execute crew members because they are irritating. They do not fire blindly into crowded compartments. They do not empty weapons into incapacitated personnel merely to demonstrate certainty.
Trigger discipline preserves ammunition, prevents accidental casualties, and maintains the crew’s trust.
A security officer feared by every person aboard is not maintaining order. They are creating the next emergency.
