## V. TRIALS, SUPPRESSION, AND THE BLACKLIST YEARS

The growth of Union 208 was not peaceful.
Corporate operators regarded collective bargaining as interference with commercial freedom.
Station administrators described union action as obstruction.
Private security companies treated organizers as agitators.
Several early union representatives disappeared during transit.
Others were arrested on charges that ranged from contractual interference to sedition, theft of company time, illegal assembly, and failure to respect management authority.
Employers established blacklists.
A worker identified as a union organizer could be denied employment across several stations.
Captains who recognized union standards were refused contracts.
Merchants were pressured not to supply vessels carrying union representatives.
In response, the union developed its own employment and supply network.
Union captains hired blacklisted workers.
Union dockworkers prioritized compliant vessels.
Union-affiliated merchants extended credit.
Mechanics shared parts.
Medical officers created emergency treatment funds.
Submarines carried messages between isolated chapters.
The attempt to starve the union produced the mutual-aid system that ultimately made it far more powerful.
The blacklists failed because they assumed workers required access to employers more than employers required access to workers.
On Europa, this assumption has a limited operational lifespan.
A corporation may own twelve submarines.
Without crews, it owns twelve expensive pressure vessels attached to a dock.
