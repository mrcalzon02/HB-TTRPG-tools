## PORT IS NOT WASTED TIME

Crew members are often eager to leave a station as quickly as possible.
This is understandable. A submarine earns little while sitting motionless in a docking berth. No cargo is being delivered. No wreck is being salvaged. No mineral deposit is being harvested. No bounty is being collected. The vessel is making no visible progress toward its next objective.
To use the language occasionally heard from less patient crew members, the voyage is not “moving forward.”
This view mistakes movement for progress.
Time spent at a station is frequently as valuable as time spent underway.
While docked, the submarine’s immediate power and pressure requirements are supported by the station’s infrastructure. The reactor can be taken out of its ordinary operating cycle. Batteries can be restored. Damaged systems can be examined without the vessel actively descending through hostile waters. Supplies can be counted in relative safety. Departments can compare what was consumed during the previous mission against what will be required during the next.
It may appear that nothing is happening.
In reality, the next voyage is being made possible.
A port call is often the crew’s only reliable period of respite between emergencies. It is a time to rest, reorganize, replace missing personnel, purchase supplies, review damage, fabricate equipment, examine new mission opportunities, and determine what dangers lie ahead.
Rushing the submarine away from the station without proper preparation may save several minutes at the dock and cost the crew an entire voyage beneath the ice.
