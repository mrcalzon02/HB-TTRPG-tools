## THE EXTENDED VOYAGE

As a submarine travels toward the less settled edges of Europan society, stations become more distant, less reliable, and more poorly stocked.
The voyage between ports may extend across many kilometres of hostile water. The next station may lack the supplies available at the current one. It may be abandoned, damaged, quarantined, occupied by hostile forces, or unwilling to trade.
A vessel preparing for such a crossing must think beyond the next few minutes.
Extended voyages require reserves.
This may mean carrying:
* Multiple reactor fuel rods.
* Additional welding and oxygen tanks.
* Spare ammunition for every installed weapon.
* Materials for fabricating additional supplies.
* Reserve medical treatment.
* Away-team provisions.
* Replacement batteries and tools.
* Backup diving equipment.
* Supplies for unexpected return travel.
* Enough material to survive more than one major engagement.
The correct amount depends upon the submarine, route, mission, crew size, installed equipment, and expected danger.
There is no universal answer.
There is, however, one reliably incorrect answer:
“We have one fuel rod in the reactor. We cannot possibly need another.”
A reserve is not wasted simply because it was not consumed.
A fuel rod that returns unused has still served its purpose by ensuring that the submarine was not stranded.
A medical kit that remains unopened was not unnecessary.
A box of ammunition that was not fired still provided the vessel with the ability to respond to an attack.
Preparation should be measured against what could reasonably happen, not merely against what happened during the easiest previous voyage.
