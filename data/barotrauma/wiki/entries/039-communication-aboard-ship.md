## COMMUNICATION ABOARD SHIP

A submarine survives on accurate information delivered at the proper time.
The crew does not need a continuous account of every thought passing through your mind. It does need to know that the ballast tank has breached, the reactor is overheating, a mudraptor has entered engineering, the medic is incapacitated, or a diving party has not returned.
Mission-critical communication should be concise.
State the location, the problem, and what assistance is required.
Examples:
* “Flooding in aft ballast. One mechanic responding.”
* “Fire in engineering. Electrical panel involved.”
* “Two crawlers on the upper hull.”
* “Medic required in the reactor compartment.”
* “Dive team returning with one casualty.”
* “Railgun ammunition low.”
* “Unknown contact approaching from below.”
Avoid speaking over the captain during navigation, over security during a boarding action, or over a medic coordinating casualty treatment. During an emergency, unnecessary radio traffic can conceal the one report that matters.
This does not mean crew members are forbidden from speaking, making suggestions, or maintaining morale. A silent vessel can become as dangerous as a chaotic one. Conversation is welcome when circumstances permit.
The important distinction is between communication and interference.
Report what the crew needs to know. Allow others to answer. Keep arguments off the main channel. Do not demand the captain’s immediate attention for personal inconvenience while the vessel is sinking.
You are part of the crew, not the sole subject of the voyage.
