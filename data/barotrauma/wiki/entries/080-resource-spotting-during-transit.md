## RESOURCE SPOTTING DURING TRANSIT

Mining is among the most common away-team operations conducted by independent submarines.
It is also one of the most important.
A vessel may survive through contracts alone for a time, but ammunition, medicine, fuel, replacement components, equipment, repairs, and crew wages steadily consume its income. Mineral recovery can mean the difference between a submarine that remains financially viable and one that spends every port call deciding which essential system it can no longer afford to support.
Crew members are therefore encouraged to remain observant during transit.
Anyone stationed at an external gun, camera, observation window, or periscope may notice mineral formations along cavern walls. These deposits may be valuable for direct sale or useful in fabricating ammunition, medicine, fuel, tools, electrical components, explosives, and other necessary supplies.
Reporting such a formation is optional unless resource spotting has been assigned as part of your duty.
You are not expected to abandon your post or continuously distract yourself from more important work merely to search for ore. During periods of calm, however, informing the engineering department or captain that a promising formation has been sighted may provide the vessel with a valuable opportunity.
Useful reports include:
* “Mineral formation on the lower cavern wall.”
* “Possible uranium deposit beneath us.”
* “Pyromorphite or similar ore visible near the aft quarter.”
* “Large mixed deposit beside the cave entrance.”
* “Resources visible, but the approach appears occupied.”
* “Mineral field marked on sonar; recommend returning after the current mission.”
Avoid presenting uncertain identification as fact.
From inside the submarine, poor lighting, distance, movement, and obstruction may make exact identification difficult. “Possible uranium” is more useful than confidently announcing uranium and sending the entire engineering department outside to discover several kilograms of something nearly worthless.
The captain must then decide whether stopping is worthwhile.
A deposit may be valuable, but the vessel may be under time pressure, carrying vulnerable cargo, running low on fuel, pursued by hostile forces, or navigating an area too dangerous for a prolonged stop.
The crew member who spots the resource provides information.
The captain decides whether the submarine can safely exploit it.
