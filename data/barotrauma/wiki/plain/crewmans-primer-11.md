## RESOURCE SPOTTING DURING TRANSIT

Mining is among the most common away-team operations conducted by independent submarines.

It is also one of the most important.

A vessel may survive through contracts alone for a time, but ammunition, medicine, fuel, replacement components, equipment, repairs, and crew wages steadily consume its income. Mineral recovery can mean the difference between a submarine that remains financially viable and one that spends every port call deciding which essential system it can no longer afford to support.

Crew members are therefore encouraged to remain observant during transit.

Anyone stationed at an external gun, camera, observation window, or periscope may notice mineral formations along cavern walls. These deposits may be valuable for direct sale or useful in fabricating ammunition, medicine, fuel, tools, electrical components, explosives, and other necessary supplies.

Reporting such a formation is optional unless resource spotting has been assigned as part of your duty.

You are not expected to abandon your post or continuously distract yourself from more important work merely to search for ore. During periods of calm, however, informing the engineering department or captain that a promising formation has been sighted may provide the vessel with a valuable opportunity.

Useful reports include:

- “Mineral formation on the lower cavern wall.”

- “Possible uranium deposit beneath us.”

- “Pyromorphite or similar ore visible near the aft quarter.”

- “Large mixed deposit beside the cave entrance.”

- “Resources visible, but the approach appears occupied.”

- “Mineral field marked on sonar; recommend returning after the current mission.”

Avoid presenting uncertain identification as fact.

From inside the submarine, poor lighting, distance, movement, and obstruction may make exact identification difficult. “Possible uranium” is more useful than confidently announcing uranium and sending the entire engineering department outside to discover several kilograms of something nearly worthless.

The captain must then decide whether stopping is worthwhile.

A deposit may be valuable, but the vessel may be under time pressure, carrying vulnerable cargo, running low on fuel, pursued by hostile forces, or navigating an area too dangerous for a prolonged stop.

The crew member who spots the resource provides information.

The captain decides whether the submarine can safely exploit it.

## THE COST OF STOPPING

A mining opportunity must be considered against the danger of remaining stationary.

A submarine that stops beside a cavern wall becomes easier for hostile wildlife to approach. The engines no longer provide the same ability to evade contact. Diving personnel outside the hull limit the captain’s freedom to manoeuvre. Weapon crews may have restricted firing angles because friendly divers are operating near the target area.

A rich mineral deposit can preserve the vessel’s finances.

It can also keep the submarine in one place long enough to attract every unnatural abomination within several kilometres.

The away team should therefore work deliberately.

Do not spend ten minutes discussing which person should mine the first piece of ore. Do not wander into side passages while the submarine waits. Do not mine three fragments and then begin a recreational tour of the cave system.

Recover the intended resources, remain aware of the situation, and return when recalled.

The captain may terminate the operation at any time because of:

- Approaching hostile contacts.

- Changing current or terrain conditions.

- Reactor or electrical problems.

- Hull damage.

- Loss of communication.

- Mission time constraints.

- Low ammunition.

- Another crew emergency.

- The discovery of a larger threat nearby.

A recall order is not a suggestion.

The mineral deposit will still be present if the submarine survives long enough to return.

## EQUIPPING THE AWAY TEAM

The equipment carried should match the mission.

Carrying too little leaves the team unable to complete its work. Carrying too much deprives the submarine of supplies, slows the diver, and concentrates a dangerous amount of valuable equipment on a person who may not return.

A basic away-team loadout may include:

- A functioning diving suit.

- A full oxygen tank.

- At least one reserve oxygen supply when the mission warrants it.

- An underwater scooter for longer journeys.

- A handheld sonar or navigation aid.

- A radio headset.

- A light source.

- The tools required for the objective.

- Basic medical treatment.

- A weapon appropriate to the expected threat.

- Storage for recovered materials.

- Spare batteries or fuel where necessary.

A mining team may require plasma cutters and ore storage.

A salvage team may require cutting tools, crowbars, wiring equipment, and additional carrying capacity.

A security operation may require weapons, ammunition, restraints, breaching tools, and medical support.

An expedition into an alien ruin may require all of the above, along with substantially more caution than the average crew member believes necessary.

Before cycling the airlock, check:

- Suit condition.

- Oxygen level.

- Radio function.

- Weapon condition.

- Ammunition.

- Tool batteries or fuel.

- Medical supplies.

- Storage capacity.

- Team readiness.

Do not discover that your oxygen tank is empty after leaving the submarine.

Do not take every spare oxygen tank aboard because you are afraid of running out.

Carry a reasonable reserve and leave enough equipment for the other people who may need to respond if your team encounters trouble.

## TEAM ORGANIZATION

Away teams should avoid operating as an unstructured mass.

Even a small operation benefits from basic role assignment.

Depending upon the mission, the team may include:

Team Leader

The leader maintains communication with the submarine, directs movement, monitors the objective, and decides when the team should advance, withdraw, or request assistance.

Specialist

The specialist performs the principal task: mining, repairs, salvage, medical examination, scientific collection, or artifact recovery.

Security

Security watches the surrounding environment, protects the specialist, maintains a clear line of fire, and avoids becoming so fascinated by the work that an approaching crawler reaches the team unnoticed.

Medical Support

For dangerous or extended missions, a medic or a crew member carrying field treatment should accompany the group.

Porter or General Assistant

Recovered ore, cargo, equipment, and salvage must be carried. A general assistant can transport materials, manage oxygen reserves, help recover casualties, and support whichever task becomes urgent.

One person may perform several of these functions on a small team.

What matters is that someone is completing the objective while someone else is watching for the danger approaching from behind.

## MAINTAINING A WATCH

Once outside the vessel, every crew member should remain aware of their surroundings.

Europa’s oceans contain natural predators, territorial creatures, parasites, hostile human forces, pirates, separatists, rogue clowns, automated defences, unstable machinery, and entities whose classification is still disputed by several scientific institutions.

Threats may approach from above, below, behind, or from inside the structure the team is exploring.

Do not allow every member of the team to stare at the same mineral formation.

Do not let the entire group crowd around one storage container while nobody watches the corridor.

Do not become so absorbed in operating a plasma cutter that you fail to notice the creature biting the person beside you.

Maintain periodic visual checks.

Listen to the radio.

Watch the sonar when available.

Be alert for:

- Movement in dark water.

- Sudden changes in local wildlife.

- Damaged or opened doors.

- Fresh blood.

- Unexplained equipment.

- Disturbed silt.

- Electrical activity.

- Mechanical sounds.

- Acidic clouds.

- Unidentified growth.

- Missing team members.

- Radio silence.

- Objects that have moved since the last observation.

A person who sees a threat should report it immediately and clearly.

“Movement behind us.”

“Crawler above the entrance.”

“Possible human contact in the wreck.”

“Green cloud forming near the cave wall.”

“Unknown machine active inside the ruin.”

Do not scream incoherently into the radio and assume the team will understand what has happened.

Location matters.

Direction matters.

The nature of the threat matters.
