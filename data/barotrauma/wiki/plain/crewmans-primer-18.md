## XXIII. COMMAND SUCCESSION

Every submarine requires a known order of succession.

A typical order may be:

1. Executive officer.

1. Relief captain or navigator.

1. Security chief.

1. Chief engineer.

1. Senior surviving department head.

1. Whoever can reach the helm without tripping over the previous candidates.

The exact order is less important than having one.

A submarine should not descend toward crush depth while five officers conduct a constitutional debate over whose hat is most captain-like.

The acting captain should announce:

- That command has transferred.

- Why it transferred.

- Whether the original captain is alive.

- Whether the transfer is temporary.

- What the vessel is doing next.

When the captain recovers, authority should normally be returned unless medical, disciplinary, or legal circumstances indicate otherwise.

## XXIV. THE CAPTAIN AND THE HELM

The captain has primary authority over the helm.

The captain should not be the only person capable of using it.

Train a relief navigator.

Explain the route.

Teach basic docking.

Ensure another officer can hold depth, stop the vessel, and avoid large stationary objects.

A captain who hides all navigational knowledge to protect their authority may succeed until they become unconscious.

At that point, the submarine inherits both the captain’s secrets and a wall.

Authority supported by crew competence is stronger than authority protected through ignorance.

## XXV. THE CAPTAIN DURING A CRISIS

When several emergencies occur at once, establish priorities.

A useful order is:

1. Prevent immediate destruction.

1. Arrest uncontrolled descent or collision.

1. Stabilize the reactor.

1. Control catastrophic flooding.

1. Repel boarders.

1. Preserve breathable compartments.

1. Recover critical casualties.

1. Restore propulsion.

1. Reestablish department control.

1. Decide whether the mission still deserves to exist.

The captain should avoid leaving the helm to personally repair the first damaged item reported.

The captain’s value lies in seeing the entire crisis.

If command abandons the helm to repair one junction box, nobody may remain to notice that the submarine is approaching crush depth, the away team is still outside, and the repair in question supplies the vending machine.

Delegate.

Monitor.

Reassign.

Intervene personally only when necessary.

A captain carrying a wrench may feel useful.

A captain maintaining control is useful.

## XXVI. WHEN TO RETREAT

Retreat may become necessary when the vessel faces:

- Severe ammunition depletion.

- Multiple critical casualties.

- Reactor instability.

- Uncontrollable flooding.

- Navigation failure.

- Loss of medical capability.

- Overwhelming hostile forces.

- Insufficient fuel.

- Activated alien defenses.

- Heavy away-team losses.

- Damage beyond the crew’s repair capacity.

Retreat does not mean the mission is lost forever.

It means the submarine remains available to fail again under better circumstances.

Preserving the crew and vessel provides another opportunity.

Continuing until everyone dies provides a wreck for someone else to salvage.

## FINAL DUTY OF THE CAPTAIN

The captain’s authority is broad because their responsibility is broader.

They are responsible for:

- The route.

- The mission.

- The department assignments.

- The crew roster.

- The away teams.

- The fuel.

- The ammunition.

- The medical reserves.

- The engineering supplies.

- The discipline.

- The pay.

- The succession plan.

- The decision to proceed or retreat.

- The general likelihood that anyone aboard will agree to another voyage.

A captain need not be the finest engineer, physician, gunner, mechanic, or diver.

They must know enough to ask each department the correct questions.

They must understand enough to recognize when the answer is alarming.

Keep the vessel moving.

Keep the departments informed.

Keep the crew paid.

Keep the sonar useful.

Keep the creatures outside.

Keep track of everyone entrusted to your command.

The captain does not own the crew.

The captain holds their survival in trust.

Command is not proven by how many people obey when security is standing behind you.

It is proven by how many crew members, after surviving the voyage and receiving their wages, willingly step aboard when the docking clamps release again.

Preferably without first demanding hazard pay.

Though, under Standard Union 208, they are entirely within their rights to ask.

## CONCLUSION

The submariner’s trade is not built upon heroic individuals acting alone.

It is built upon ordinary people performing necessary work under extraordinary pressure.

The most valuable crew member is not always the finest shot, the most gifted engineer, the quickest medic, or the boldest diver. It is often the person who can be trusted to carry out an order, report a problem, conserve supplies, help a struggling crewmate, and keep their judgment when the alarms begin.

Do not operate equipment you do not understand.

Do not take supplies you do not require.

Do not leave the vessel without authorization.

Do not use weapons without identifying the target and what lies beyond it.

Do not interfere with the helm or chain of command for personal satisfaction.

Do not turn mistakes into feuds.

Do not make Europa’s work easier.

Learn your station. Maintain your equipment. Answer the radio. Watch the hatches. Return what you borrow. Help recover the wounded. Keep the decks clear. Respect the people whose lives are sealed inside the same hull as yours.

Beyond the glass is darkness, pressure, cold, radiation, and hunger.

Inside the hull is the crew.

Treat that distinction as sacred.

Mariners’ Crew Rights, Submarine Labor Law, and Mutual Support Beneath the Ice

## FOREWORD

No Vessel Sails Alone

Every submarine operating beneath the ice of Europa depends upon more than its captain, crew, reactor, and pressure hull.

It depends upon dockworkers who load its cargo.

It depends upon fabricators who produce its parts.

It depends upon miners who recover its fuel stock.

It depends upon station personnel who maintain docking arms, pumps, atmosphere, communications, and navigation beacons.

It depends upon medical workers who receive its wounded.

It depends upon merchants willing to sell it food, oxygen, ammunition, tools, medicine, batteries, welding fuel, and reactor rods.

It depends upon other submariners who answer distress calls in waters where no government vessel is likely to arrive in time.

This interdependence is the foundation of Standard Union 208.

The union’s central principle is simple:

No mariner beneath the ice survives entirely by their own hand.

The most heavily armed captain still needs fuel.

The wealthiest station still needs food.

The most feared cartel still needs spare parts.

The most isolated outpost still requires someone willing to pilot a cargo vessel through the dark.

Europa has many governments, several currencies, dozens of legal systems, and more self-appointed authorities than it has reliable toilets. Yet nearly all of them have learned to recognize Standard Union 208 labor protections.

Some recognize them because they believe in fair treatment.

Some recognize them because they respect tradition.

Some recognize them because their insurers require it.

Some recognize them because the last administrator who ignored the union spent three months attempting to operate an outpost without food deliveries, replacement oxygen membranes, medical stock, or anyone willing to repair the docking collar.

The motivation is less important than the result.

Do not trifle with Union 208.

The sea is cold, the routes are long, and stations that lose the cooperation of mariners tend to die surprisingly quickly.

## I. WHAT STANDARD UNION 208 IS

Standard Union 208 is the largest and oldest broadly recognized submariners’ labor organization operating beneath the Europan ice.

It represents, supports, or provides contractual standards for:

- Captains.

- Navigators.

- Engineers.

- Mechanics.

- Medical officers.

- Security personnel.

- Gunnery crews.

- Cargo handlers.

- Salvage divers.

- Miners.

- Researchers assigned to submarine crews.

- Assistants and general hands.

- Dockworkers.

- Shuttle pilots.

- Station logistics personnel.

- Rescue and recovery teams.

- The numerous unfortunate specialists whose official job description ends with “and other duties as required.”

The number 208 originally referred to the registration designation of the earliest recognized mutual-assistance compact under which the organization operated.

The designation survived long after the original registry ceased to matter.

Several later administrations attempted to rename the union.

The proposed titles included:

- Europan Maritime Labor Federation.

- Subaquatic Transit Workers’ Association.

- United Under-Ice Operations Guild.

- Coalition-Recognized Submersible Personnel Compact.

- Interstation Nautical Employment Standards Authority.

None survived ordinary conversation.

Submariners continued calling it Union 208, and eventually the administrators stopped wasting paper.

The union is not a government, though it frequently performs duties that governments have neglected.

It is not a navy, though it has coordinated convoys, rescues, evacuations, and defensive actions.

It is not a commercial company, though it manages employment registries, benefit funds, emergency contracts, and supply guarantees.

It is not a criminal organization, though several station authorities have described it as one immediately before requesting union assistance.

It is, at its core, an agreement among working mariners that no crew should be forced to face Europa alone, unpaid, unequipped, abandoned, or without recourse.
