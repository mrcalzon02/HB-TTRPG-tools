## BIOLOGICAL AND CHEMICAL HAZARDS

Not every danger in the Europan sea has teeth.

Some organisms defend themselves through toxic, acidic, infectious, or corrosive substances. Others create hazardous environments merely by existing.

Cave growths, ballast flora, parasitic organisms, and various native life-forms may release acidic or otherwise harmful material when damaged, disturbed, or placed under stress.

This material can injure exposed personnel and damage equipment.

Acid may degrade:

- Diving suits.

- Metallic tools.

- Plastic seals.

- Weapon components.

- Storage containers.

- Electrical equipment.

- Submarine hull structures.

- The crew member unfortunate enough to be standing in it.

One common warning sign is a cloudy green substance dispersing through the water.

Among professional medical and scientific personnel, this may be described using terms such as acidic discharge, corrosive particulate cloud, toxic biological effluent, or a similarly respectable expression.

Among working submariners, it is frequently called fart gas.

The medical and scientific communities generally dislike this name.

It remains useful because an experienced submariner hearing:

“The cave is full of fart gas,”

will usually understand that the area contains a visible, harmful cloud, that breathing or protective equipment must be checked, and that prolonged exposure may injure both personnel and equipment.

Informal terminology is acceptable when it communicates danger quickly.

It should not replace a useful report.

“Fart gas in the lower tunnel, green cloud expanding toward the exit” is significantly more valuable than merely shouting “gas” and disappearing from the radio.

When corrosive or toxic material is encountered:

- Avoid unnecessary contact.

- Do not remain inside the cloud longer than required.

- Monitor suit condition.

- Check oxygen and breathing equipment.

- Withdraw if equipment begins failing.

- Warn the submarine and other team members.

- Treat exposure promptly.

- Do not cut, burn, or attack the source without understanding how it may react.

Some biological growths become more dangerous when damaged.

The solution to an unpleasant organism is not always to shoot it repeatedly.

## BALLAST FLORA AND RELATED GROWTHS

Ballast flora is particularly dangerous because it can infest the submarine itself.

An away team encountering similar growth outside the vessel should report it, especially if the organism may be carried back aboard on equipment, salvage, suits, or recovered materials.

Do not drag unidentified biological matter through the airlock without warning medical and engineering personnel.

Do not store it beside food, medicine, reactor supplies, or ballast controls.

Specimens may be valuable.

They may also attempt to colonize the vessel.

When returning with biological samples:

- Seal them in appropriate containers.

- Report what was collected.

- Identify where it was found.

- Keep the sample isolated until examined.

- Do not open it in a shared compartment.

- Do not consume it.

- Do not inject it.

- Do not introduce it into the ballast system to “see what happens.”

The submarine is not a laboratory unless the captain has specifically agreed that it is one.

Even then, it should remain a laboratory capable of reaching the next station.

## MINING OPERATIONS

Mining appears simple.

Locate a deposit. Exit the submarine. Cut the material free. Carry it back.

The basic procedure is simple.

The environment is not.

Before mining begins, determine whether the submarine can hold position safely and whether its weapons have firing coverage that will not endanger the divers.

The mining team should approach the deposit together. Security should watch the surrounding water while the miners work.

Recover the material systematically.

Do not scatter ore across the cavern floor. Do not fill every personal inventory slot and then abandon mission tools because there is no room left. Use bags, crates, or other storage whenever available.

The team should communicate when:

- The deposit has been reached.

- Mining has begun.

- Hostile movement is observed.

- Oxygen is becoming low.

- Carrying capacity has been reached.

- The deposit is exhausted.

- The team is returning.

Mining teams should also understand the materials they recover.

Certain minerals may be valuable because they can be refined into reactor fuel, ammunition, medicines, explosives, or electrical components. Others may be worth more when sold directly.

The away team does not need to conduct a full market analysis at the cave wall.

It should avoid discarding potentially useful material because its value is not immediately obvious.

When carrying capacity becomes limited, consult the engineering department or captain regarding priorities.

## WRECK AND SALVAGE OPERATIONS

Wrecks may contain cargo, tools, weapons, medicine, fuel, machinery, mission records, and survivors.

They may also contain flooding, electrical hazards, unstable reactors, armed occupants, parasites, structural collapse, hostile wildlife, and former crew members who are no longer entirely former.

Approach every wreck as an unknown environment.

Do not assume it is abandoned because no lights are visible.

Do not assume the occupants are dead because nobody answered the radio.

Do not assume the dead occupants will remain inactive.

Before entry:

- Inspect the exterior.

- Identify accessible hatches or breaches.

- Look for signs of movement.

- Check for weapon systems.

- Determine whether the wreck is stable.

- Establish a return route.

- Inform the submarine where the team is entering.

Inside the wreck, maintain formation and mark the route when necessary.

Avoid opening every door simultaneously. A sealed compartment may contain water pressure, fire, infection, hostile life, or a remarkably angry survivor.

Recover useful supplies, but remember the purpose of the mission.

An away team assigned to retrieve one cargo manifest should not keep the submarine waiting while it removes every chair, cup, and damaged coil from the wreck.

Prioritize:

1. Mission objectives.

1. Survivors.

1. Critical supplies.

1. Valuable or useful salvage.

1. Objects of uncertain value.

1. Decorative rubbish.

Do not allow greed to keep the team inside a deteriorating structure after conditions have become unsafe.

## HOSTILE PERSONNEL AND BOARDING OPERATIONS

Away teams may encounter pirates, separatists, hostile station personnel, rogue mercenaries, or other armed humans.

Human enemies may be more dangerous than wildlife because they understand doors, cover, firearms, explosives, deception, and surrender.

Maintain clear lines of fire.

Do not fire through teammates.

Do not allow the team to bunch tightly in a doorway.

Do not throw explosives into a compartment until the position of every friendly crew member is known.

During an assault, communicate movement:

- “Taking the left side.”

- “Crossing.”

- “Holding fire.”

- “One hostile behind the door.”

- “Friendly ahead.”

- “Room secure.”

- “Casualty.”

- “Withdrawing.”

A boarding operation is not improved by every person charging through the same hatch while firing over one another’s shoulders.

Security should control the pace of entry. Medical personnel should remain protected but accessible. Specialists should not advance beyond security merely because they are eager to inspect a console or container.

If the hostile force withdraws, do not pursue blindly into an unknown structure.

They may know the terrain.

You do not.

## ALIEN RUINS

Alien ruins are among the most valuable and dangerous locations an away team may encounter.

Their origin remains uncertain. Their purpose is often unclear. Their internal systems may have remained active for an unknown length of time beneath the Europan ice.

Crews have entered such ruins seeking artifacts, scientific data, rare materials, or station contracts.

Some have returned wealthy.

Some have returned altered.

Some have not returned.

Entire submarines have gone dark after disturbing mechanical entities released from alien structures. Reports describe automated guardians, energy discharges, shifting internal systems, hostile machines, unusual biological manifestations, and devices that appear inactive until a crew member approaches.

Treat every ruin as an active installation until proven otherwise.

Do not assume age means safety.

Do not assume silence means inactivity.

Do not assume a closed structure is unoccupied.

When entering:

- Maintain communication with the submarine.

- Keep track of the exit route.

- Avoid splitting the team unnecessarily.

- Watch for mechanical movement.

- Do not touch every visible device.

- Do not activate controls merely to observe the result.

- Maintain sufficient oxygen for the return journey.

- Withdraw if unfamiliar systems begin powering up.

- Report any defensive construct immediately.

Ruins should be explored slowly enough to observe danger but quickly enough that the submarine is not left exposed indefinitely.

Curiosity is part of the mission.

Survival remains the priority.
