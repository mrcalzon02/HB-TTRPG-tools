## BOARDING ACTIONS AND ASSAULTS

During an assault on an enemy vessel, hostile outpost, pirate position, wreck, ruin, or occupied compartment, the danger of friendly fire increases sharply.

Corridors are narrow. Doorways create bottlenecks. Visibility is limited. Teams frequently stack behind one another. Hostile personnel may retreat through friendly positions, and crew members may rush forward without announcing themselves.

An assault team must maintain order.

The crew member at the front controls the immediate engagement. Personnel behind them do not fire through them merely because they can see part of the enemy.

If the front member cannot safely engage, they should move, crouch behind actual cover, withdraw, or communicate that another shooter should take the angle.

The second crew member should cover an alternate direction rather than placing a weapon against the first crew member’s spine.

Explosive weapons require even greater restraint. Grenades, grenade launchers, explosive ammunition, nuclear ordnance, and volatile compounds should not be used in occupied compartments unless the threat clearly justifies the probable casualties and structural damage.

Before throwing or launching an explosive, consider:

- The blast radius.

- The confined nature of the compartment.

- Nearby crew members.

- The integrity of the hull.

- Fuel, oxygen, ammunition, or reactor systems nearby.

- Whether the explosive may rebound from a hatch or wall.

- Whether the enemy is close enough to friendly personnel that both will be caught in the detonation.

A successful assault does not leave the enemy dead, the assault team dead, and the objective compartment open to the Europan sea.

## HOSTILE BOARDERS ABOARD YOUR OWN VESSEL

When hostile boarders or creatures enter the submarine, indiscriminate fire becomes especially dangerous.

Every missed shot may strike:

- A crew member.

- A reactor control.

- A junction box.

- A fuel rod.

- Medical storage.

- Ammunition.

- A window or weakened section of hull.

- A pump required to control flooding.

- A volatile chemical container.

- An oxygen tank.

- An explosive stored nearby.

The objective is not merely to kill the intruder. The objective is to kill or contain the intruder while preserving the vessel and its crew.

Position defenders so that they fire away from occupied compartments whenever possible. Use doorways and hatches to control the enemy’s approach. Do not surround a hostile creature and then fire toward one another from opposite sides.

If two security officers are positioned on opposite sides of an enemy, neither has a safe line of fire.

One must move.

Never create a firing arrangement in which a missed shot continues directly into another crew member.

## AUTOMATIC FIRE AND SHOTGUNS

Automatic weapons create a false sense that accuracy can be replaced with volume.

It cannot.

Firing a longer burst does not make an obstructed line of fire safer. It merely increases the number of opportunities to strike a friendly.

Use controlled bursts. Stop firing when a friendly crosses the sight line. Reassess after the target moves. Do not continue holding the trigger while sweeping the weapon across a crowded compartment.

Shotguns require particular care. The user must account for the entire spread, not merely the centre of the weapon’s aim.

A friendly standing beside the target may still be struck.

A friendly crouching beneath the target may still be struck.

A friendly fighting the target at close range is almost certain to be struck.

Do not use a shotgun through a cluster of crew members and then express surprise when medical reports multiple pellet wounds.

The spread is part of the weapon.

You are responsible for all of it.

## HARPOONS, SPEARGUNS, AND HEAVY WEAPONS

Harpoons and spears may appear slower and more predictable than firearms, but the same line-of-fire rules apply.

Do not fire a harpoon through a friendly diver. Do not launch explosive harpoons into a close-quarters struggle. Do not assume a diving suit will protect a crewmate from a projectile intended for a mudraptor.

Heavy weapons demand still greater caution.

Railguns, flak cannons, coilguns, chainguns, pulse lasers, discharge coils, and depth charges can kill personnel outside the vessel as readily as the creatures attacking them.

Before firing an external weapon, confirm the location of the diving party.

If divers are near the target, communicate with them before firing. If communication has been lost, assume they may still be present until their location is confirmed.

The operator of a submarine weapon must know not only what appears on the periscope, but also who has been sent outside and where they were expected to operate.

A hostile contact beside an unaccounted-for dive team is not automatically a clear target.

## FIRE DISCIPLINE

Fire discipline means knowing when to shoot, when to stop, and when not to begin.

A disciplined shooter:

- Keeps the weapon pointed away from friendly personnel.

- Keeps their finger off the trigger until prepared to fire.

- Identifies the target before engaging.

- Checks the foreground and background.

- Stops firing when a friendly enters the line of fire.

- Uses controlled fire rather than panic.

- Repositions when the firing angle is unsafe.

- Warns others before using explosives or heavy weapons.

- Reports accidental friendly fire immediately.

- Does not conceal a gunshot wound they caused.

- Assists the injured once the threat is controlled.

If you accidentally shoot a crew member, report it.

State who was hit, where they were hit, and whether the threat remains active.

For example:

- “Friendly hit in engineering. One GSW to the torso. Medic required.”

- “Accidental discharge near the airlock. Diver wounded.”

- “Cease fire. Security is in the line.”

- “Shotgun pellets struck the mechanic. Threat contained.”

Do not continue firing while pretending nothing happened. Do not blame the casualty before medical personnel have even arrived. Do not allow embarrassment to delay treatment.

A concealed mistake becomes negligence.

An immediately reported mistake can still be managed.

## FINAL RULE OF THE FIRING LINE

The officer, diver, or crew member holding the weapon is responsible for every projectile leaving it.

The enemy does not excuse the shot.

Confusion does not excuse the shot.

Poor visibility does not excuse the shot.

The urgency of the battle does not excuse the shot.

If the line of fire is occupied by a friendly, do not fire.

If you are uncertain whether the line of fire is occupied, do not fire.

If you believe you can probably shoot over, under, around, or past a friendly, do not fire.

Move.

Wait.

Communicate.

Change weapons.

Change angles.

Help in another way.

Your crewmate already has enough to contend with. They do not need to survive claws, teeth, bullets, pressure, and the darkness of Europa only to be killed by the person standing behind them.

## COMMUNICATION ABOARD SHIP

A submarine survives on accurate information delivered at the proper time.

The crew does not need a continuous account of every thought passing through your mind. It does need to know that the ballast tank has breached, the reactor is overheating, a mudraptor has entered engineering, the medic is incapacitated, or a diving party has not returned.

Mission-critical communication should be concise.

State the location, the problem, and what assistance is required.

Examples:

- “Flooding in aft ballast. One mechanic responding.”

- “Fire in engineering. Electrical panel involved.”

- “Two crawlers on the upper hull.”

- “Medic required in the reactor compartment.”

- “Dive team returning with one casualty.”

- “Railgun ammunition low.”

- “Unknown contact approaching from below.”

Avoid speaking over the captain during navigation, over security during a boarding action, or over a medic coordinating casualty treatment. During an emergency, unnecessary radio traffic can conceal the one report that matters.

This does not mean crew members are forbidden from speaking, making suggestions, or maintaining morale. A silent vessel can become as dangerous as a chaotic one. Conversation is welcome when circumstances permit.

The important distinction is between communication and interference.

Report what the crew needs to know. Allow others to answer. Keep arguments off the main channel. Do not demand the captain’s immediate attention for personal inconvenience while the vessel is sinking.

You are part of the crew, not the sole subject of the voyage.

## THE MEDICAL OFFICER’S CHARGE

The medical officer’s duty is to preserve the health, mobility, and survival of the crew.

It is not to distribute narcotics to anyone who wanders into the infirmary announcing, “I hurt.”

It is not to empty every ampoule of morphine, fentanyl, antibiotics, anti-radiation medication, combat stimulants, and experimental compounds into the nearest crew member merely because the vessel has encountered mild turbulence.

It is certainly not to become the dealer.

The title of medical officer carries considerable authority aboard a submarine because the crew must trust that the person standing over them with a syringe has some understanding of what is inside it. On Europa, that trust is frequently granted long before the medical officer has acquired anything resembling a terrestrial medical licence.

Many submarine medics are former field orderlies, emergency technicians, pharmaceutical workers, research assistants, military corpsmen, self-taught practitioners, or people who were handed a medical uniform because they were the only applicant who could distinguish blood from hydraulic fluid.

Formal licensing may therefore be uncommon.

Basic competence is not optional.

A crew member who agrees to serve as medical officer is expected to understand the vessel’s medical systems, recognize common injuries, use diagnostic equipment, administer appropriate treatment, avoid preventable overdoses, preserve medical supplies, and establish priorities when several casualties require attention at once.

A medic does not need to cure every injury instantly.

They do need to avoid becoming the leading cause of death aboard the vessel.
