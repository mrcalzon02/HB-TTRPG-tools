## ENGINEERING RESPONSIBILITIES

Engineering should maintain the oxygen generator in good repair and understand how its operation affects both power demand and acoustic output.

Responsibilities include:

- Repairing generator damage.

- Monitoring its electrical load.

- Checking atmospheric circulation.

- Maintaining pumps and associated machinery.

- Confirming ventilation into occupied compartments.

- Reporting abnormal noise or vibration.

- Assisting the captain with atmospheric estimates.

- Restoring operation promptly after silent running.

- Determining whether the generator is louder than normal because something has begun coming apart inside it.

An oxygen generator becoming unusually loud may indicate mechanical wear, damaged components, poor mounting, failing pumps, or electrical instability.

Do not dismiss every new grinding noise as part of its charming personality.

A badly maintained generator may become louder, less efficient, and more likely to fail precisely when the crew needs it most.

## MEDICAL RESPONSIBILITIES

Medical personnel should monitor the crew during prolonged oxygen shutdowns.

Priority should be given to:

- Existing casualties.

- Crew members with blood loss.

- Unconscious personnel.

- Those exposed to smoke.

- Divers returning with depleted oxygen.

- Personnel performing heavy physical work.

- Anyone already experiencing respiratory distress.

A crew member with severe blood loss may tolerate reduced oxygen poorly because the blood remaining in their body is already less capable of supplying their tissues.

Restoring breathable atmosphere does not automatically correct every effect of prolonged hypoxia.

Medical should remain prepared to provide oxygen and treatment after the generator resumes operation.

## THE LIMITS OF SILENCE

Turning off the oxygen generator does not make the submarine invisible.

The vessel may still be detected through:

- Engine noise.

- Pump operation.

- Reactor activity.

- Electrical systems.

- Active sonar.

- Weapon discharge.

- Hull vibration.

- Heat.

- Movement.

- External lights.

- Crew activity.

- The enormous creature already chewing on the engine compartment.

Silent running reduces detection.

It does not grant immunity.

The captain must still navigate carefully, monitor contacts, and decide whether concealment remains useful.

Do not remain oxygen-starved for ten minutes after the threat has clearly begun attacking.

At that point, secrecy has concluded.

## FINAL RULE OF OXYGEN SILENCE

The oxygen generator is one of the submarine’s most vital machines.

It is also one of its loudest continuous advertisements.

Depowering it can help a vessel disappear into Europa’s black water.

It can also turn the submarine into a sealed container full of increasingly confused people.

Use oxygen silence deliberately.

Inform the crew.

Monitor the atmosphere.

Know the threat.

Know the time.

Know when to turn the machine back on.

The purpose of silent running is to avoid being eaten.

Achieving perfect acoustic concealment only to suffocate the entire crew is technically successful silence, but captains are advised not to include it in the mission report.

## THE CAPTAIN’S CHARGE

Command, Navigation, Crew Welfare, and the Ancient Maritime Art of Pretending Everything Is Under Control

The captain’s first duty is to keep the crew alive.

The captain’s second duty is to maintain forward momentum.

The captain’s third duty is to convince everyone aboard that the first two duties remain achievable, even when the reactor is complaining, engineering is waist-deep in water, security has misplaced a shotgun, medical has begun asking whether anyone knows where the blood went, and the sonar display contains a contact large enough to qualify for its own postal district.

A captain is not merely the person sitting at the helm.

They are navigator, coordinator, employer, quartermaster, negotiator, disciplinarian, mission planner, emergency dispatcher, and—during particularly difficult voyages—the only person aboard still pretending to have read the instruction manual.

The captain does not personally repair every junction box, treat every casualty, reload every weapon, or count every fuel rod.

They are responsible for knowing who is supposed to be doing those things, whether those people are still alive, and why none of them have answered the radio.

Command is not the art of doing everything yourself.

It is the art of ensuring everything necessary is being done by someone, somewhere, preferably before it catches fire.

## I. THE FUNDAMENTAL RESPONSIBILITY OF COMMAND

A captain must balance survival against progress.

A submarine that never leaves port is safe, but it earns nothing and eventually becomes an unusually expensive apartment.

A submarine driven from mission to mission without pause may earn quickly, but it will eventually exhaust its fuel, ammunition, medicine, engineering supplies, crew loyalty, or all five at once.

The captain must maintain momentum without spending the vessel’s future to accelerate its present.

At any given moment, command should have a reasonable understanding of:

- The current mission.

- The next mission.

- The route between them.

- The condition of the hull.

- The state of the reactor.

- Available fuel.

- Remaining ammunition.

- Medical reserves.

- Engineering materials.

- Oxygen and welding fuel.

- The condition of pumps, engines, and ballast systems.

- The position of hostile contacts.

- The location of away teams.

- The location of the crew members who were specifically told not to join the away team.

- The amount of operating time remaining before something essential runs out.

The captain must think beyond the next few minutes.

A submarine may presently be intact while possessing insufficient fuel to reach the next station. It may have ammunition but no medicine, medicine but no ammunition, or plenty of both but no mechanic capable of keeping the sea on the correct side of the hull.

A good captain asks:

“Can we continue?”

A better captain also asks:

“Can we continue and still have enough fuel to regret the decision later?”

## II. KNOW YOUR DEPARTMENT HEADS

Every major function aboard the submarine should have a responsible officer or senior hand.

Depending upon the size of the vessel, this may include:

- Chief engineer.

- Senior mechanic.

- Medical officer.

- Security chief.

- Gunnery officer.

- Quartermaster or logistics officer.

- Away-team leader.

- Executive officer or relief navigator.

On a small submarine, one person may hold several of these responsibilities.

This is efficient until that person is eaten.

The captain should know:

- Who is operating the reactor.

- Who is repairing the hull.

- Who is responsible for medical.

- Who is coordinating security.

- Who is manning the guns.

- Who is outside.

- Who is unconscious.

- Who is detained.

- Who is missing.

- Who has command if the captain is rendered unable to continue.

Undefined responsibility produces a familiar type of emergency in which four people repair the same pump while nobody notices the reactor compartment filling with water.

Delegate clearly.

Do not say:

“Someone should probably deal with that.”

Say:

- “Chief engineer, stabilize the reactor.”

- “Mechanics, repair aft ballast.”

- “Security, protect engineering.”

- “Medical, prepare for casualties.”

- “Gunnery, cover the lower approach.”

- “Away team, return immediately.”

- “Assistant, stop carrying that chair and bring welding fuel.”

The difference between a suggestion and an order is often several tonnes of water.

## III. KEEP A ROSTER OR BEGIN COLLECTING GHOSTS

The captain must know who is aboard.

This sounds simple until departure, when someone asks where the medical officer is and receives six different answers:

- “Medical.”

- “The station bar.”

- “The wreck.”

- “Unconscious.”

- “I thought you had them.”

- “There was a medical officer?”

Crew members may be:

- At their stations.

- Moving between compartments.

- Outside the submarine.

- Aboard a shuttle.

- Inside a wreck or ruin.

- On station leave.

- Injured.

- Detained.

- Dead.

- Missing under circumstances that will later become a disciplinary hearing.

Before leaving a station, wreck, cave, ruin, or mission site, account for the crew.

Use names, departments, or team reports.

Do not accept “everyone is probably aboard” as a final accounting.

Probability is useful in mathematics.

It is less useful when the missing person is carrying the only plasma cutter.

## IV. KNOW WHO IS OUTSIDE THE HULL

The moment a crew member leaves the submarine, the captain’s freedom to manoeuvre becomes limited.

Divers may be mining beside the hull, exploring caves, attached to wreckage, crossing weapon arcs, or attempting to recover something described only as “very valuable” and “probably safe.”

The captain should know:

- How many people are outside.

- Who leads them.

- Their objective.

- Their expected duration.

- Their approximate position.

- Whether they are near external weapon arcs.

- Whether the submarine can move without dragging them into terrain.

- Whether hostile contacts are approaching.

Before moving, warn the away team.

Before firing heavy weapons, locate the away team.

Before departing, count the away team.

A captain who forgets divers does not merely lose personnel.

They establish a reputation.

Reputation travels quickly between stations, particularly when the story begins with:

“They left four miners behind because the captain thought the green lights meant everyone was aboard.”
