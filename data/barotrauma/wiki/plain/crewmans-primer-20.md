## VII. THE SAVILLE SACRIFICE

No history of Standard Union 208 is complete without the crew of the Saville.

The Saville was a medium cargo submarine operating along a frontier supply route serving several mining stations and one heavily fortified commercial enclave controlled by the Harrow Deep Combine.

The Combine was not legally recognized as a government.

It possessed weapons, territory, debt contracts, prisons, security personnel, and a tax office.

On Europa, this distinction is often considered academic.

The Saville carried food, replacement oxygen membranes, reactor components, medical supplies, and pressure-seal materials.

Its crew operated under a Union 208 contract.

When the submarine reached the Combine’s territory, cartel representatives ordered the captain to accept a revised contract.

The revised terms reduced crew pay, transferred all salvage rights to the Combine, permitted indefinite extension of the voyage, and allowed armed supervisors to discipline crew members without appeal.

The captain refused.

The crew supported the refusal.

The Combine impounded the vessel and detained twelve members of the crew.

Union negotiators demanded their release.

The Combine responded that union law did not apply within its territory.

The union then issued what became known as the Saville Notice.

No Union 208 vessel would deliver cargo to the enclave.

No union dockworker would service its submarines.

No affiliated mechanic would certify its pressure systems.

No union medical officer would contract with Combine transports.

No merchant protected by union convoy agreements would carry restricted supplies into its territory.

The Combine believed it could replace the union.

It hired independent captains.

Several accepted payment, collected cargo, and declined to complete delivery after learning the route had lost rescue coverage.

Others discovered that no qualified dockworkers would load their vessels.

Nonunion repair crews attempted to maintain the enclave’s submarines and produced an impressive series of electrical fires.

Food became scarce.

Reactor parts failed.

Medical supplies dwindled.

The enclave’s station pumps began operating without reliable replacement components.

The Combine retaliated against the detained Saville crew.

Several were beaten.

Several were subjected to forced labor.

Four died in custody.

The surviving crew members continued refusing the revised contract.

They were offered individual release in exchange for renouncing the union and accepting personal employment under the Combine.

None accepted.

After thirty-seven days, the enclave’s outer atmosphere-processing array suffered a major failure.

The Combine requested emergency parts.

Union 208 replied that humanitarian oxygen support would be delivered for civilians, but no commercial service would resume until the surviving crew were released, the bodies of the dead were returned, and the original contracts were honored.

The Combine yielded.

The surviving Saville crew left under union escort.

The dead were carried aboard four submarines, each escorted by another vessel with its lights extinguished.

Every union vessel along the route sounded one controlled sonar pulse as the convoy passed.

The pulse later became part of the annual Saville observance.

The four dead became known as the Saville Crewmen, though later memorials often honor all twelve detainees under the same title.

Their sacrifice established a principle now understood throughout Europa:

An attack upon a union crew may be answered not by gunfire, but by absence.

No food.

No fuel.

No medicine.

No parts.

No crews.

No deliveries.

The Saville settlement was recognized not only by Coalition authorities and legitimate station governments but by cartels, pirate enclaves, separatist commands, smugglers, and private security organizations.

They did not all develop respect for labor rights.

They developed respect for logistics.

That has proven sufficient.

## VIII. THE SAVILLE CLAUSE

Modern Union 208 agreements contain a provision commonly called the Saville Clause.

It states that deliberate detention, torture, disappearance, execution, forced labor, or coercive contract alteration affecting a protected crew may trigger collective suspension of services.

The scope of such a suspension may include:

- Cargo refusal.

- Docking refusal.

- Repair refusal.

- Crew refusal.

- Fuel embargo.

- Suspension of convoy protection.

- Withdrawal of rescue guarantees.

- Refusal to recognize local employment contracts.

- Public listing of the offending station or organization.

- Free passage for civilians fleeing the affected territory.

- Humanitarian deliveries administered independently of the offending authority.

The union distinguishes between pressure upon an abusive authority and harm to ordinary station residents.

Food and oxygen may still be provided directly to civilians.

Medical evacuations may continue.

Emergency rescue may be rendered.

The purpose of a Saville action is not to kill a station.

It is to demonstrate how quickly a station’s leadership can lose control when the labor supporting it withdraws cooperation.

This distinction is important.

It is also frequently inconvenient for station leadership, which is the point.

## IX. WHY CARTELS RECOGNIZE UNION 208

Criminal organizations do not generally recognize laws because they are impressed by their moral clarity.

They recognize consequences.

A cartel may ignore a court ruling.

It may bribe an inspector.

It may assassinate an administrator.

It may threaten a captain.

It cannot easily manufacture experienced submariners, reactor technicians, pressure-hull welders, surgeons, and cargo pilots on demand.

Criminal stations are often more dependent upon maritime labor than lawful ones.

They cannot openly purchase certain goods.

They rely upon smugglers.

They need pilots familiar with hidden routes.

They require mechanics willing to work on modified vessels.

They require medical officers who ask few questions.

They require merchants willing to enter dangerous territory.

A cartel that violates Union 208 rules may discover that all of these people have independently decided to be elsewhere.

Even pirates observe certain union protections.

They may seize cargo.

They may demand ransom.

They may rob a vessel.

But many will release ordinary crew members, honor medical neutrality, and avoid interfering with union relief shipments.

This restraint should not be mistaken for kindness.

It is informed self-preservation.

A pirate captain may steal ten crates of ammunition.

They will hesitate before creating a labor dispute that prevents every friendly station from selling them food.

## X. STANDARD EMPLOYMENT CONTRACT LAW

Union 208 agreements form the basis of common submarine employment law across much of Europa.

Local jurisdictions may vary, but a valid maritime employment contract is generally expected to identify:

- The employer.

- The captain.

- The vessel.

- The crew member.

- The assigned profession.

- The expected duties.

- The base wage.

- Mission shares.

- Salvage rights.

- Hazard compensation.

- Contract duration.

- Expected route.

- Conditions for extension.

- Medical provisions.

- Death benefits.

- Disciplinary procedures.

- Grounds for termination.

- Conditions for resignation.

- The responsible jurisdiction.

- The process for dispute resolution.

A contract that omits all meaningful obligations while granting the employer unlimited authority is not considered “flexible.”

It is considered suspect.

The fact that a crew member signed a document does not automatically make every term enforceable.

Union arbiters routinely reject provisions that involve:

- Unpaid indefinite service.

- Debt inheritance.

- Forced medical experimentation.

- Unrestricted corporal punishment.

- Summary execution for minor violations.

- Confiscation of all personal salary.

- Forced abandonment.

- Waiver of rescue rights.

- Retroactive wage reduction.

- Unlimited extension without compensation.

- Ownership of a crew member’s body after death.

- Mandatory participation in clown-related research.

The last provision was added after an arbitration panel determined that existing language was somehow not specific enough.

## XI. THE RIGHT TO CLEAR TERMS

Every mariner has the right to understand the essential conditions of employment.

A captain or employer should state:

- What the vessel is doing.

- Where it is going.

- What the crew member will be paid.

- What dangers are reasonably expected.

- How mission rewards will be divided.

- What equipment will be provided.

- What conduct may result in discipline.

- When the contract ends.

No employer may rely upon deliberately obscure language to conceal dangerous duties.

A salvage contract is not automatically an assault contract.

A cargo voyage is not automatically a ruin expedition.

A station-transfer agreement is not automatically consent to remain aboard for six additional missions.

Operations may change unexpectedly.

Europa is not known for respecting paperwork.

When conditions materially change, compensation and consent may need to be renegotiated.

## XII. THE RIGHT TO FAIR COMPENSATION

Submariners are entitled to the compensation agreed upon before departure.

This may include:

- Base wages.

- Mission completion pay.

- Salvage shares.

- Hazard bonuses.

- Overtime or extended-voyage pay.

- Specialist pay.

- Death or injury benefits.

- Recruitment bonuses.

- Retention bonuses.

- Compensation for assuming additional duties.

- Emergency rescue awards.

Wages may not be reduced after the work has been completed merely because the voyage proved less profitable than expected.

The crew does not become an involuntary investor whenever the captain makes a poor commercial decision.

Likewise, a captain may not promise extraordinary shares during an emergency and later dismiss the promise as something said under stress.

Most important statements aboard a submarine are made under stress.

That does not make them imaginary.
