## BECOMING DANGEROUSLY COMPETENT

A capable assistant should gradually learn several basic skills.

These may include:

- Repairing small hull breaches.

- Operating pumps.

- Replacing oxygen tanks.

- Extinguishing fires.

- Applying bandages.

- Moving unconscious crew members.

- Reloading common weapons.

- Repairing simple mechanical or electrical damage.

- Using diving equipment.

- Recognizing alarms.

- Reporting emergencies properly.

- Fabricating routine supplies.

- Carrying salvage without wandering into another cave.

- Holding the helm steady if every qualified officer has become temporarily horizontal.

The objective is not to replace specialists.

It is to keep the problem from worsening until the specialist arrives.

A basic repair performed promptly may preserve a system.

A bandage may keep someone alive.

A pump switched on at the correct moment may save a compartment.

A door closed during flooding may save half the vessel.

The assistant’s greatest victories often appear very small.

They are only small because they prevented the larger disaster.

## ASSISTANTS DURING FLOODING

Flooding is where a useful assistant earns the undying affection of mechanical engineering, or at least a temporary reduction in the amount of swearing directed at them.

When water enters the submarine:

- Locate the breach.

- Inform the crew.

- Close doors to slow the spread.

- Put on appropriate equipment.

- Retrieve a welding tool.

- Assist the mechanic.

- Operate or repair the pump.

- Watch for creatures entering through the opening.

- Avoid sealing yourself on the wrong side of the door.

- Avoid sealing the mechanic on the wrong side of the door.

- Avoid declaring the compartment secure while the water reaches your helmet.

If the breach is beyond your ability, report it and support the person repairing it.

Do not continue welding a tiny crack in an empty storage room while the reactor compartment becomes an indoor sea.

Priorities matter.

The ocean will patiently wait for you to choose incorrectly.

## ASSISTANTS DURING COMBAT

In combat, assistants may:

- Reload guns.

- Move ammunition.

- Carry medical supplies.

- Transport casualties.

- Seal damaged compartments.

- Repair pumps.

- Watch uncovered approaches.

- Support security.

- Operate a weapon if trained and assigned.

- Refrain from standing in the line of fire.

Do not seize the largest weapon aboard because the sound of combat has inspired you.

Do not fire through crew members.

Do not open the armory and distribute weapons to everyone with pockets.

Do not approach security during an active firefight by silently running toward them while holding an unannounced firearm.

If you must cross a firing line, communicate.

If you are carrying ammunition, say so.

If you see a hostile creature, report its location rather than yelling “THING” over the radio.

Europa contains a very large number of things.

Be specific.

## ASSISTANTS IN MEDICAL

A general crewman does not need to become a doctor to help medical personnel.

Useful medical support includes:

- Carrying casualties.

- Applying simple bandages.

- Retrieving blood packs.

- Bringing oxygen.

- Clearing the treatment area.

- Following the doctor’s instructions.

- Preventing untreated patients from wandering away.

- Informing medical what caused the injury.

- Refraining from administering every drug in the cabinet because the patient “looked poorly.”

The phrase “I found them like this” is less useful than:

“They were bitten by a mudraptor, lost blood, and were unconscious for approximately thirty seconds.”

Do not perform experimental surgery.

Do not prescribe morphine because you like the patient.

Do not prescribe morphine because you dislike the patient.

Do not prescribe morphine because the ampoule is already in your hand.

The assistant helps the doctor work.

The assistant does not establish a competing medical practice in the corridor.

## ASSISTANTS IN ENGINEERING

Engineering departments adore a competent assistant for the same reason exhausted parents adore anyone willing to carry groceries.

Useful engineering support includes:

- Delivering components.

- Refilling welding tanks.

- Moving fuel rods.

- Sorting fabrication materials.

- Repairing secondary junction boxes.

- Watching pumps.

- Relaying reactor status.

- Helping drain compartments.

- Recovering tools.

- Keeping other crew members away from controls they do not understand.

Do not change reactor settings without instruction.

Do not add fuel rods because “more fuel means more power.”

Do not rewire systems because you have discovered the wiring tool.

Do not unplug the oxygen generator during an ordinary voyage to test the silent-running chapter.

The engineer may teach you these systems over time.

Until then, your most valuable electrical skill may be locating the qualified electrical engineer.

## ASSISTANTS ON AWAY MISSIONS

Away teams benefit enormously from a capable assistant.

Specialists carry mission equipment.

Security carries weapons.

Medical carries treatment.

The assistant carries everything else and somehow still has room for the mineral everyone claims is valuable.

An assistant may serve as:

- Porter.

- Lookout.

- Secondary miner.

- Radio operator.

- Rear guard.

- Casualty carrier.

- Oxygen custodian.

- Salvage handler.

- Spare pair of hands.

- Person repeatedly reminding the team that the submarine has recalled them.

Do not wander off.

Do not touch artifacts without permission.

Do not collect every object in a wreck simply because it can be picked up.

Do not announce that you have found something “very important” and then return carrying a chair.

Unless the submarine genuinely needs a chair.

In that case, the quartermaster may be forced to apologize.

## THE ASSISTANT AND THE CLOWN QUESTION

At some point, an assistant may encounter clown clothing.

This may arrive through personal choice, local tradition, union ritual, station surplus, or an unknown process by which clown masks spontaneously appear wherever assistants gather.

Wearing clown attire does not automatically make an assistant incompetent.

It does, however, increase the burden of proof.

A clown carrying ammunition to the correct gun is useful.

A clown applying first aid is useful.

A clown welding the hull is useful, if mildly unsettling.

A clown honking repeatedly beside the captain during a docking maneuver is conducting an experiment in security response times.

Morale is valuable.

Timing is more valuable.

Learn the distinction between relieving tension and creating a new reason for tension.

## WHEN ALL SPECIALIST ROLES ARE TAKEN

Sometimes you join a crew and discover that every specialist position already has someone assigned.

Good.

This means the vessel may actually possess enough qualified personnel to survive.

It does not mean you have no role.

The crew does not become complete merely because every uniform has been filled.

A submarine with one person in each specialty may still have:

- One engineer handling twenty junction boxes.

- One mechanic responsible for the entire hull.

- One doctor treating several casualties.

- One security officer defending multiple decks.

- One captain trying to navigate while everyone speaks at once.

The assistant gives these people breathing room.

You are not competing with the specialist.

You are expanding what the specialist can accomplish.

A second set of hands is not redundant when the first set is holding a welding tool and trying to keep the sea outside.

## FINDING YOUR FUTURE SPECIALTY

Many excellent specialists begin as assistants.

The assistant role lets you observe the entire vessel before deciding where your aptitudes belong.

You may discover that you enjoy:

- Repairing machinery.

- Managing power.

- Treating injuries.

- Defending the crew.

- Navigating.

- Mining.

- Fabrication.

- Logistics.

- Away-team operations.

- Organizing everyone else while insisting you have no desire to become captain.

Take time to learn.

You do not need to select a specialist career during your first voyage.

Practice under supervision.

Ask questions.

Build familiarity.

Learn which alarms require immediate action and which alarms merely indicate that someone has turned off the oxygen generator intentionally.

When you feel prepared, speak with the relevant department head.

A mechanic will appreciate an assistant who already knows the pumps.

An engineer will appreciate one who understands junction boxes.

A doctor will appreciate one who knows that painkillers are not a universal cure.

Security will appreciate one who has mastered the advanced doctrine of not shooting through teammates.

## THE VETERAN ASSISTANT

Some assistants never transfer into a specialist position.

This is not because they failed to develop.

It is because they developed in every direction.

A veteran assistant may know:

- Enough engineering to stabilize the reactor.

- Enough mechanics to repair the hull.

- Enough medicine to preserve a casualty.

- Enough security procedure to defend a compartment.

- Enough navigation to hold depth.

- Enough logistics to know why every department is lying about its supply consumption.

They may not be the best person aboard at any individual profession.

They may be the second-best person aboard at five of them.

That makes them exceptionally valuable.

When a specialist is incapacitated, the veteran assistant fills the gap.

When the vessel is short-handed, they move where needed.

When several departments fail at once, they understand enough of each to keep the submarine alive until the crisis becomes organized.

The veteran assistant is not the crew member who has failed to specialize.

They are the crew member who has turned adaptability into a profession.
