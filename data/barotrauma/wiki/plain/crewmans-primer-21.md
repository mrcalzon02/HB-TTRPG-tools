## XIII. HAZARD PAY

Hazard is an ordinary feature of submarine work.

Extraordinary hazard is a separate matter.

Hazard compensation may be required for:

- Entering known hostile nests.

- Boarding enemy vessels.

- Exploring active alien ruins.

- Handling unstable artifacts.

- Working in radiation.

- Clearing severe biological contamination.

- Operating beyond agreed safe routes.

- Continuing after major loss of medical capability.

- Performing duties outside the original contract.

- Entering waters subject to active military conflict.

- Working with defective safety equipment.

- Recovering bodies from highly dangerous environments.

The captain should not describe every danger as ordinary merely because danger is common.

There is a meaningful difference between routine submarine risk and being ordered to carry a heat-emitting alien device through a compartment full of volatile ammunition.

Union arbiters are familiar with the distinction.

## XIV. THE RIGHT TO NECESSARY EQUIPMENT

A crew member must be provided with equipment reasonably required to perform assigned duties.

This includes, where applicable:

- Functional diving suits.

- Oxygen.

- Repair tools.

- Medical supplies.

- Protective clothing.

- Weapons.

- Ammunition.

- Radiation protection.

- Secure communications.

- Lighting.

- Access to appropriate workspaces.

- Safe storage.

- Emergency escape equipment.

An employer may require reasonable care of issued equipment.

They may charge for deliberate theft or reckless destruction under an agreed procedure.

They may not require a miner to enter open water without oxygen and later classify the resulting death as a performance issue.

Equipment must be functional, not merely present.

A diving suit with a missing seal is not protective equipment.

It is a burial garment with sleeves.

## XV. THE RIGHT TO FOOD, WATER, AIR, AND HABITABLE CONDITIONS

Every crew member is entitled to the basic materials required for life.

A vessel must maintain, within reasonable operational limits:

- Breathable atmosphere.

- Potable water or equivalent supply.

- Adequate food.

- Sanitary facilities.

- Safe sleeping arrangements when voyages require extended rest.

- Protection from unnecessary radiation.

- Reasonable temperature control.

- Access to emergency medical care.

The union recognizes that combat and disaster may temporarily make conditions intolerable.

It does not recognize deliberate deprivation as ordinary management.

Food may not be withheld as punishment.

Oxygen may not be restricted to encourage productivity.

Medical treatment may not be conditioned upon obedience unrelated to the treatment.

A captain attempting to motivate the crew by disabling the oxygen generator will generally obtain increased movement.

Most of it will be toward the captain.

## XVI. THE RIGHT TO MEDICAL CARE

Injured or ill crew members are entitled to timely treatment consistent with available resources.

Medical personnel must prioritize according to clinical need, not rank, wealth, popularity, or ability to threaten the doctor.

The captain is entitled to command consideration where survival of the vessel genuinely depends upon their recovery.

This does not mean a captain with a bruised wrist takes priority over an engineer suffering catastrophic blood loss.

Union medical law also recognizes:

- Confidential handling of personal medical information.

- Protection from involuntary experimentation.

- The right to refuse nonemergency treatment.

- The duty to report contagious or vessel-threatening conditions.

- Access to pain treatment when medically justified.

- Protection against punitive withholding of medication.

- Compensation for occupational injury.

Medical treatment is not a privilege granted for good behavior.

Neither is the medical cabinet a public narcotics buffet.

The distinction is addressed elsewhere in this guide and should not require another incident involving Jerry from engineering.

## XVII. THE RIGHT TO REFUSE MANIFESTLY SUICIDAL ORDERS

A crew member may refuse an order when:

- The order presents an immediate and extreme danger.

- The danger is not reasonably necessary to preserve the vessel or crew.

- The crew member lacks required equipment or training.

- The order serves no legitimate operational purpose.

- Compliance would violate a recognized humanitarian protection.

- The order requires deliberate criminal harm unrelated to survival.

This right is narrow.

It does not permit refusal of every unpleasant, dangerous, or inconvenient task.

Submarine work is inherently dangerous.

A mechanic may still be ordered to repair a breach during an attack.

A security officer may still be ordered to confront hostile boarders.

A medic may still be required to enter a damaged compartment to reach casualties.

The relevant question is whether the danger is necessary, proportionate, and connected to preserving the vessel or completing agreed work.

“Go outside without a suit because I am curious what happens” is not a lawful order.

“Repair the external docking mechanism before we are crushed against the station” may be.

## XVIII. THE RIGHT OF RETURN

Crew members sent outside the submarine have a right to reasonable recovery.

The captain must not knowingly abandon an away team while recovery remains practical and does not create an overwhelming danger to the vessel.

This does not require a captain to sacrifice the entire submarine for one missing diver.

It requires:

- Maintaining an accurate roster.

- Establishing recall procedures.

- Allowing reasonable return time.

- Attempting communication.

- Considering rescue.

- Recording why departure became necessary.

- Reporting abandoned personnel at the earliest safe opportunity.

Deliberately leaving a disliked crew member outside is not command discretion.

It is attempted murder with navigational elements.

## XIX. THE RIGHT TO RESCUE

Union 208 vessels are expected to respond to distress calls when reasonably capable.

The duty is affected by:

- Distance.

- Fuel.

- Current mission.

- Crew condition.

- Threat level.

- Vessel capability.

- Whether responding would create a second casualty.

A submarine need not destroy itself attempting an impossible rescue.

It should not ignore a viable rescue merely because no payment has been guaranteed.

Union rescue obligations helped create the under-ice routes as a functioning network rather than a collection of isolated commercial risks.

Mariners aid mariners because Europa is too large, too dark, and too hostile for anyone to assume another vessel will answer.

Rescue compensation may be negotiated afterward.

Breathing should begin first.

## XX. THE RIGHT TO SHORE LEAVE AND REST

Crew members are entitled to reasonable rest and shore leave when operational circumstances permit.

This does not mean every station call must become a three-day festival.

It means crews cannot be expected to operate indefinitely without recovery.

Fatigued workers:

- Make mistakes.

- Miss threats.

- Mismanage reactors.

- Misdiagnose patients.

- Fire through teammates.

- Forget fuel.

- Fall asleep at the helm.

- Become increasingly receptive to mutiny as a form of recreation.

The captain may recall personnel for departure or emergency duty.

Repeated denial of rest without necessity may trigger grievance, compensation claims, or refusal of additional missions.

A crew member who has been awake for thirty hours is not displaying admirable dedication.

They are an equipment failure that has learned to speak.

## XXI. THE RIGHT TO REPRESENTATION

A union crew may select or recognize a representative.

The representative may:

- Present grievances.

- Review contract terms.

- Participate in disciplinary proceedings.

- Negotiate compensation.

- Communicate with station chapters.

- Request arbitration.

- Document unsafe conditions.

- Coordinate collective action.

- Ensure benefits reach injured workers or dependents.

The representative is not a second captain.

They do not direct navigation or countermand emergency orders merely because they possess a union badge.

The captain commands the vessel.

The representative protects the agreement under which the crew serves.

Competent captains understand that these roles can coexist.

Incompetent captains generally learn through arbitration.

## XXII. THE RIGHT TO GRIEVANCE

Crew members may raise concerns about:

- Unpaid wages.

- Unsafe equipment.

- Discriminatory treatment.

- Contract violations.

- Improper punishment.

- Withheld medical care.

- Abandonment.

- Unfair salvage distribution.

- Coercive extension of service.

- Retaliation.

- Inadequate food or oxygen.

- Unlawful orders.

- Misrepresentation during recruitment.

A grievance should be reported through the vessel’s established process when possible.

This may involve:

- Reporting to the department head.

- Reporting to the captain.

- Consulting the union representative.

- Recording the complaint.

- Requesting review at the next station.

- Seeking emergency arbitration when delay would cause serious harm.

The union encourages resolution aboard the vessel when practical.

It does not require workers to continue using an internal process controlled entirely by the person accused of the violation.
