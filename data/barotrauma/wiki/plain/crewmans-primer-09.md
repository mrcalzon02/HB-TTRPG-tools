## MEDICAL REPORTS

The medical officer must inventory the infirmary before departure.

This includes more than glancing into one cabinet and concluding that several colorful ampoules remain.

Report shortages of:

- Morphine and other necessary analgesics.

- Bandages and wound-treatment supplies.

- Blood packs and replacement fluids.

- Antidotes.

- Antibiotics.

- Anti-radiation medicine.

- Emergency stimulants.

- Sedatives.

- Treatment for burns and environmental injuries.

- Supplies needed for away-team medical kits.

- Any specialist medicine required by the anticipated mission.

The captain cannot purchase what the medical officer does not report.

If the submarine leaves a well-stocked station and the medic later announces that no morphine remains aboard, the captain is entitled to ask several pointed questions.

The vessel was just in port.

The merchants had medical supplies.

The ship possessed funds.

The opportunity existed.

Why was nothing said?

A shortage concealed through inattention is functionally little different from a shortage created through waste. In both cases, a wounded crew member may suffer because the medical department failed to perform a basic responsibility.

Medical personnel should also explain unusual levels of consumption.

If the infirmary began the last voyage with a full narcotics cabinet and returned with nothing despite suffering no serious casualties, “Jerry from engineering encouraged everyone to see how much morphine it would take to kill them” will not improve the captain’s disposition.

Inventory is not merely counting.

It is accountability.

## ENGINEERING AND MECHANICAL REPORTS

Engineers and mechanics should inspect the submarine’s operational reserves before departure.

Critical concerns include:

- Reactor fuel rods.

- Welding fuel.

- Oxygen tanks.

- Diving-suit oxygen supplies.

- Portable batteries.

- Electrical components.

- Mechanical repair materials.

- Replacement tools.

- Fabrication resources.

- Hull-repair supplies.

- Fire extinguishers.

- Damaged pumps, engines, batteries, junction boxes, or docking systems.

- Materials required to manufacture ammunition or medicine underway.

Saying, “There is a fuel rod in the reactor,” is not a complete fuel report.

The relevant questions are:

- How much life remains in that rod?

- Are replacement rods aboard?

- How much power will the next route require?

- Will the submarine be operating high-load systems?

- Can additional fuel be fabricated?

- Is the next outpost close enough to depend upon immediate resupply?

- What happens if the vessel must reverse course or remain active longer than expected?

The same reasoning applies to welding fuel.

One partially filled welding tank may be sufficient while the submarine is docked and intact. It will not remain sufficient after multiple hull breaches, a prolonged creature attack, and an away team returning with three empty tanks.

Inspect reserves according to the likely demands of the next voyage, not according to the calm conditions present at the station.

## PERSONAL PURCHASES AND SHIPBOARD READINESS

Crew members may spend their salary as they please, but personal purchases must not interfere with the vessel.

Do not obstruct corridors with crates of personal goods. Do not store unstable chemicals beside reactor controls. Do not place recreational items in emergency cabinets. Do not fill the diving locker with alcohol and then complain that no room remains for diving suits.

Weapons purchased ashore must be handled according to the captain’s rules and the station’s laws. Dangerous chemicals, poisons, explosives, and experimental compounds should be declared before being brought aboard.

The phrase “I bought it with my own money” does not make every object safe to store beside the ammunition magazine.

Likewise, shore leave does not excuse a crew member from being fit for duty when the submarine departs.

If the captain has authorized drinking, drink with the understanding that the vessel may still need to leave port. Emergencies, mission opportunities, station unrest, hostile action, or docking restrictions may shorten leave unexpectedly.

A crew member who returns aboard so intoxicated that they cannot climb a ladder, operate their equipment, or distinguish the airlock from the ballast tank has converted a personal purchase into a shipboard problem.

Freedom ashore comes with the responsibility to return capable of surviving the departure.

## REMAIN AVAILABLE FOR RECALL

Before leaving the submarine, know whether the captain has established a departure time.

Maintain radio contact when possible. Listen for recall announcements. Do not enter restricted station areas, distant ruins, or inaccessible tunnels without informing someone where you are going.

The captain should not be required to search every bar, merchant stall, maintenance corridor, and prison cell on the station because one crew member vanished without explanation.

When recalled:

1. Finish the transaction you are conducting.

1. Return to the submarine.

1. Report aboard.

1. Store your purchases safely.

1. Return to your assigned station or assist with final preparations.

If you expect a delay, report it immediately.

“Returning from medical merchant.”

“Held at station security.”

“Completing ammunition delivery.”

“Two minutes from docking bay.”

These are useful reports.

Silence is not.

The rest of the crew should not be held indefinitely because one submariner is still attempting to negotiate a discount on a decorative hat.

## THE EXTENDED VOYAGE

As a submarine travels toward the less settled edges of Europan society, stations become more distant, less reliable, and more poorly stocked.

The voyage between ports may extend across many kilometres of hostile water. The next station may lack the supplies available at the current one. It may be abandoned, damaged, quarantined, occupied by hostile forces, or unwilling to trade.

A vessel preparing for such a crossing must think beyond the next few minutes.

Extended voyages require reserves.

This may mean carrying:

- Multiple reactor fuel rods.

- Additional welding and oxygen tanks.

- Spare ammunition for every installed weapon.

- Materials for fabricating additional supplies.

- Reserve medical treatment.

- Away-team provisions.

- Replacement batteries and tools.

- Backup diving equipment.

- Supplies for unexpected return travel.

- Enough material to survive more than one major engagement.

The correct amount depends upon the submarine, route, mission, crew size, installed equipment, and expected danger.

There is no universal answer.

There is, however, one reliably incorrect answer:

“We have one fuel rod in the reactor. We cannot possibly need another.”

A reserve is not wasted simply because it was not consumed.

A fuel rod that returns unused has still served its purpose by ensuring that the submarine was not stranded.

A medical kit that remains unopened was not unnecessary.

A box of ammunition that was not fired still provided the vessel with the ability to respond to an attack.

Preparation should be measured against what could reasonably happen, not merely against what happened during the easiest previous voyage.

## HOW TO CONTRIBUTE PRODUCTIVELY IN PORT

Crew members wishing to accelerate departure should not repeatedly ask when the submarine is leaving.

They should help make the submarine ready to leave.

Useful work includes:

- Counting departmental supplies.

- Reporting shortages.

- Refilling oxygen and welding tanks.

- Recharging batteries.

- Restocking diving equipment.

- Recovering loose items from the deck.

- Sorting ammunition.

- Repairing damaged systems.

- Deconstructing unwanted salvage.

- Fabricating commonly used supplies.

- Preparing away-team kits.

- Moving purchased supplies aboard.

- Updating the captain on departmental readiness.

- Learning the likely requirements of the next mission.

A concise departmental report is especially valuable.

For example:

“Engineering ready. Two full fuel rods in reserve, six welding tanks filled, one junction box still damaged, and we need more electrical components before the long crossing.”

Or:

“Security ready. Coilgun ammunition is adequate, but we have one railgun shell remaining and no explosive rounds. Recommend purchasing shells before the nest-clearing mission.”

Or:

“Medical ready except for morphine and blood packs. Away-team kit is prepared, but we need replacements before departure.”

These reports permit the captain to make decisions.

“Can we go yet?” does not.

## FINAL RULE OF SHORE LEAVE

A station call belongs to the whole crew.

It is a period of rest, commerce, recreation, repair, planning, and preparation. Crew members should be permitted to enjoy it, spend their wages, explore the station, and recover from the strain of the previous mission.

They should also recognize that the submarine cannot leave safely until its needs have been identified and met.

Enjoy your leave.

Buy what you please with your own salary.

Drink when permitted.

Explore within reason.

But before the docking clamps release, make certain your department has reported what it used, what it lost, what it lacks, and what it expects to need.

There are few sounds more frustrating to a captain than hearing, ten minutes after departure:

“Captain, did anyone buy fuel?”

Europa provides enough surprises.

The crew should not manufacture additional ones at the supply counter.
