## ARTIFACT HANDLING

Alien artifacts are frequently worth substantial amounts of money.

Scientific teams, research institutions, military offices, and private collectors may pay exorbitant prices for functioning examples of alien technology.

There is a reason for this.

Artifacts may produce heat, radiation, electrical interference, pressure anomalies, structural damage, psychological effects, biological reactions, or other consequences that remain poorly understood.

Some artifacts appear stable until moved.

Others react to water, air, electricity, nearby machinery, living organisms, or other artifacts.

A device operated in the wrong environment may produce an extremely exothermic response—which is the scientific way of saying that it may become catastrophically hot, ignite its surroundings, damage the hull, cook the carrier inside their suit, or cause the submarine to experience a brief and educational disaster.

Do not activate alien technology casually.

Do not strike it with tools.

Do not connect it to the submarine’s electrical grid.

Do not carry several unknown artifacts together merely because they fit inside the same crate.

Do not place one beside the reactor to determine whether it “likes power.”

Before recovering an artifact:

- Observe it from a distance.

- Check for visible activity.

- Determine whether it is secured to the structure.

- Confirm the team has a safe carrying method.

- Warn the submarine that an artifact is being recovered.

- Keep it isolated from critical systems.

- Monitor the carrier for unusual effects.

- Be prepared to abandon it if it threatens the team or vessel.

No artifact is worth the submarine.

No salvage payment is high enough to compensate a crew that never reaches the station.

When an artifact begins producing heat, interference, radiation, electrical discharge, inexplicable sound, movement, or effects upon nearby life, report it immediately.

Do not conceal the problem because you are afraid the captain will order you to leave the valuable object behind.

The captain probably will.

That may be why the crew survives.

## COMMUNICATION OUTSIDE THE SUBMARINE

The submarine must know where the away team is and what condition it is in.

Away personnel should report at meaningful stages of the operation:

- Departure from the airlock.

- Arrival at the objective.

- Discovery of a major hazard.

- Contact with hostile forces.

- Discovery of survivors.

- Completion of the objective.

- Beginning the return.

- Entry into the airlock.

- Final accounting of personnel.

Do not provide constant commentary about every stone, fish, door, and empty container.

Do not remain silent for the entire mission.

The purpose of communication is to keep the team coordinated and allow the submarine to respond if conditions change.

Useful reports are brief and specific.

“Mining team at the deposit. No contacts.”

“Wreck entered through upper breach.”

“Hostile life in the western tunnel.”

“One diver injured; returning.”

“Artifact recovered. It is emitting heat.”

“Team inside the airlock. All personnel accounted for.”

If communication fails, the away team should follow the contingency established before departure.

This may mean returning immediately, waiting at a known location, or using another signal.

Do not continue deeper into an unknown ruin after losing all contact unless the mission and emergency circumstances clearly justify it.

## OXYGEN, DISTANCE, AND THE RETURN JOURNEY

A reserve oxygen tank is not additional exploration time.

It is insurance against delay.

Plan the mission so that the team can return before reaching its final supply. Movement may become slower because of casualties, heavy salvage, damaged equipment, hostile pursuit, difficult terrain, or a lost route.

A diver who begins returning only after the current tank is nearly empty has not preserved a reserve.

They have merely postponed suffocation.

Monitor oxygen regularly.

Do not rely upon another crew member to remind you.

If someone reports low oxygen, acknowledge the problem and begin planning the return. Do not tell them to endure a little longer while the team searches one final cabinet.

A recovered artifact is not valuable enough to justify carrying it while abandoning the person who helped retrieve it.

When necessary, leave salvage behind and recover it later.

The living crew can conduct another expedition.

The dead generally cannot.

## CASUALTY RECOVERY

If a crew member is injured outside the submarine, the team must decide whether treatment can be safely provided at the location or whether the casualty must be evacuated.

Control immediate bleeding. Restore oxygen. Remove the casualty from continuing danger. Use field supplies to stabilize them.

Do not administer every drug in the medical kit merely because the team is frightened.

Report:

- Who is injured.

- The nature of the injury.

- Whether the casualty is conscious.

- Whether the area remains dangerous.

- Whether the team can return without assistance.

Examples:

- “Miner injured by crawler. Moderate bleeding controlled. Returning.”

- “Diver unconscious with oxygen loss. Medical needed at airlock.”

- “Security has a GSW. Hostiles still active.”

- “One casualty immobile. Request recovery team.”

The submarine should prepare medical assistance before the casualty arrives.

Do not drag an injured diver into the airlock and only then inform the medical officer that they have not been breathing for several minutes.

If a casualty cannot be recovered without causing additional deaths, the team leader may order withdrawal.

This is a grave decision.

It is sometimes necessary.

Europa readily turns one casualty into an entire missing team when rescuers abandon judgment.

## ABORTING THE MISSION

An away mission may be aborted for any reasonable threat to the team or submarine.

Reasons include:

- Insufficient oxygen.

- Serious injury.

- Loss of communication.

- Overwhelming hostile contact.

- Equipment failure.

- Unexpected radiation or chemical exposure.

- Artifact instability.

- Activation of alien defences.

- The submarine coming under attack.

- Loss of the route back.

- Direct recall from the captain.

Aborting a mission is not cowardice.

It is recognition that the objective cannot presently be completed at an acceptable cost.

The team may return later with additional equipment, personnel, ammunition, knowledge, or a better-positioned submarine.

A dead crew cannot make a second attempt.

When an abort is ordered:

1. Acknowledge it.

1. Gather personnel.

1. Recover essential equipment if immediately possible.

1. Leave nonessential salvage.

1. Follow the safest known route.

1. Report progress toward the submarine.

1. Account for everyone before departure.

Do not delay withdrawal because one crew member insists upon retrieving a final mineral fragment.

Do not conceal an abort from the submarine while attempting to complete the mission anyway.

## RETURNING ABOARD

The away mission is not complete when the team reaches the airlock.

Before the submarine resumes movement, confirm:

- Every living team member has returned.

- Casualties have been delivered to medical.

- Recovered equipment has been secured.

- Dangerous specimens have been isolated.

- Artifacts have been reported.

- Weapons have been made safe.

- Empty oxygen tanks have been separated for refilling.

- Mission-critical items have been delivered.

- No one remains outside without authorization.

Report the team’s return clearly.

“All divers aboard.”

“One casualty in medical.”

“Artifact secured in the forward storage compartment.”

“Mining team returned with six containers.”

“Two members aboard; third team member still outside.”

Do not answer “everyone is aboard” merely because you personally have returned.

The captain needs an accurate accounting before moving the submarine.

Once the team is safe, conduct a brief inventory. Report what was recovered, expended, lost, damaged, or discovered.

This information will affect the next port call and the next away mission.

## FINAL RULE OF THE AWAY TEAM

Away missions keep submarines alive.

They provide minerals, medicine, salvage, artifacts, intelligence, cargo, and access to places the vessel itself cannot enter. They are also among the most dangerous operations a crew can undertake.

A successful away-team member is not the person who travels farthest into the cave, carries the strangest artifact, kills the most creatures, or returns with the most dramatic story.

It is the person who helps complete the objective and brings the team home.

Watch the water.

Watch your oxygen.

Watch your line of fire.

Listen to the recall.

Do not touch what you do not understand.

Do not remain for one more piece of salvage when the situation has already become unsafe.

Beyond the submarine’s hull, Europa does not distinguish between bravery, greed, curiosity, and stupidity.

It consumes all four with equal efficiency.

## THE OXYGEN GENERATOR

The Loudest Thing Nobody Remembers to Turn Off

When captains discuss the acoustic profile of a submarine, attention generally falls upon the obvious offenders.

The reactor hums.

The engines thunder.

The pumps hammer.

The guns announce themselves with all the subtlety of a collapsing station.

Yet repeated acoustic surveys have identified another machine as one of the loudest, most distinctive, and most persistent sources of detectable noise aboard the average Europan submarine:

The oxygen generator.

This tends to surprise new captains.

It should not surprise experienced ones, although it frequently does.

The oxygen generator is active almost continuously. It draws substantial electrical power, cycles pumps and processing machinery, moves atmosphere through the vessel, separates useful gases, and performs the ongoing technological miracle required to keep an entire crew from quietly becoming corpses.

It is less a household appliance and more an industrial atmospheric plant compressed into a compartment barely large enough for an engineer to swear inside.

Naturally, it is loud.
