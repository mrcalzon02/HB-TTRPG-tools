## XVII. STANDARD UNION 208

Because Even at Crush Depth, Someone Will Ask About Overtime

Submariner labor organization began almost as early as permanent settlement.

One of the earliest recorded disputes involved a Crampton-class cargo submarine supporting a prestigious government scientific mission.

The expedition’s sponsors assumed the honor of serving science would compensate for long hours, dangerous transit, poor conditions, and miserable pay.

The crew concluded that honor could not be spent at the supply counter.

They refused departure, organized collectively, and demanded compensation proportionate to the voyage.

The dispute delayed the mission, embarrassed its sponsors, and produced one of Europa’s earliest recognized submarine labor agreements.

From that tradition emerged Standard Union 208, now among the most widely recognized submariner organizations beneath the ice.

The union’s influence differs between settlements, employers, and jurisdictions, but its standards are commonly referenced even aboard nonunion vessels.

Captains should familiarize themselves with the Standard Union 208 Standards of Operations, Submarine Fairness, and Crew Justice.

The standards generally recognize:

- Agreed pay scales.

- Hazard compensation.

- Clear duties.

- Reasonable rest.

- Necessary protective equipment.

- Transparent division of mission rewards.

- Medical treatment.

- Grievance procedures.

- Limits on arbitrary punishment.

- Compensation for extended duties.

- Restrictions on abandoning personnel.

- The radical principle that crew members should not be used as experimental subjects without asking first.

Union agreements may appear inconvenient.

They are generally less inconvenient than a reactor operator deciding mid-voyage that the collective bargaining session begins now.
