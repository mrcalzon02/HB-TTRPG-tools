## THE ASSISTANT’S PRIVATE SUPERPOWER

Every assistant eventually develops one of several unusual abilities.

Some can locate any missing tool.

Some can carry an impossible number of items.

Some always arrive with a fresh oxygen tank.

Some know which compartment is flooding before the alarm sounds.

Some can calm an angry engineer with the simple phrase:

“I already brought the components.”

Some can cross the entire submarine during combat without being shot, bitten, drowned, or accidentally locked in ballast.

Some somehow know exactly when the captain is about to ask for a status report.

These are not talents listed in recruitment material.

They emerge through repeated exposure to crisis, poor storage discipline, and coworkers who habitually leave essential equipment in their pockets.

Cultivate your ability.

Use it responsibly.

If your ability is “discovering new ways to poison the crew,” consider cultivating another one.

## FINAL RULE OF THE GENERAL CREWMAN

The assistant is not a consolation prize.

It is not the chair left vacant after the important jobs have been assigned.

It is not a declaration that you are unskilled, unnecessary, or waiting to be replaced.

It is the crew position with the broadest field of action and the greatest freedom to learn.

A new assistant contributes by listening, carrying, observing, and responding.

An experienced assistant contributes by anticipating, organizing, repairing, supporting, and filling gaps before they become disasters.

A veteran assistant can make the entire submarine operate more smoothly simply by being present, alert, and willing to go where the work is accumulating.

Bring the mechanic welding fuel.

Bring security ammunition.

Bring medical casualties.

Bring engineering components.

Bring the captain accurate information.

Bring the away team home.

Bring back the tools everyone else forgot.

And when every department insists it is too busy to solve the latest problem, step forward and ask the traditional question of the Europan general crewman:

“All right. What has broken now?”

You are not the least important member of the crew.

You are the person who makes the important members of the crew substantially less helpless.

Wear that distinction proudly.

The novelty hat is optional.

## THE CARRYING OF ARMS

A firearm aboard a submarine is not a symbol of rank, authority, or personal importance. It is a tool entrusted to a crew member for the defence of the vessel and the lives aboard it.

Security personnel, armed divers, and away-team members must understand that possessing a weapon does not require its immediate use. The ability to fire is not the same as having a safe opportunity to fire.

A single crawler outside the hull may not justify a nuclear railgun shell. A crew member wearing a clown suit is not automatically a hostile combatant. An argument in the reactor compartment is rarely improved by launching a grenade into it. A mudraptor surrounded by fleeing crew requires a clear line of fire, not indiscriminate panic.

Before firing, identify:

1. The threat.

1. What lies between you and the threat.

1. What lies behind the threat.

1. Which weapon is appropriate.

1. Whether friendly personnel are within or near the line of fire.

1. Whether the ammunition may be required against a greater threat later.

The use of force should always be proportionate to the danger.

When dealing with disorder among the crew, begin with clear instructions. Use restraint, stun weapons, handcuffs, or other nonlethal measures when circumstances allow. Lethal force is reserved for immediate and serious threats to life, the vessel, or the mission.

Security officers are expected to protect the crew, not terrorize them.

They are not permitted to manufacture confrontations, demand personal obedience beyond their assigned authority, or punish imagined disrespect. They do not execute crew members because they are irritating. They do not fire blindly into crowded compartments. They do not empty weapons into incapacitated personnel merely to demonstrate certainty.

Trigger discipline preserves ammunition, prevents accidental casualties, and maintains the crew’s trust.

A security officer feared by every person aboard is not maintaining order. They are creating the next emergency.

## LINES OF FIRE

Every armed crew member must understand one rule before carrying a loaded weapon:

If a crew member is standing between you and the enemy, your line of fire is not clear.

It does not matter how confident you are.

It does not matter how close the enemy appears to be.

It does not matter whether the crew member in front of you is standing, crouching, crawling, fighting, retreating, or being dragged across the deck.

It does not matter whether you believe you can fire over their shoulder, past their helmet, between their legs, or a fraction of a metre above their head.

Do not fire through another crew member.

Weapons are rarely as accurate under combat conditions as their operators imagine. The submarine may be moving. The target may lunge. The shooter may stumble. Recoil may pull the barrel off target. Visibility may be poor. Water, smoke, blood, fire, damaged lighting, confined corridors, and panicked movement may obscure the exact position of both friend and foe.

A shot that appears perfectly aligned may strike the person you are supposedly attempting to protect.

This is especially important when a crew member is already engaged in close combat with a hostile life-form. If a mechanic, diver, security officer, or assistant is being bitten, clawed, or dragged by a creature, firing through their body does not improve their chances of survival.

It gives them an additional injury.

A GSW, or gunshot wound, creates another source of bleeding, organ damage, pain, blood loss, and incapacitation. If your crewmate is already being mauled by the forces of the deep, shooting them in the back of the head is not assistance.

It is friendly fire.

The rule is therefore simple:

Friendly in front. Enemy behind. Do not fire.

End of instruction.

## THE CROUCHING FALLACY

Some armed personnel believe that a crouched teammate provides sufficient clearance to fire over them.

This belief has filled more medical wards than any Europan predator.

A crouched crew member is not cover, a weapon rest, or an acceptable firing aperture. They may stand without warning. They may be struck and thrown upward. They may climb a ladder, recoil from an attack, stumble over loose equipment, or move directly into the path of your shot.

The enemy may also move lower at the exact moment you fire.

The supposed gap above a crouching crew member is not a safe corridor for bullets, harpoons, shotgun pellets, grenades, explosives, or directed energy.

If a friendly crew member is crouching between you and the target, the line of fire remains obstructed.

Do not fire.

## ELEVATED AND OFFSET TARGETS

An enemy being slightly above, below, or to one side of a friendly does not automatically create a clear shot.

Do not assume that a weapon will place every round precisely where the sights are pointed. A burst may climb. A shotgun may spread. A harpoon may deviate. A moving shooter may drag the barrel across a friendly silhouette. An explosive projectile may detonate against a railing, doorway, hatch frame, or the crew member themselves.

If any portion of a crewmate occupies the likely path of the projectile, the shot is unsafe.

A narrow angle is not a clear angle.

A partial opening is not a clear opening.

A hopeful shot is not a clear shot.

Wait, move, or choose another method.

## REPOSITION BEFORE FIRING

When a friendly blocks your shot, your first responsibility is not to fire more carefully.

Your first responsibility is to establish a safer angle.

You may:

- Move to the side of the engagement.

- Fall back and cover a wider approach.

- Use another corridor, hatch, ladder, or compartment.

- Order the forward crew member to disengage or move aside.

- Allow the closer combatant to finish the engagement.

- Prepare to fire if the friendly is thrown clear.

- Cover additional enemies approaching from another direction.

- Provide medical, defensive, or logistical support instead of shooting.

Do not crowd directly behind a crew member who is already fighting. Doing so restricts their ability to retreat and places your weapon directly against their back.

Spread out where the environment permits. Establish overlapping fields of fire that do not overlap friendly bodies.

A firing position is useful only when it allows you to engage the enemy without first shooting through your own crew.

## CROSSING A FIRING LINE

Crew members not currently firing must also respect the weapons around them.

Do not run in front of an armed security officer who is actively engaging a target. Do not step through a doorway while someone is firing through it. Do not climb a ladder into an established firing position without warning the shooter.

When crossing is necessary, communicate.

Use clear calls such as:

- “Crossing!”

- “Hold fire!”

- “Moving in front!”

- “Cease fire!”

- “Friendly entering!”

- “Clear behind me!”

- “Taking point!”

The shooter should acknowledge the warning and stop firing before the crew member crosses.

The person carrying the weapon remains responsible for the shot. However, every member of an armed team shares responsibility for maintaining clear and predictable movement.

Do not dart unpredictably through active firing lanes and then blame the shooter for failing to anticipate your path.

Likewise, a shooter may not excuse reckless fire by claiming that a friendly “should not have moved.”

Both parties must communicate.
