## FINAL DUTY OF THE MEDICAL OFFICER

The crew does not require the medical officer to be a miracle worker.

It requires them to be dependable.

Learn the common afflictions.

Understand what the medicines aboard the vessel actually do.

Treat dangerous conditions promptly.

Allow minor injuries to recover without consuming strategic reserves.

Control narcotics, poisons, and antidotes.

Prepare away teams for the hazards they are likely to face.

Maintain enough supplies to survive the voyage that has not yet gone wrong.

Above all, remember that medicine is not measured by how many injections have been administered. It is measured by how many crew members remain alive, functional, and capable of returning to their stations.

You are not the dealer.

You are the medical officer.

Please conduct yourself in a manner that permits the crew to recognize the difference.

## GOING ASHORE

Station Leave, Resupply, and Preparation Between Voyages

When a submarine docks at an inhabited station, members of the crew may be granted leave to go ashore.

“Going ashore” is the traditional expression, although there is very little shore beneath Europa’s ice and what passes for dry land is generally a pressurized corridor bolted into a cavern wall. The meaning remains the same: the vessel is secure, the docking connection has been established, and the crew may temporarily leave the submarine to enter the station.

Unless otherwise ordered by the captain or station authorities, crew members on leave are free to explore the station, speak with its inhabitants, visit merchants, seek local employment, purchase personal equipment, and spend their own salary as they see fit.

Your wages belong to you.

You may save them. You may purchase tools, clothing, weapons where permitted, medical equipment, personal luxuries, questionable food, decorative objects, or enough ethanol to make your next financial review deeply embarrassing.

The captain is not ordinarily responsible for supervising every personal purchase made by the crew. Neither is the captain required to finance those purchases from the vessel’s operational budget.

A distinction must always be maintained between:

- Personal salary, belonging to the individual crew member.

- Ship funds, reserved for the operation, maintenance, and continued survival of the submarine.

Do not ask the captain to purchase a recreational item from ship funds simply because you want one.

A novelty water gun, ceremonial hat, musical instrument, crate of alcohol, decorative pet, or exotic chemical may be entertaining. It is not automatically a legitimate vessel expense.

The captain may choose to authorize such purchases when the submarine is profitable and adequately supplied. They may even decide that crew morale justifies buying enough ethanol to leave engineering drunk as a monkey’s uncle.

That is the captain’s decision.

It should not become the subject of repeated demands while the command staff is calculating whether the submarine possesses enough fuel and ammunition to survive the next crossing.

## PORT IS NOT WASTED TIME

Crew members are often eager to leave a station as quickly as possible.

This is understandable. A submarine earns little while sitting motionless in a docking berth. No cargo is being delivered. No wreck is being salvaged. No mineral deposit is being harvested. No bounty is being collected. The vessel is making no visible progress toward its next objective.

To use the language occasionally heard from less patient crew members, the voyage is not “moving forward.”

This view mistakes movement for progress.

Time spent at a station is frequently as valuable as time spent underway.

While docked, the submarine’s immediate power and pressure requirements are supported by the station’s infrastructure. The reactor can be taken out of its ordinary operating cycle. Batteries can be restored. Damaged systems can be examined without the vessel actively descending through hostile waters. Supplies can be counted in relative safety. Departments can compare what was consumed during the previous mission against what will be required during the next.

It may appear that nothing is happening.

In reality, the next voyage is being made possible.

A port call is often the crew’s only reliable period of respite between emergencies. It is a time to rest, reorganize, replace missing personnel, purchase supplies, review damage, fabricate equipment, examine new mission opportunities, and determine what dangers lie ahead.

Rushing the submarine away from the station without proper preparation may save several minutes at the dock and cost the crew an entire voyage beneath the ice.

## THE LOGISTICAL CONFERENCE

During a port call, the captain must coordinate with the vessel’s departments.

This may involve the engineer, mechanic, medical officer, security personnel, gunnery crews, quartermaster, logistics officer, or whichever unfortunate crew member has inherited responsibility for keeping track of the ship’s stores.

These conversations are not idle chatter.

They are how the captain determines:

- What was expended during the previous voyage.

- What was damaged or lost.

- What remains aboard.

- What must be purchased immediately.

- What can be fabricated from existing materials.

- What may be required by the next mission.

- Which supplies must be reserved for a longer crossing.

- Whether the vessel can afford discretionary purchases.

- Whether the selected mission is practical with the resources available.

Interrupting this process with repeated requests for toys, alcohol, recreational chemicals, personal equipment, or nonessential modifications does not make departure happen sooner.

It makes departure happen later.

Every time the captain must stop reviewing fuel reserves to answer whether the crew may purchase a super-soaker, the actual work of preparing the vessel is delayed.

Every time the engineer must leave a discussion about reactor endurance to explain why the submarine does not need twelve decorative lamps, the logistics process slows.

Every time the medical officer is interrupted while reporting shortages because someone wishes to know whether station alcohol is cheaper by the bottle or the crate, a necessary detail may be forgotten.

Nonessential requests can wait until the vessel’s operational needs have been established.

The quickest way to leave port is to help complete the work required before departure.

## DEPARTMENTAL INVENTORIES

Every department should inspect its own equipment before wandering away from the submarine.

Do not assume someone else knows what you used.

Do not assume the captain has personally counted every shell, bandage, fuel rod, oxygen tank, welding cylinder, diving suit, and screwdriver aboard the vessel.

The captain directs the process.

The departments provide the information.

Before going ashore for personal business, determine what your section consumed, lost, damaged, or requires.

Report shortages while the submarine is still docked beside merchants capable of correcting them.

Discovering a critical shortage after departure is not merely inconvenient. It means the crew had an opportunity to solve the problem and failed to use it.

## SECURITY AND GUNNERY REPORTS

Security personnel and weapon operators should inspect the vessel’s ammunition reserves after every substantial engagement.

Count ammunition by type, not merely by container.

Report:

- Empty or partially depleted ammunition boxes.

- Railgun shells expended.

- Coilgun, chaingun, flak, or pulse ammunition consumed.

- Nuclear or explosive ammunition remaining.

- Handheld ammunition used by security personnel.

- Lost or damaged weapons.

- Stun weapons, restraints, armor, or diving equipment requiring replacement.

- Specialized ammunition likely to be needed for the next mission.

A report stating “we still have some ammunition” is not sufficient.

The captain and engineering department need to know whether “some” means six full boxes or fourteen rounds rattling around in the bottom of an otherwise empty container.

Ammunition types are not interchangeable in purpose.

The rounds effective against small, fast-moving creatures may not be appropriate against armored targets. Weapons used for defending the submarine may require different supplies from those carried by an assault team boarding an enemy installation.

When the captain returns with proposed mission objectives, security should consider the expected threat.

If the next route is likely to involve pirate activity, hostile outposts, large creatures, swarms, nest clearance, or dangerous wreck exploration, say what ammunition and equipment will be required.

Do not remain silent at port and then announce halfway through the battle that the weapon best suited to the enemy has been empty since the previous voyage.
