## “I HURT” IS A SYMPTOM, NOT A DIAGNOSIS

Crew members will frequently enter medical and announce that they are injured without providing any further information.

The medical officer is expected to evaluate them, not automatically medicate them.

Ask where they are hurt and what happened. Use the health scanner or other diagnostic tools. Determine whether the condition is serious enough to justify treatment.

A crew member who has suffered a minor bruise, a small laceration, or ordinary soreness may not require narcotics. They may need a bandage, observation, a brief rest, or nothing at all.

This is not neglect.

It is responsible resource management.

Morphine is not a reward for reporting to medical. Fentanyl is not a morale ration. Combat stimulants are not refreshments. Sedatives are not tools for making annoying crew members quieter.

If the patient does not medically require the drug, do not administer it.

The medic who responds to every complaint with narcotics will soon discover that the crew has developed an extraordinary number of mysterious aches immediately before every mission.

## NARCOTICS AND CONTROLLED MEDICATION

The submarine’s medical cabinet may contain a staggering variety of narcotics, stimulants, sedatives, poisons, antidotes, antibiotics, anti-radiation treatments, and experimental compounds.

Access to these substances is granted because the medical officer may need them to save lives.

It is not granted so the infirmary can become the liveliest social compartment on the vessel.

The medical officer must control access to high-risk medication. Do not distribute narcotics merely because a crew member asks. Do not leave dangerous drugs scattered across the deck. Do not hand a crate of morphine to an assistant because they promise to “be responsible.”

A medication can be appropriate in one dose and lethal in another.

Before administering a controlled drug, consider:

- The patient’s condition.

- The seriousness of their pain.

- Other drugs already administered.

- The likelihood of overdose.

- The possibility of addiction or withdrawal.

- The supply remaining aboard.

- Whether a safer treatment is available.

Do not repeatedly medicate a patient without checking what they have already received. In an emergency involving several medics, communicate doses clearly.

A patient should not receive three separate injections from three separate medical personnel because none of them spoke to one another.

Mark treated casualties when practical. Report significant medication over the radio or through established medical channels.

“Patient stabilized with morphine” is useful.

“I gave them something” is not.

## OVERDOSE IS NOT AN EXPERIMENT

There will occasionally be a crew member who wishes to determine how many doses of morphine, fentanyl, stimulants, or other medication a human body can tolerate.

The medical officer’s role is to prevent this experiment, not supervise it.

The answer is not medically useful. It is operationally expensive and frequently fatal.

Encouraging the crew to inject themselves for amusement consumes supplies that may later be required during boarding actions, salvage operations, mining expeditions, nest clearances, ruin exploration, or catastrophic hull failure.

The captain will not appreciate being informed at the next outpost that another crate of morphine is required when the crew suffered no serious injuries during the previous voyage.

The following explanation will not improve matters:

“Jerry in engineering wanted to know how many injections it would take to kill someone, and the other half of the crew volunteered to help establish the answer.”

The answer, for procurement purposes, will invariably be:

Every dose we had.

A medical officer who encourages this degree of waste should expect to be voluntold into the next several sea-poppy harvesting expeditions. They may spend a considerable portion of subsequent voyages scraping aquatic poppies from cave walls under the supervision of an irritated security officer.

Medical supplies are purchased to keep the crew alive.

Do not use them to establish exactly how difficult that task can be made.

## POISONS, VENOMS, AND THEIR ANTIDOTES

The medical department may possess substances that have legitimate medical, research, security, or mission-specific uses but are also capable of causing paralysis, organ failure, unconsciousness, psychosis, or death.

These materials must be controlled.

Do not carry poisons casually in personal clothing. Do not leave them beside ordinary medication. Do not replace legitimate ampoules with dangerous compounds as a joke. Do not inject unconscious patients with experimental chemicals unless circumstances justify the risk and the procedure has been authorized.

Labels should remain visible. Dangerous substances should be stored separately from routine treatment whenever the vessel’s layout permits.

The medical officer should maintain sufficient antidotes to respond to the toxins most likely to be encountered. They should not consume the entire antidote supply treating imagined exposure or experimenting on volunteers.

A poison and its antidote are not a matched recreational set.

## TRIAGE DURING MASS-CASUALTY EVENTS

When several crew members are injured simultaneously, the medical officer must establish priorities.

The loudest patient is not necessarily the most critical.

Treat first those who will die without immediate assistance and who can still be saved through timely intervention. Then treat those whose conditions may deteriorate. Minor injuries can wait.

A practical order of attention is:

1. Patients who cannot breathe or are suffering severe oxygen deprivation.

1. Patients with uncontrolled bleeding or catastrophic blood loss.

1. Patients who are unconscious, rapidly deteriorating, or suffering life-threatening poisoning.

1. Patients with severe burns, major trauma, or dangerous infection.

1. Patients with moderate injuries that impair their ability to function.

1. Patients with minor injuries and manageable pain.

1. Crew members requesting narcotics because they bumped their knee while climbing a ladder.

Triage may require directing uninjured personnel to assist.

A mechanic can apply a bandage. A security officer can carry a casualty. An assistant can retrieve blood packs or oxygen. An engineer can help move patients out of a flooded compartment.

The medical officer should coordinate these efforts rather than attempting to perform every task alone.

During a major emergency, give direct instructions:

- “Apply pressure to that wound.”

- “Bring two blood packs.”

- “Move this patient into oxygen.”

- “Carry the unconscious diver to medical.”

- “Do not administer anything else.”

- “Security, restrain the infected patient.”

- “Captain, medical requires evacuation.”

Clear medical command saves lives.

Frantic rummaging through cabinets does not.

## PREPARING AWAY TEAMS

The medical officer’s responsibility extends beyond the infirmary.

Away missions expose personnel to hostile creatures, gunfire, pressure, drowning, infection, venom, environmental hazards, and the perennial danger of someone forgetting to bring enough oxygen.

Before a salvage, mining, exploration, assault, or recovery operation, the medical officer should help prepare an appropriate field kit.

The kit should reflect the actual mission.

A short mining excursion near the submarine does not require the entire medical cabinet. An assault on a hostile installation should not depart with one bandage and a positive attitude.

An away-team medical kit may include:

- Basic wound treatment.

- Supplies for controlling serious bleeding.

- Blood replacement material when justified.

- Appropriate pain relief.

- Antidotes relevant to the expected threat.

- Treatment for burns or environmental exposure.

- Additional oxygen.

- Emergency stimulants or revival equipment.

- Tools required to transport or stabilize casualties.

Distribute supplies sensibly. Do not place the entire medical reserve on one diver who may be the first person incapacitated.

The medical officer should know who is carrying the field supplies and whether a trained medic is accompanying the mission. When possible, retain enough material aboard to treat casualties caused by a simultaneous attack on the submarine.

Europa has a habit of creating two emergencies whenever the crew prepares for one.

## MEDICAL READINESS ABOARD THE VESSEL

A good medical officer does not wait for someone to begin dying before checking whether the department is functional.

Before departure, inspect medical storage.

Confirm that:

- Essential medicines are present.

- Blood and emergency supplies are available.

- Dangerous drugs are secured.

- Diagnostic equipment is operational.

- Diving medical kits are prepared.

- Empty or depleted containers are identified.

- Supplies are stored where they can be found quickly.

- Previous patients have not wandered away carrying half the pharmacy.

During quieter periods, fabricate or organize commonly required treatments. Monitor the consumption of scarce materials. Inform the captain before the submarine reaches a crisis.

“Medical is low on blood packs and broad-spectrum antibiotics” is useful before the next mission is selected.

“We have nothing left” is less useful after the crew has been boarded.

## THE MEDICAL OFFICER IS NOT EXEMPT FROM ACCOUNTABILITY

Medical authority does not permit experimentation without consent, poisoning crew members for entertainment, withholding care as punishment, or using sedation to settle arguments.

Nor does it excuse incompetence that the officer refuses to correct.

Every medical officer will make mistakes. Europan medicine is performed in moving vessels under poor lighting while alarms sound and creatures attempt to enter the room. Perfection is not expected.

Learning is expected.

When an error occurs, report it honestly. If the wrong medication was given, say what was administered and how much. If supplies were lost, tell the captain. If a patient may have been exposed to infection, do not conceal it.

A medic who admits a mistake gives the crew an opportunity to correct it.

A medic who hides a mistake converts it into a second emergency.
