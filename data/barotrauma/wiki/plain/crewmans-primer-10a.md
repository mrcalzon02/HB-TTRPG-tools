## KEEP THE DECKS CLEAR

Loose objects are a hazard.

They obstruct movement, conceal damage, make emergency supplies difficult to locate, create noise, and may be swept out of the vessel during a hull breach. A tool abandoned in a corridor is of no use when the engineer searching for it is three compartments away and taking on water.

Return equipment to appropriate storage.

Place diving equipment near the airlock. Return medical supplies to medical storage. Store ammunition near the weapon it serves, unless the vessel’s officers have established another arrangement. Refill tanks and place them with the full supplies. Separate empty, damaged, or contaminated equipment when possible.

Reusable equipment should not be discarded casually.

Harpoons can be recovered. Oxygen and welding tanks can be refilled. Empty containers, spent materials, and unwanted items may be recycled or deconstructed. Tools dropped during an emergency should be collected once the immediate danger has passed.

Bodies must also be handled with care.

The dead may carry identification, equipment, evidence of infection, or valuable supplies. More importantly, they were members of the crew. When conditions permit, recover and secure the remains. Follow the captain’s instructions regarding burial, quarantine, storage, or transfer at the next outpost.

Dismembering a fallen crew member and dropping the remains into the ballast tank is not a recognized funerary custom.

A well-kept submarine is not merely pleasing to look at. It is faster to repair, easier to defend, safer to evacuate, and harder to lose in confusion.

Order aboard ship is a survival mechanism.

## PROFESSIONAL CONDUCT

Life beneath the ice is stressful.

Crew members will make mistakes. Orders will be misunderstood. Equipment will fail at inconvenient moments. People will become frightened, tired, impatient, and angry. A submariner is not expected to remain cheerful through every disaster.

They are expected to remain civil.

Abuse, harassment, deliberate humiliation, threats, and sustained personal hostility have no place aboard a functioning vessel. They damage concentration, discourage necessary communication, and often escalate minor disagreements into violence.

Correct mistakes without degrading the person who made them.

A clear instruction is more useful than an insult. A concise explanation is more effective than a public tirade. If someone is inexperienced, teach them when circumstances permit. If they are unwilling to learn or repeatedly endanger the vessel, report the matter through the proper chain of command.

There is a difference between incompetence and sabotage. There is also a difference between harmless foolishness and conduct that threatens the ship.

Not every joke requires punishment. Not every disagreement is mutiny. Not every mistake proves malicious intent.

At the same time, “I was only joking” does not excuse firing weapons in occupied compartments, wasting vital supplies, opening hatches during flooding, tampering with the reactor, poisoning the crew, or interfering with navigation.

When frustration begins to affect your judgment, step away from the argument. Change compartments. Mute unnecessary communications. Request relief from a station when possible. A brief pause is preferable to a confrontation that ends in injury, arrest, or banishment at the next outpost.

The voyage is supposed to be survivable.

It may even, on rare occasions, be enjoyable.

## DEPARTMENTAL RESPONSIBILITY

Every profession aboard a submarine exists because the vessel cannot survive without it.

Captain

The captain directs navigation, coordinates departments, selects missions, manages priorities, and accepts responsibility for the vessel’s overall operation.

Engineer

Engineers maintain the reactor, electrical grid, junction boxes, batteries, and powered systems. They should be informed before anyone makes significant changes to the vessel’s wiring or fuel configuration.

Mechanic

Mechanics repair the hull, pumps, engines, fabrication equipment, and other mechanical systems. They are generally among the first personnel required during flooding.

Medical Officer

Medical personnel preserve life, manage pharmaceuticals, diagnose injuries and infections, and establish treatment priorities during mass-casualty events. Medical storage is not a public dispensary.

Security Officer

Security protects the crew, controls access to weapons, responds to hostile boarding, investigates sabotage, and maintains order using the least force reasonably necessary.

Assistant

Assistants provide flexible labour wherever needed. A capable assistant may transport supplies, repair damage, support diving operations, reload weapons, recover casualties, and fill gaps between departments.

No profession exists merely to “fix things,” “shoot things,” or “annoy things.”

Learn the boundaries of your role, but do not use them as an excuse to ignore an obvious emergency. A security officer may fix a pump. A medic may carry ammunition. An assistant may weld a breach. An engineer may drag a casualty to medical.

Specialization keeps the submarine efficient.

Cooperation keeps it alive.

## WHEN EVERYTHING GOES WRONG

There will be voyages during which several emergencies occur at once.

The hull breaches. The reactor begins overheating. Hostile creatures enter the ballast system. The medic is injured. The captain loses consciousness. Half the crew begins shouting contradictory reports over the radio.

When this happens, remember the order of survival:

1. Preserve breathable compartments.

1. Control flooding.

1. Stabilize the reactor and electrical systems.

1. Contain immediate hostile threats.

1. Recover and treat casualties.

1. Restore propulsion, navigation, and weapons.

1. Account for the crew.

1. Recover, reorganize, and resupply.

Do not run aimlessly between emergencies. Choose the most urgent problem you are capable of addressing and report that you are responding to it.

“Repairing aft breach.”

“Taking reactor.”

“Moving casualties to medical.”

“Reloading dorsal coilgun.”

Clear reports allow the captain to understand which problems remain unattended.

Panic spreads quickly through a submarine. So does competence.

Be the crew member whose arrival improves the situation.
