## THE PURPOSE OF MEDICAL TREATMENT

Medical treatment exists to restore a crew member to useful health, prevent an injury from worsening, and keep the casualty alive until more complete treatment is available.

These are not always the same objective.

A minor bruise may require no treatment beyond time and caution. Moderate bleeding may require bandaging before pain relief is considered. A crew member suffering oxygen deprivation requires breathable air before narcotics. A patient with catastrophic blood loss requires blood replacement more urgently than they require reassurance.

The medical officer must determine what is wrong before reaching for medication.

That evaluation does not need to become an hour-long consultation while the submarine fills with water. It should, however, involve more than asking, “Does it hurt?” and injecting the patient with the first substance found in the cabinet.

At minimum, determine:

- What injured the patient.

- Where the injury is located.

- Whether the patient is bleeding.

- Whether significant blood has already been lost.

- Whether the patient can breathe.

- Whether they are conscious and responsive.

- Whether they have been poisoned, infected, burned, irradiated, or exposed to pressure.

- Whether they have already taken medication.

- Whether another casualty requires more urgent attention.

The medic’s purpose is not to eliminate every discomfort immediately. It is to identify which conditions are dangerous, which are becoming dangerous, and which can safely be left to recover without consuming limited supplies.

## COMMON CONDITIONS OF THE SUBMARINER’S TRADE

A competent medical officer should be able to recognize and respond to the injuries most frequently encountered aboard a Europan submarine.

Lacerations and Bleeding

Cuts and lacerations may result from creature attacks, broken machinery, shattered structures, knives, harpoons, or personnel attempting to repair electrical equipment while holding entirely inappropriate tools.

The immediate concern is uncontrolled bleeding.

Stop the bleeding before treating the patient’s complaints about pain. A conscious crew member with a painful wound can wait several seconds for analgesia. A crew member rapidly emptying their circulatory system onto the deck cannot.

Bandages and other wound treatments should be used according to the severity of the injury. Do not consume advanced medical supplies on scratches that could be managed with simpler treatment.

Once bleeding has been controlled, assess blood loss and any underlying injury.

## Gunshot Wounds

A gunshot wound, commonly reported over the radio as a GSW, may involve bleeding, internal trauma, organ damage, pain, loss of mobility, or rapid unconsciousness.

The fact that security describes the shooting as accidental does not make the injury less severe.

Determine the number and location of wounds. Control bleeding. Evaluate blood loss. Stabilize the patient before beginning an argument about who crossed whose firing line.

If an armed crew member has shot a teammate, the medic requires useful information:

- What weapon was used.

- How many times it was fired.

- Where the patient was struck.

- Whether the area is now secure.

- Whether additional casualties are incoming.

“Something happened in engineering” is not an adequate medical report.

## Blood Loss

Blood loss is a condition in its own right and must not be mistaken for ordinary pain.

A patient may appear pale, weak, unsteady, confused, or close to unconsciousness. Closing the wound prevents further loss, but it does not replace what has already been lost.

A medic who bandages a heavily exsanguinated patient and immediately sends them back to work has completed only half the treatment.

Blood products and replacement fluids must be preserved for patients who genuinely require them. They should not be used as refreshments, demonstrations, practical jokes, or ingredients in experiments conducted without the captain’s knowledge.

## Blunt-Force Trauma

The submarine is filled with ladders, hatches, machinery, narrow access ways, steep companionways, and crew members who believe descending headfirst is faster.

As a result, the infirmary will receive a steady procession of people who have fallen down ladders, collided with doors, been struck by loose equipment, or injured themselves during abrupt changes in depth and motion.

Not every bruise requires narcotics.

Minor blunt-force injuries can frequently be observed and allowed to recover naturally. A crew member suffering a small amount of pain after falling down a ladder may be advised to rest, work more carefully, and—in the traditional medical terminology of the Europan fleet—sleep it off.

More serious trauma requires attention when it causes substantial pain, impaired movement, unconsciousness, internal injury, or a rapidly worsening condition.

The medical officer must distinguish discomfort from disability.

## Burns

Burns may result from fire, reactor accidents, electrical discharge, welding incidents, chemical exposure, or a mechanic attempting to extinguish flames by standing inside them.

Remove the casualty from the source of injury. Extinguish burning clothing. Treat the burn with appropriate supplies and consider whether the patient has also suffered smoke inhalation, oxygen deprivation, or electrical injury.

Do not inject a heavily burned casualty with an impressive collection of medication merely because the injuries look dramatic. Treat the actual conditions present.

## Oxygen Deprivation

A patient suffering from oxygen deprivation requires oxygen.

This principle appears obvious, but it has repeatedly escaped medical personnel who attempt to treat unconscious divers with pain medication while leaving an empty oxygen tank attached to the patient’s suit.

Check the airway, the surrounding atmosphere, the diving suit, and the oxygen supply. Move the patient into a breathable compartment when possible.

Medication cannot compensate for the continued absence of oxygen.

## Pressure and Diving Injuries

Personnel exposed to the Europan sea without adequate protection may suffer catastrophic pressure trauma. These casualties require immediate recovery into a safe compartment and rapid treatment.

The medical officer should know when a diving team has departed, what hazards they expect to encounter, and which supplies may be required upon their return.

A medic who learns that an away team exists only when three unconscious divers are deposited on the infirmary floor has not been properly monitoring the operation.

## Poisoning

Europa offers a remarkable variety of substances capable of killing a human being.

Some are produced by local life. Others are carried aboard deliberately. Still others were fabricated by the medical department after someone discovered that the chemical synthesizer would permit it.

The medical officer should understand the common effects of the poisons, venoms, paralysants, and toxins carried or encountered by the crew. They must also know which antidotes correspond to which agents.

An antidote is not universally effective merely because the container contains the word “antidote.”

When poisoning is suspected, determine what substance may have been involved and how long ago exposure occurred. Administer the appropriate countermeasure and observe the patient for continuing deterioration.

Do not respond to an unknown toxin by injecting every antidote aboard the vessel in alphabetical order.

## Infection and Parasites

Any crew member returning from a wreck, ruin, cave, nest, or flooded compartment may have encountered infection.

Unexplained difficulty breathing, abnormal wounds, unusual movement, progressive weakness, behavioral changes, or visible signs of parasitic exposure should be investigated immediately.

The medical officer must balance treatment with containment. A suspected infection may endanger more than the patient.

Inform the captain when quarantine is necessary. Do not conceal a serious communicable condition because the infected crew member is a friend, senior officer, or particularly persuasive clown.

## Radiation and Environmental Exposure

Depending on the route, mission, equipment, and condition of the vessel, the crew may encounter radiation or other environmental hazards.

Anti-radiation treatment is not a recreational stimulant and should not be issued merely because someone stood near the reactor and became nervous.

Confirm meaningful exposure before consuming scarce treatment.

The engineer’s continued habit of sleeping beside the reactor does not automatically constitute a medical emergency, though it may warrant a separate discussion.

## Pain, Shock, and Psychological Effects

Pain is medically relevant, but pain alone does not determine treatment priority.

A shouting patient with a minor wound may appear more urgent than a silent patient who is bleeding internally, losing oxygen, entering shock, or becoming unconscious. The medical officer must treat severity rather than volume.

Shock, confusion, panic, psychosis, and other psychological conditions can also affect crew safety. A patient may become irrational due to injury, poisoning, oxygen deprivation, medication, fear, infection, or an underlying psychological condition.

Do not assume every distressed crew member is merely being difficult.

Do not assume every difficult crew member requires sedation.

Assess the situation, reduce immediate danger, and use restraint or medication only when justified.
