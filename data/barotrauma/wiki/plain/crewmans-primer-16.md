## V. FORWARD MOMENTUM

The captain is responsible for keeping the submarine moving toward useful objectives.

This does not mean keeping the engines at full power simply so the scenery changes faster.

Forward momentum includes:

- Completing contracts.

- Reaching new stations.

- Improving the submarine.

- Maintaining financial solvency.

- Recovering resources.

- Building crew experience.

- Expanding into more distant waters.

- Avoiding pointless engagements.

- Withdrawing from missions that have become ruinously expensive.

- Preparing for longer routes.

Sometimes progress means advancing.

Sometimes it means stopping to mine uranium.

Sometimes it means remaining in port while engineering explains, for the third time, that one fuel rod is not a strategic reserve.

Sometimes it means retreating before a difficult mission becomes a permanent landmark on someone else’s sonar.

Motion is not always progress.

A submarine falling toward crush depth is moving very efficiently.

## VI. NAVIGATION

The captain’s most visible responsibility is guiding the vessel from point A to point B without discovering an uncharted point C inside a cavern wall.

Navigation requires awareness of:

- Cavern ceilings.

- The seabed.

- Narrow passages.

- Ice formations.

- Wreckage.

- Ruins.

- Hostile contacts.

- Mineral deposits.

- Mission objectives.

- Docking structures.

- Current depth.

- Ascent and descent rates.

- Ballast authority.

- Engine response.

- Hull protrusions.

- Any poorly placed exterior gun whose barrel extends farther than the designer admitted.

The captain must navigate the entire submarine, not merely the centre marker displayed on the helm.

The central point may pass cleanly through a gap while the engine, railgun, docking port, or decorative observation blister removes itself against the rock.

Submarine designers refer to this as “operational clearance.”

Captains refer to it as “that bloody corner.”

## VII. THE LEGEND OF THE BLIND CAPTAIN

Veteran captains occasionally swear upon their epaulets that they can navigate Europa without active sonar.

They claim to follow cavern roofs by engine echo, hull vibration, water movement, intuition, or an ancestral maritime sense inherited from relatives who once operated fishing boats on Earth.

Some of them can.

Some of them merely collide with confidence.

Running without active sonar may be appropriate when avoiding creatures sensitive to sound or attempting to conceal the vessel’s position. It should not become a theatrical demonstration of seniority.

Navigation information may come from:

- Active sonar.

- Passive sonar.

- Cameras.

- Periscopes.

- Viewports.

- Gunnery reports.

- Away-team observations.

- Known charts.

- Prior experience.

- The mechanic shouting that the wall is getting closer.

When operating without active sonar:

- Reduce speed.

- Assign visual observers.

- Use passive contacts.

- Follow known terrain.

- Request reports from gunnery.

- Use brief sonar pulses when necessary.

- Resist the urge to prove that rocks respect rank.

Navigating by instinct is impressive.

Navigating by instinct while ignoring a fully functional sonar set is how captains become cautionary anecdotes.

## VIII. SONAR DISCIPLINE

Active sonar allows the submarine to see.

It also allows much of Europa to see the submarine.

The captain must decide whether the immediate danger is blindness or attention.

Available options include:

- Continuous active sonar.

- Periodic pulses.

- Directional sonar.

- Passive listening.

- External observation.

- Reduced-speed silent running.

- Asking the gun crews whether anything enormous appears to be following the vessel.

The crew should know when active sonar is disabled.

Useful commands include:

- “Active sonar off. Gunnery maintain visual watch.”

- “One short pulse.”

- “Large contact below.”

- “Running silent until clear.”

- “Resume active sonar.”

- “Someone explain why the contact is now above us.”

The captain need not narrate every sonar return.

They should report anything likely to alter the crew’s remaining life expectancy.

## IX. HAZARD AVOIDANCE

Not every creature must be fought.

Not every cave must be entered.

Not every ruin must be investigated.

Not every mineral formation justifies parking the submarine beside what appears to be a nest containing every crawler born since settlement began.

The captain should weigh:

- Threat size.

- Threat speed.

- Ammunition reserves.

- Weapon coverage.

- Hull condition.

- Crew readiness.

- Nearby terrain.

- Escape routes.

- The presence of divers.

- Mission urgency.

- Potential reward.

- Whether the thing on sonar appears to have noticed the submarine.

Avoidance is not cowardice.

It is ammunition conservation performed at engine speed.

A captain who hunts every contact will eventually find one that has also been hunting.

## X. SPEED AND THE SCIENCE OF ARRIVING IN ONE PIECE

Maximum engine output is not always the fastest route to success.

High speed reduces reaction time, increases collision risk, strains the reactor, and makes docking resemble an insurance fraud attempt.

High speed may be appropriate:

- In open water.

- During escape.

- During a timed mission.

- On a known route.

- When something behind the vessel has more teeth than the vessel has ammunition.

Reduced speed is appropriate:

- Near terrain.

- Near stations.

- Near wrecks.

- With divers outside.

- During silent running.

- When ballast control is impaired.

- During reactor instability.

- When operating an unfamiliar submarine.

- Whenever the mechanic says, “Please stop doing that.”

The engine control is not a moral test.

A captain may reduce speed without surrendering their epaulets.

## XI. REACTOR AND FUEL AWARENESS

The captain need not personally operate the reactor.

They should understand what it means when engineering says it is “fine.”

“Fine” may mean:

- Stable.

- Temporarily stable.

- On automatic control.

- Being manually held together.

- One load spike away from becoming educational.

- The engineer does not currently have time to explain.

The captain should know:

- Who is operating the reactor.

- Whether it is on manual or automatic control.

- The condition of the active fuel rod.

- The number of reserves.

- Whether the grid is stable.

- Whether heavy systems are drawing excessive power.

- How long the vessel can continue.

- Whether more fuel can be fabricated.

Ask for useful reports.

“One rod active at forty percent, two reserves, and enough uranium for another.”

This is useful.

“Probably enough.”

This is not a fuel report.

It is a prophecy.

Fuel planning must include the return journey, delays, combat, detours, and the possibility that the next station has no rods for sale because another captain purchased all of them after receiving the same prophecy.

## XII. SUPPLIES AND THE CAPTAIN’S BROADLY INFORMED ANXIETY

The captain should maintain a general understanding of the vessel’s stores.

They need not personally count every bandage.

They should know whom to ask and whether that person has recently been using the bandages as decorative streamers.

Important supplies include:

- Reactor fuel.

- Welding fuel.

- Oxygen.

- Ammunition.

- Medical treatment.

- Blood products.

- Mechanical components.

- Electrical components.

- Fabrication materials.

- Diving equipment.

- Batteries.

- Tools.

- Anything the crew insists is “mission critical” after purchasing it personally.

The captain should also know what can be fabricated aboard.

Minerals may become ammunition, medicine, fuel, explosives, tools, or electrical components.

They do not become those things through optimism.

The vessel still requires working machinery, power, trained personnel, and time.

A cargo hold full of minerals is not the same as a stocked submarine.

It is a stocked submarine after engineering has spent twenty minutes arguing about fabrication priorities.

## XIII. MISSION SELECTION

The highest-paying mission is not always the most profitable.

A generous contract may be offset by:

- Ammunition expenditure.

- Hull repairs.

- Medical treatment.

- Fuel consumption.

- Crew replacement.

- Equipment loss.

- Reputation damage.

- Fines.

- Bribes.

- An artifact deciding that the engine room should become hotter.

Mission selection should account for:

- Crew skill.

- Vessel capability.

- Distance.

- Supplies.

- Threats.

- Political consequences.

- Salvage opportunities.

- Mining opportunities.

- Whether objectives can be combined.

- Whether the destination is likely to purchase the things the crew will inevitably steal along the way.

A captain who accepts every contract may appear ambitious.

They may also discover that escorting a transport, killing a swarm, recovering an artifact, clearing a nest, and delivering volatile cargo do not become easier when attempted simultaneously.

Take work the submarine can complete.

There is no shame in declining a mission.

There is considerable shame in accepting one, losing half the crew, and discovering the payment does not cover the ammunition.

## XIV. COMMUNICATION FROM THE HELM

The captain should provide enough information for the crew to understand what the submarine is doing.

The crew does not require a poetic narration of every adjustment.

It should know when:

- Danger is approaching.

- Speed is changing.

- Depth is changing.

- Sonar is being disabled.

- Heavy weapons may be needed.

- Engineering should expect increased load.

- Divers must return.

- Docking has begun.

- The vessel is retreating.

- Mission priorities have changed.

- Someone should check why medical is on fire.

Useful commands are concise:

- “Large contact below. Gunnery prepare.”

- “Engineering, maximum engine load incoming.”

- “Away team recall.”

- “Mechanics to aft breach.”

- “Running silent.”

- “Medical, prepare for casualties.”

- “All departments report readiness.”

The captain should also listen.

Command does not improve merely because the captain has successfully occupied every radio frequency at once.
