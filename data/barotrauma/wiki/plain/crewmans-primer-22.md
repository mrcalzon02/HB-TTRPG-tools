## XXIII. DISCIPLINE AND DUE PROCESS

Union rules do not prevent discipline.

They prevent arbitrary discipline.

Crew members may be corrected or punished for:

- Sabotage.

- Assault.

- Theft.

- Deliberate insubordination.

- Unauthorized weapons use.

- Reckless endangerment.

- Abandoning duty.

- Misuse of medical supplies.

- Tampering with the reactor.

- Unauthorized movement of the submarine.

- Repeated refusal of agreed work.

- Bringing an unidentified organism aboard and placing it in the ballast tank.

Discipline should be proportionate to the violation.

Possible measures include:

- Warning.

- Retraining.

- Reassignment.

- Loss of sensitive privileges.

- Temporary detention.

- Restitution.

- Dismissal.

- Transfer to station authorities.

- Emergency force when an immediate threat exists.

A crew member should be informed of the reason for serious discipline.

Evidence should be preserved when possible.

Witnesses should be consulted.

The accused should be permitted to respond unless the vessel is in immediate danger.

Summary execution because someone “looked suspicious” is generally disfavored.

This remains true even when the person is dressed as a clown.

## XXIV. MUTINY, WORK STOPPAGE, AND COLLECTIVE REFUSAL

Union action is not automatically mutiny.

Mutiny seeks unlawful control of the vessel.

A labor action seeks enforcement or renegotiation of employment terms.

The distinction may become difficult when the reactor operator folds their arms beside the control panel, but it remains important.

A protected collective refusal may arise when:

- Wages have not been paid.

- The vessel is manifestly unsafe.

- Essential equipment is unavailable.

- The crew is ordered beyond agreed duties without negotiation.

- The captain refuses to address a serious contract violation.

- A union representative has been unlawfully detained.

- The employer attempts forced labor.

A work stoppage must not deliberately destroy the vessel or abandon innocent personnel to immediate danger.

Essential life-support functions should be preserved where possible.

Crew members may refuse to depart from port more safely than they can refuse to repair an active hull breach.

The ocean does not suspend pressure while labor and management resolve their differences.

Union law expects both judgment and solidarity.

## XXV. SALVAGE RIGHTS

Salvage is a common source of dispute.

Contracts should specify:

- Whether salvage belongs to the vessel, employer, or crew.

- What percentage is allocated to operating costs.

- How individual discoveries are treated.

- Whether personal items may be claimed.

- How artifacts are valued.

- How mission-required objects differ from discretionary salvage.

- Whether dangerous recovery earns additional compensation.

A captain should not declare all valuable objects ship property while assigning all worthless objects to the crew.

Neither should crew members conceal high-value salvage in personal lockers and expect the union to defend the practice.

Transparent division prevents the traditional portside argument in which everyone suddenly remembers a different version of the agreement.

## XXVI. INJURY, DISABILITY, AND DEATH BENEFITS

Submarine labor carries a high risk of permanent injury.

Union agreements may provide:

- Treatment costs.

- Recovery wages.

- Disability compensation.

- Prosthetic assistance.

- Transportation to suitable medical facilities.

- Retraining for another profession.

- Support for dependents.

- Funeral or recovery expenses.

- Death benefits.

- Payment of wages owed at the time of death.

The loss of a crew member does not erase the employer’s obligations.

A captain may not classify the dead as having resigned.

A corporation may not withhold wages because the worker failed to personally collect them.

The union maintains beneficiary registries so that compensation can reach families, partners, children, or other designated dependents across distant stations.

Where no beneficiary exists, agreed funds may support crew memorials, recovery operations, or mutual aid.

## XXVII. DEBT AND FORCED LABOR

Debt is common on Europa.

Forced labor disguised as debt repayment is also common.

Union 208 recognizes legitimate employment advances and repayment agreements.

It does not recognize:

- Infinite interest.

- Inherited employment debt.

- Debt created through employer-controlled prices.

- Charges for basic air.

- Charges for mandatory safety equipment.

- Retroactive recruitment fees.

- Debt extended through punishment.

- Forced continuation of service after repayment.

- Sale of a crew member’s contract without consent.

A mariner who owes money remains a person, not cargo.

Criminal organizations frequently disagree.

They nevertheless tend to respect union intervention after being reminded how many debtors operate their cargo routes.

## XXVIII. CONTRACT EXTENSION

A captain may request that a crew extend service beyond the original contract.

The crew may agree.

Additional compensation should be established.

Emergency circumstances may require temporary extension when immediate departure would endanger the vessel or leave the crew stranded.

Such necessity does not grant indefinite ownership of the crew’s labor.

Once a safe station is reached, workers must be allowed to renegotiate, transfer, or end service according to the agreement.

The phrase “one more mission” has been used to justify voyages lasting several months.

Union contracts therefore recommend replacing it with an actual number.

## XXIX. CAPTAIN’S RIGHTS UNDER UNION 208

Union law protects captains as workers and commanding officers.

Captains are entitled to:

- Agreed compensation.

- Clear ownership or employment terms.

- Accurate crew qualifications.

- Protection against sabotage.

- Authority to direct ordinary vessel operations.

- Emergency command powers.

- Disciplinary support for serious misconduct.

- Arbitration with employers.

- Protection from being forced to operate an unfit vessel.

- Refusal of missions beyond contractual or mechanical capability.

- Crew cooperation with lawful orders.

- Union rescue and mutual aid.

Many captains are themselves union members.

The union does not treat command and labor as natural enemies.

A captain may be responsible for enforcing an employer’s contract while also being underpaid by the same employer.

Europa is efficient in that way.

## XXX. CREW OBLIGATIONS

Rights carry obligations.

Union-protected crew members must:

- Perform agreed duties.

- Follow lawful orders.

- Preserve ship property.

- Report hazards.

- Maintain reasonable competence.

- Avoid sabotage.

- Avoid theft.

- Avoid unnecessary violence.

- Conserve supplies.

- Observe safety procedures.

- Return from authorized leave.

- Assist during emergencies.

- Respect the rights of other crew members.

- Participate honestly in investigations and arbitration.

Union membership is not immunity from consequences.

The union will defend fair process.

It will not necessarily defend the conduct that created the process.

A member caught deliberately putting volatile compound into the reactor does not improve their case by presenting a union card.

## XXXI. UNION ARBITRATION

Disputes that cannot be resolved aboard ship may be referred to Union 208 arbitration.

Arbitrators may be:

- Senior captains.

- Engineers.

- Medical officers.

- Labor delegates.

- Station representatives.

- Legal specialists.

- Experienced mariners recognized by both parties.

Arbitration reviews:

- Contracts.

- Logs.

- Wage records.

- Medical reports.

- Crew testimony.

- Security recordings.

- Voyage data.

- Supply records.

- Disciplinary actions.

- Evidence of changed mission conditions.

Remedies may include:

- Payment of wages.

- Restitution.

- Contract termination.

- Reinstatement.

- Blacklisting.

- Removal from a hiring registry.

- Compensation.

- Revised salvage distribution.

- Formal censure.

- Referral to station authorities.

- Collective action.

Union arbitration is often faster than station courts.

This is partly because union arbiters understand submarine work.

It is also because they do not require six months to determine whether a completely flooded reactor room was unsafe.

## XXXII. THE RED REGISTRY

Union 208 maintains records of employers, captains, vessels, and stations accused or found responsible for severe violations.

This is commonly known as the Red Registry.

Listings may warn of:

- Unpaid wages.

- Unsafe vessels.

- Abandonment.

- Forced labor.

- Disappearance of crew.

- Fraudulent contracts.

- Repeated medical neglect.

- Violent discipline.

- Salvage theft.

- Union suppression.

- Failure to honor arbitration.

- Saville-level violations.

A registry listing may prevent an employer from recruiting experienced crew.

It may increase prices.

It may eliminate access to convoy protection.

It may cause dockworkers to refuse service.

The registry is not intended for personal revenge.

False reporting is itself a serious union offense.

A Red Registry entry must be supported by evidence, repeated credible complaints, or formal findings.

The system’s power depends upon trust.

## XXXIII. LOGISTICAL SANCTIONS

The union’s greatest power is not military.

It is logistical.

A submarine cannot operate without:

- Fuel.

- Oxygen.

- Ammunition.

- Welding supplies.

- Food.

- Medicine.

- Parts.

- Repairs.

- Docking.

- Crew.

- Rescue coverage.

A station cannot survive without:

- Cargo.

- Replacement machinery.

- Medical shipments.

- Agricultural support.

- Power components.

- Skilled labor.

- Transport.

- Waste removal.

- Emergency evacuation.

- Trade access.

When a station violates fundamental union protections, the union may suspend cooperation.

This does not require every mariner to share the same political beliefs.

They need only understand that permitting one station to seize crews without consequence makes every crew less safe.

Today it is someone else’s contract.

Tomorrow it is your engineer.

The day after, it is you.

Solidarity beneath the ice is not merely ideological.

It is preventative maintenance for civilization.
